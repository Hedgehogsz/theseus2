# Home and urban emergencies: power cuts, water, warmth, toilets, chemical/radiation incidents, towns, leaving home.

=== grid-down-first
title: Power cut: the first hours
summary: What to do when the power goes off at home: check if it's just you, protect food, light safely, stay informed and save your phone battery.
tags: power cut, power outage, blackout, electricity, grid down, no power, 105, fuse box, trip switch, fridge, freezer, Priority Services Register
icon: plug-zap
time: 15–30 min
level: easy
related: home-water-storage, cold-house, batteries-electronics, fire-safety
---
!quick
1. **Check if it's just you:** look at neighbours' windows and street lights, then your fuse box (consumer unit) for a tripped switch.
2. **Keep the fridge and freezer shut.** Use **torches, not candles**.
3. **Switch off or unplug** cookers, heaters, irons and other appliances that were on. Leave **one light switched on** so you know when power returns.
4. **Report it on 105** when you can reach a working phone or the internet, and **check on vulnerable neighbours**.
!end

## Is it just your home?
- **Neighbours dark, street lights off:** it's a local or wider power cut. Nothing to fix inside. Go to "Report it" below.
- **Neighbours have power, you don't:** open your consumer unit (fuse box). If a switch has flipped down, a circuit has tripped. Switch off or unplug everything on that circuit, push the switch back up, then plug things back in one at a time. The one that trips it again is faulty: leave it unplugged.
- **It won't reset, or you smell burning:** leave it off and get an electrician. Don't keep resetting it.
- **Prepayment meter:** check you haven't run out of credit or emergency credit.

## Report it
**105** is the free power cut number for England, Scotland and Wales. It connects you to your local network operator (not your energy supplier), who can tell you what's happening and how long it might last. Report it on 105 when you can reach a working phone or the internet. This phone has no SIM and cannot make calls.

!danger Fallen power lines
Stay **at least 10 m (about 30 ft)** away from fallen or damaged lines, and anything touching them (fences, puddles, trees). Assume they are live. Keep others and pets away, and report the location on 105 as soon as you can reach a working phone.
!end

## Protect your food
- A **fridge** keeps food cold for about **4 hours** if you keep the door shut.
- A **full freezer** stays frozen for about **48 hours**; a **half-full** one for about **24 hours**. Don't open it to check.
- Eat from the fridge first, then the freezer, then the cupboard. See [[a:home-food-storage]] for what to throw away.
- In winter, a cold garage or outdoor box in the shade can act as a fridge, but keep food sealed from animals.

## Light
- **Torches and head torches, not candles.** Candles cause many house fires every year, and power cuts are when they get used. Use the phone's [[t:torch]] only briefly: it drains the battery fast.
- If you must use candles or tea lights: on a heat-resistant holder, away from curtains, out of reach of children and pets, never left alone and never in a bedroom while you sleep. See [[a:home-security]] for fire safety.
- Put a torch somewhere you can find it in the dark, every time: by the bed, by the door.

## Stay informed
- A battery or wind-up **radio** is the best source of news. BBC local radio carries emergency updates.
- This phone has an **FM radio app**, but it needs **wired headphones** plugged in: the cable acts as the aerial.
- Network operators and councils post updates online and on local radio. Mobile networks may fail after a few hours as masts run out of backup power.

## Save your phone battery
- Turn on airplane mode and low brightness ([[t:phone-mode]]), and **power the phone off** between uses. Check charge with [[t:battery]].
- Charge from the [[a:solar-panel]] in daylight, or from a power bank or a car (engine running, outside, never in a garage).
- See [[a:batteries-electronics]] for cold weather and battery care.

## Check on others
- Knock on the doors of **older, disabled or unwell neighbours**, families with babies, and anyone who uses **powered medical equipment** (oxygen concentrators, home dialysis, stairlifts, ventilators). They may need help urgently.
- See [[a:community]] for working together if it lasts.

!tip Priority Services Register
The **Priority Services Register** is free. Energy suppliers and network operators keep it for people who need extra help in a power cut: pension age, disabled, long-term ill, dependent on medical equipment, with young children, or with communication needs. People on it get priority updates and, in long cuts, may get help such as hot food or a place to stay. Water companies keep a similar register. Sign up yourself or vulnerable relatives through your energy supplier or network operator once services are back.
!end

## If it goes on
- **Water:** fill containers while the taps still run ([[a:home-water-storage]]). Water and sewage pumping can fail in a long cut.
- **Warmth:** most gas central heating needs electricity for its pump and controls, so it will stop too. See [[a:cold-house]].
- **Cooking:** [[a:cooking-without-power]].
- **Compensation:** under Ofgem's guaranteed standards you may be owed a payment if a cut lasts more than 12 hours in normal weather. Claim later; it's not a priority now.

## When the power comes back
Plug things in one at a time over a few minutes, not all at once. Check the freezer: food with ice crystals can usually be refrozen; fully thawed food needs a decision (see [[a:home-food-storage]]). Reset clocks and the boiler if needed.

=== home-water-storage
title: Storing and finding water at home
summary: How much water to keep, how to store tap water, and where to find hidden water in your home: hot water cylinder, loft tank, pipes and cistern.
tags: water storage, store water, bottled water, water off, no water, hot water cylinder, tank, cistern, loft tank, rainwater, water butt, emergency water
icon: container
time: 30–60 min
level: easy
needs: steel-pot, "clean containers with lids", "spanner or adjustable wrench", "hose"
related: settle-strain-boil, water-needs, rain-dew-collection, boiling-limits
---
!quick
1. **Fill clean containers and the bath now** if the taps still run. Bath water is for flushing and washing.
2. Allow **2–3 litres per person per day to drink**, about **10 litres** with cooking and basic washing.
3. Water already in your home: the **pipes**, the **hot water cylinder** and the **toilet cistern**. Turn the heating off before draining the cylinder.
4. **Boil** anything that isn't fresh tap or bottled water before drinking ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]).
!end

## How much you need
- **Drinking:** 2–3 litres per person per day as a minimum. More in hot weather, for the ill, for breastfeeding mothers, and if you're working hard. Use [[t:calc]] to estimate.
- **Drinking, cooking and basic hygiene:** about 10 litres per person per day. In an emergency, UK water companies must provide at least 10 litres per person per day, usually as bottled water or from tankers (bowsers).
- **Pets:** a dog needs roughly 50 ml per kg of body weight a day.
- **Example:** a family of four for 3 days needs at least 36 litres just to drink. That's three 12-litre containers.

## Storing tap water
1. Use **food-grade containers with tight lids**: bought water carriers, or well-cleaned drinks bottles. Avoid milk bottles (hard to clean) and anything that held chemicals.
2. Wash them with hot soapy water and rinse well.
3. Fill from the **cold kitchen tap** (it comes straight from the mains). Fill to the top and close the lid.
4. Label with the date. Store somewhere **cool, dark** and away from fuel, paint and cleaning products (plastic lets fumes through).
5. **Replace every 6 months.** Use the old water on plants. Shop-bought bottled water keeps until its date if unopened.

When a cut is coming (a storm warning, a burst main nearby, a long power cut), also fill the **bath**, saucepans, kettles and spare buckets. A bath holds 100–150 litres: perfect for flushing toilets and washing, but don't drink it (baths aren't clean enough).

## Water already in your house

### Pipes
When the mains goes off, water stays in the pipes. **Open the highest tap** in the house (upstairs) to let air in, then collect from the **lowest tap** (usually the kitchen cold tap). This is mains water: fine to drink unless there's a boil water notice.

### Hot water cylinder
If you have a hot water cylinder (a tall tank, often in an airing cupboard), it holds about **100–200 litres**. Combi boilers have no cylinder.
1. **Turn off the boiler and immersion heater first.** Heating an empty cylinder can damage it or start a fire. Leave them off until the cylinder is full again.
2. Let the water cool if it was hot.
3. Find the **drain valve** at the bottom of the cylinder or on the cold pipe going into it. It often has a ridged spout for a hose and a square head you turn with a spanner.
4. Open a hot tap upstairs to let air in. Attach a hose or hold a container under the valve, and open it slowly.
5. The first water out may be rusty or full of sediment: let it settle.

In many older homes the cylinder is fed from a cold-water tank in the loft, so this water isn't drinking quality. **Boil it before drinking.** A pressurised (unvented) cylinder has safety valves: drain it only from the proper drain point, and never interfere with the valves.

### Loft tank
The cold-water tank in the loft holds 100–200 litres. It's often dusty, with dead insects in it. Fine for washing and flushing; **boil before drinking**.

### Toilet cistern
The **cistern** (the tank behind or above the toilet, not the bowl) holds 6–9 litres of clean mains water. Use it for washing or flushing. Drink it only if you've boiled it and there's **no blue block or cleaning chemical** in it. Never use water from the bowl.

## Making it safe
- **Boil** for 1 minute ([[a:settle-strain-boil]], [[t:boil]]). This kills germs but **does not remove chemicals**. See [[a:boiling-limits]].
- If your water company issues a **boil water notice**, boil tap water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth and making ice, until it's lifted.
- When the supply comes back, run the cold kitchen tap until the water runs clear before drinking it.

## Rainwater
- A **water butt** on a downpipe is good for flushing and washing.
- Roofs collect bird droppings, moss and dust. Let the first few minutes of a downpour run away, then collect. Boil before drinking. See [[a:rain-dew-collection]].
- Don't use water from roofs with old lead flashing or tar coatings for drinking.

=== cold-house
title: Staying warm in a cold house
summary: How to keep yourself and your household warm when the heating fails: one warm room, draught-proofing, layers, food and avoiding carbon monoxide.
tags: cold house, no heating, heating off, warm room, keep warm, winter, draughts, hot water bottle, carbon monoxide, CO, hypothermia, elderly, insulation
icon: heater
time: 30–60 min
level: easy
needs: warm-layers, survival-bag, "hot water bottle", "blankets and duvets"
related: hypothermia, layering, sleeping-warm, cooking-without-power
---
!quick
1. **Pick one small room** and close its door. Everyone lives in it. Block draughts and close curtains at dusk.
2. **Layer up**, including **hats and socks indoors**. Wrap up in duvets.
3. **Eat and drink something warm** regularly, and **move** every hour.
4. **Never** use a BBQ, generator, patio heater, camping stove or gas cooker to heat a room: **carbon monoxide** kills.
!end

## Choose one warm room
- Pick a **small room**, with as few outside walls and windows as possible, and **upstairs** if you can (heat rises). A south-facing room warms in daytime sun.
- Move everyone into it, including for sleep. People and pets give off heat: a small room with several people stays noticeably warmer.
- Close the doors to every other room.

## Keep the heat in
- **Close curtains** as soon as it gets dark, and **open them** on sunny days to let the sun in.
- Hang blankets or duvets over windows and over the inside of the door. Bubble wrap or cardboard on glass helps.
- **Draughts:** roll towels along the bottom of doors, cover letterboxes and keyholes, and block unused fireplaces with a bag of newspaper or cardboard (remove it before lighting a fire).
- Put rugs or cardboard on bare floors. Most heat loss from a seated person goes into the chair and floor.
- Don't block air bricks or trickle vents completely: you need some fresh air, especially if anything is burning.

## Keep your body warm
- **Layers:** several thin layers trap more warm air than one thick one. Wool and fleece stay warm when damp; cotton doesn't. See [[a:layering]].
- **Hat, scarf, gloves and thick socks** indoors. Keep feet off cold floors.
- **Hot water bottles:** fill with hot, not boiling, water. Squeeze the air out and check the stopper. Cover them. Replace bottles that are old or perished (look for the date mark moulded into the neck). Your steel bottle with a sock round it also works ([[a:boiling-steel-bottle-pot]]).
- **Eat:** regular meals and hot drinks. Your body makes heat by burning food. Carbohydrates and fats both help.
- **Move:** get up and walk around, or march on the spot, at least once an hour. Don't sit still for long periods.
- **Sleep together:** share beds or lie close on mattresses on the floor of the warm room, with duvets above and below you. Pull a survival bag or sleeping bag over or around a duvet for extra warmth. Wear a hat in bed.
- **Stay dry:** change damp clothes straight away.

!danger Carbon monoxide (CO)
Every winter, people die from CO in their homes during power cuts. You can't see, smell or taste it.
- **Never** bring a BBQ (even one that's gone out), generator, patio heater, camping stove or paraffin heater not designed for indoor use into the house, garage or a tent.
- **Never** use a gas hob or oven to heat a room.
- Run generators outside, well away from doors and windows.
- **Symptoms:** headache, dizziness, nausea, tiredness, confusion, breathlessness. It's like flu but with no fever, and it often affects several people (and pets) at once. Symptoms ease when you go outside.
- **If you suspect it:** turn off the appliance if safe, open doors and windows, get everyone into fresh air. Anyone with symptoms needs medical help as soon as you can get it.
- A **battery CO alarm** keeps working in a power cut. Test it.
!end

## Open fires and wood burners
If you have a working, swept chimney with a fire or stove, use it: it's the best heat source in a power cut. Burn dry wood only, keep a fireguard in place, never leave it unattended, and keep the CO alarm in the same room. Don't burn painted or treated wood, plastics or rubbish.

## Older people, babies and the unwell
- **Older people** can get hypothermia slowly indoors without realising. They may not shiver. Watch for confusion, slurred speech, drowsiness, and **cold, pale skin on the chest or tummy** (not just hands). See [[a:hypothermia]].
- Warm them gently: blankets, warm (not hot) drinks if they can swallow, a hat, and other people close by. Don't rub their limbs or put them in a hot bath.
- **Babies** lose heat fast but must not overheat while sleeping. Add or remove layers, and feel their tummy or the back of their neck: it should be warm, not hot or sweaty. Don't put hot water bottles in a baby's bed.
- Check on vulnerable neighbours at least twice a day ([[a:community]]).

## How warm should it be?
UK health advice is to heat the rooms you use to at least **18°C** where you can. Without power you may not manage that. Aim to keep people warm rather than the room, and watch for early signs of cold: constant shivering, numb fingers, and clumsiness.

=== cooking-without-power
title: Cooking without power
summary: Safe ways to heat food and water with no electricity: camping stoves, BBQs, rocket stoves and retained-heat (hay-box) cooking, plus no-cook food.
tags: cooking, no power, camping stove, gas stove, BBQ, barbecue, rocket stove, hay box, haybox, retained heat, solar oven, wonder bag, carbon monoxide, fuel
icon: flame-kindling
time: 20–60 min
level: moderate
needs: steel-pot, arc-lighter, "camping stove and gas", "matches"
related: cooking-on-fire, fire-safety, home-food-storage, settle-strain-boil
---
!quick
1. **Cook outdoors** on a camping stove or BBQ. **Never** use a BBQ indoors, and never heat a room with a cooker or stove.
2. **Save fuel:** lid on the pot, small pans, cook once a day, and use **retained heat** (wrap the hot pot in a duvet).
3. **Eat no-cook food first** (fridge food, then tins, then dried).
4. **Boiling water to make it safe** comes first; use any fuel for that before luxury cooking.
!end

## What still works
- **Mains gas hob:** many gas hobs still work in a power cut if the gas is on. Light the burner with a match or lighter if the electric ignition doesn't spark. Ovens with electronic controls usually won't work. Never leave a gas hob burning to heat the room.
- **Wood burner with a flat top:** good for a kettle and a stew pot.
- Everything else needs one of the methods below.

## Camping stoves
- **Best outdoors**, in a sheltered spot: a back step, a carport, a garden under a tarp with open sides. Out of the wind they use much less gas.
- If you can't go outside, use it only by a **wide-open door** with another window open, never in a small closed room or a tent, never where anyone sleeps, and never unattended. Keep a CO alarm nearby.
- Use the gas the stove was made for. Change cartridges outdoors, away from flames. Never try to refill disposable cartridges.
- Put it on a stable, level, heat-proof surface. Keep children, pets and curtains away.

## BBQs
- **Outdoors only**, well away from fences, sheds and the house. Never in a garage, tent, porch or conservatory.
- **Disposable and charcoal BBQs keep giving off carbon monoxide** for hours after the flames go out. Never take one indoors to keep warm.
- A charcoal BBQ uses a lot of fuel for one meal: cook everything at once, and boil water on it at the same time.

## Rocket stove
A rocket stove burns small sticks very efficiently, so a few handfuls of twigs boil a pot.
1. Build an L-shaped tube: 4–5 bricks or blocks on each side of a fuel opening, with a chimney 30–40 cm tall above it. Or use a large tin can with a smaller can as the inner tube, and sand around it.
2. Feed thin dry sticks (pencil to thumb thickness) into the lower opening, a few at a time.
3. Rest the pot on bricks or a metal grid over the top, leaving a gap for the flames and smoke to escape.
4. Outdoors only. See [[a:fire-safety]] and [[a:firewood-uk]].

## Solar ovens
A box lined with foil with a glass or clear plastic lid can heat food on a **bright, calm summer day**. In the UK they are slow and unreliable, and useless on cloudy days or in winter. Treat them as a bonus, not a plan.

## Retained-heat (hay-box) cooking
This saves a lot of fuel. Food finishes cooking in its own heat.
1. Bring the food to a full boil with the lid on, and simmer for a few minutes: about 5 minutes for rice or pasta, 10–15 minutes for stews and soaked lentils.
2. Put the pot, lid on, straight into an insulated nest: a box or basket packed with duvets, pillows, sleeping bags or hay, with insulation all around and on top.
3. Leave it: rice or pasta about 1 hour, porridge overnight, stews 2–3 hours.
4. **Bring back to the boil before eating** if it has cooled, or eat it straight away while piping hot.

!warn Food safety with slow cooking
Food left between about **8°C and 63°C** for hours lets bacteria multiply. Don't leave food in a hay box for more than about 4 hours, and don't keep leftovers from it. **Red kidney beans** must be soaked, then **boiled hard for at least 10 minutes** before slow cooking, or they can make you very sick. Cooked rice left warm is a common cause of food poisoning.
!end

## No-cook food
- Eat food from the fridge first, then the freezer, then cupboard food ([[a:home-food-storage]]).
- Many tins (beans, soups, meat, fish) are already cooked and safe cold. Bread, crackers, oats soaked in milk or water, peanut butter, dried fruit and nuts need no cooking.
- Keep fuel for **boiling water** and a hot drink: warmth and morale matter.

## Fuel safety and storage
- Store gas cartridges and bottles **outdoors or in a ventilated shed**, out of the sun. Never in a cellar (gas is heavier than air and pools low down).
- Keep petrol only in approved containers, in small amounts, away from the house.
- Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of water and a fire blanket near where you cook.
- Know the signs of carbon monoxide poisoning: see [[a:cold-house]].

=== home-food-storage
title: Food storage and stocks
summary: What food to keep for emergencies, how to rotate it, and how to tell what's safe when the fridge and freezer have been off.
tags: food stocks, food store, stockpile, tins, tinned food, cans, dried food, non-perishable, pantry, larder, fridge off, freezer thawed, food safety, use by, best before
icon: package-open
time: 1–2 hours to set up
level: easy
related: grid-down-first, cooking-without-power, food-priorities, diarrhoea-ors
---
!quick
1. Keep **at least 3 days** of food that needs no fridge; **a week or two** is better.
2. **Eat the fridge first** (keep it shut), then the freezer, then the cupboard.
3. Chilled food that's been **above 8°C for more than 4 hours: throw it away.** If in doubt, throw it out.
4. **Rotate:** put new stock at the back, use the oldest first.
!end

## How much
UK government and British Red Cross advice is to keep enough food and water for **at least 3 days** without shops. A week or two of extra cupboard food is a sensible target if you have room and money, built up a little at a time.

An adult needs about **2,000–2,500 kcal a day** (more if cold or working hard). In a crisis, people can manage on less for a while, but cold and hunger make you weaker and less clear-headed.

## What to store
Choose food your household **already eats**, that keeps for months without a fridge, and that needs little or no cooking.

| Food | Approx. kcal per 100 g | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peanut butter, nuts | 550–650 | Dense, no cooking. Check for allergies. |
| Oats | 370 | Soak in cold water or milk if no fuel. |
| Pasta, rice, couscous | 350 (dry) | Needs water and fuel. Couscous just needs hot water. |
| Crackers, oatcakes, crispbread | 400–450 | Keep sealed and dry. |
| Dried fruit | 250–300 | Quick energy. |
| Tinned fish or meat | 150–250 | Protein, ready to eat. |
| Tinned beans, soup, vegetables | 50–100 | Ready to eat; also contains water. |
| UHT milk, powdered milk | 65 / 500 | UHT lasts months unopened. |
| Honey, jam, chocolate | 300–550 | Morale and energy. |

Also: salt, sugar, stock cubes, tea and coffee, cooking oil, baby food and formula, pet food, and any special-diet foods. A **manual tin opener** is essential; ring-pull tins help.

## Rotation
- Write the date on things you buy for the store. Put new items at the back.
- Check the store every 6 months. Use anything near its date in everyday cooking and replace it.
- **Best before** is about quality: tins and dried food are usually fine for a while after. **Use by** is about safety: don't eat it after that date.
- Store somewhere **cool, dry and dark**, off the floor, in sealed boxes to keep out damp and mice.

!warn Damaged tins
Throw away tins that are **bulging, leaking, badly dented on the seam or rim, or rusted through**, or that spurt or smell bad when opened. These can carry botulism, which is rare but very dangerous.
!end

## When the fridge and freezer go off
- **Keep the doors shut.** A fridge stays cold for about 4 hours; a full freezer about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24.
- **High-risk chilled food** is meat, fish, poultry, dairy, eggs, cooked rice and pasta, cooked leftovers, deli food, and anything with a "keep refrigerated" label. If it has been **above 8°C for more than about 4 hours**, throw it away. A fridge thermometer helps you know.
- **Lower-risk** fridge items (hard cheese, butter, jams, sauces in jars, fruit and raw vegetables) last longer out of the fridge. Bin anything mouldy, slimy or smelly.
- **Freezer food:** if it **still has ice crystals** and feels hard, it can go back in the freezer when power returns. If it has **fully thawed but is still cold**, cook it thoroughly and eat it within 24 hours (keep it cold meanwhile). Raw meat that has thawed can be cooked and then frozen again. If it has **warmed up**, throw it away.
- Group frozen items tightly together and cover the freezer with a duvet: it stays cold longer.
- **In winter**, a cold, shaded spot outdoors (in a sealed box, safe from animals) can keep food cold. A thermometer tells you if it's below 5°C.

!danger Don't taste to test
You can't taste, smell or see most food-poisoning bacteria. Food can look and smell fine and still make you very ill. When in doubt, throw it out. If someone gets sick, see [[a:diarrhoea-ors]].
!end

## Cooking and hygiene
- Wash hands before handling food, even if water is short (see [[a:toilets-fail]] for handwashing).
- Cook meat until it's piping hot all the way through.
- Cook only what you'll eat at one meal: leftovers are hard to keep safe without a fridge.
- See [[a:cooking-without-power]] for ways to cook.

=== toilets-fail

icon: toilet
time: 15–30 min to set up
level: easy
needs: "two buckets with lids", "strong bin bags", "sawdust, soil or cat litter", "soap"
related: water-hygiene, diarrhoea-ors, home-water-storage, settle-strain-boil
---
!quick
1. **Water off but sewers working:** flush by pouring a **bucket of non-drinking water** fast into the bowl.
2. **Sewers failing or flooded:** stop flushing. Use a **bucket lined with two bags**, and cover each use with **sawdust, soil or cat litter**.
3. **Wash hands** with soap every time, and before touching food.
4. Store tied bags in a **lidded bin outside**, away from your living space and any water source.
!end

## If the water is off but the sewers work
Most water cuts don't affect the sewers. Your toilet still works if you supply the water.
1. Use non-drinking water: bath water, rainwater, washing-up water.
2. **Pour 5–8 litres quickly** into the bowl from waist height. A fast pour flushes; a slow trickle doesn't.
3. Don't flush every wee. "If it's yellow, let it mellow" saves a lot of water.
4. Only toilet paper in the bowl. No wipes, nappies or kitchen roll: blocked sewers are the last thing you need.

## If the sewers fail
Signs: toilets gurgling, slow drains, sewage coming up in the bath, shower or downstairs toilet, or floodwater over drain covers. Or you're told not to flush. Then:
- **Stop flushing** and stop using sinks and showers down the drain.
- Put the plug in sinks and baths, and weight down the lid of a downstairs toilet (a heavy bag of sand or soil on the lid) to stop sewage backing up into the house.

## Bag-and-bucket toilet
1. **Bucket:** any sturdy bucket with a lid, 10–20 litres. Put it in a private, ventilated spot: a bathroom with the window open, a shed or a garden shelter.
2. **Two bags:** line it with two strong bin bags, one inside the other, folded over the rim. Or line the existing toilet bowl (after bailing it out) with two bags under the seat.
3. **Seat:** a toilet seat on top, or just sit carefully on the rim.
4. **Cover material:** after each use, add a scoop of **sawdust, dry soil, cat litter, wood ash, shredded paper or dry leaves** to cover it. This cuts smell and flies. Keep the lid on between uses.
5. **Separate urine** if you can: use a second bucket or bottle for wee. The poo bucket stays drier, smells much less and fills more slowly. Urine from healthy people can be diluted and poured on soil in the garden, away from food crops, wells and streams, or down a drain if the sewers work.
6. **When a third to half full** (heavier is hard to lift without splitting), lift out the inner bag, squeeze out air, tie it, then tie the outer bag too. Put in two fresh bags.

## Storage and disposal
- Put tied bags in a **lidded bin or sealed box outside**, in the shade, away from doors, windows, food and play areas.
- Your council will tell you how waste will be collected. Don't put it out with general rubbish unless told it's acceptable.
- **Don't** put bagged human waste in compost bins, rivers, canals or storm drains.
- If you have to bury waste in a long emergency, dig at least **30 cm deep**, at least **30 m from any well, stream, pond or water butt**, and well away from vegetable beds.

## Handwashing
Diarrhoeal diseases such as norovirus and *E. coli* spread fast when toilets fail. Handwashing is the main defence.
- Wash with **soap and water** after the toilet, after emptying the bucket, and before preparing or eating food.
- Make a **tippy tap**: a bottle or container with a small hole, hung or tilted so it pours a thin stream. Use rainwater or non-drinking water. Soap does the work.
- **Hand sanitiser** (at least 60% alcohol) helps when water is short, but it doesn't work well on dirty hands or against norovirus.
- Keep one cloth and bowl for toilet cleaning only, and never use it for food or dishes.
- See [[a:water-hygiene]].

## Avoiding disease
- Keep the toilet area separate from the kitchen.
- Disinfect the seat and handles daily with diluted household bleach (follow the bottle). Never mix bleach with other cleaners.
- Wear gloves to change bags, then wash your hands.
- Keep flies off food with covers or clingfilm.
- If someone has diarrhoea or vomiting, give them their own bucket if possible, and see [[a:diarrhoea-ors]].
- When the sewers return, clean anything sewage touched with hot soapy water then disinfectant, and throw away soaked soft furnishings.

=== home-security
title: Security basics
summary: Keeping your home and household safe in a long emergency: staying low-key, locks, light and noise discipline, de-escalation and fire safety.

---
!quick
1. **Lock doors and windows**, even when you're home. Keep keys by the door, out of reach from the letterbox.
2. **Stay low-key:** don't show off lights, cooking or supplies. Don't talk about what you have.
3. **Avoid confrontation.** Things can be replaced; people can't. Retreat to a safe room if you must.
4. **Fire is the biggest real risk:** torches over candles, and keep exit routes clear.
!end

## The real risks
In most UK emergencies, communities pull together and serious crime is rare. The risks that actually hurt people in a long power cut are **fire, carbon monoxide, cold and falls in the dark**. Prepare for those first. Then take sensible steps against theft.

## Keep a low profile
- **Light:** when the street is dark, a brightly lit house stands out. Close curtains or use blackout blinds, and use a dim torch or lamp.
- **Noise:** generators are loud and easily stolen. If you must run one, run it outside on a chain or lock, only when needed.
- **Smells:** cooking smells travel. Cook at ordinary mealtimes, like everyone else.
- **Supplies:** don't tell people outside your trusted circle how much food, fuel or water you have. Don't leave empty packaging or gas bottles where passers-by can see them.
- **Social media:** posts showing your stores, or that you've gone away, are an invitation.

## Doors, windows and locks
- Lock doors and windows even when you're home, especially at night. Use the window locks.
- Keep keys out of sight and away from the letterbox and windows, but where everyone in the house can find them fast in a fire.
- Check outside: sheds and garages locked, ladders and tools put away, gates closed.
- A dog barking, a light coming on, or someone at the window is often enough to send an opportunist away.

!warn Don't trap yourself in
Every adult in the house must be able to unlock the exits quickly in the dark. Never block doors, nail windows shut or lock people in. In a house fire, you have only a minute or two to get out.
!end

## Safe room
Pick one room you can retreat to: ideally with a lockable door, a window you could escape through (ground or first floor), and a way to raise the alarm (a whistle, a torch for signalling). Keep water, a torch and a first-aid kit there. It is for keeping people safe while a threat passes, not for defending property.

## Avoid confrontation
- If someone tries to get in, **don't go out to face them**. Make noise from inside: shout that the police are coming, switch on lights, blow a whistle.
- If you meet someone in the house: let them leave. Don't block their way out.
- **De-escalate:** stay calm, speak slowly and quietly, keep your hands visible and open, keep a distance of a couple of metres, and don't argue. Offer a way out. If they want food or water, giving some may end it safely.
- Write down descriptions, times and vehicle details afterwards in the [[t:notebook]], and report it to police when you can reach them.

!law Self-defence and the law
In the UK you may use **reasonable force** to protect yourself or others, or to prevent a crime. In England and Wales, householders defending themselves against an intruder in their home have extra protection, but force that is **grossly disproportionate** is never lawful. Carrying a weapon or anything adapted as one in public is a criminal offence. Getting away safely is always the best outcome.
!end

## Neighbours watching out
Agree with neighbours to keep an eye on each other's homes, especially for people who are away or living alone. Share a simple signal, such as a card in the window, meaning "we're fine" or "we need help". See [[a:community]].

## Fire safety
- **Torches and battery lanterns** are far safer than candles.
- If you use candles: in a steady, heat-resistant holder, at least 1 m from curtains and anything that can burn, out of reach of children and pets. Never leave a candle burning in an empty room, never move a lit candle, and put them all out before sleep.
- Test smoke alarms (they run on batteries). Have one on every floor.
- Keep escape routes clear and agree a meeting point outside.
- If there's a fire: get out, stay out, and send someone for help. Don't go back in. See [[a:fire-safety]].

---
!quick
1. **Knock on doors** of older, disabled and unwell neighbours, and families with babies. Visit **twice a day**.
2. Make a **list on paper**: who lives where, who needs help, who has what skills and kit.
3. Set a **noticeboard** and a **daily meeting time and place** to share news when phones are down.
4. Find out what your **council** and community emergency plan are doing, and pass information both ways.
!end

## Why it matters
In UK emergencies, neighbours are usually the first help anyone gets. Councils, emergency services and charities prioritise the worst-hit areas and the most vulnerable people, and it can take days to reach everyone. A street that works together stays warmer, fed, informed and safer.

## Check on vulnerable people first
- **Who:** older people living alone, people with dementia, disabled people, anyone on **powered medical equipment** (oxygen, dialysis, hoists, stairlifts), people with young babies, and anyone who doesn't speak much English.
- **What to check:** Are they warm? Do they have water, food and medicines? Any signs of cold, confusion or illness ([[a:hypothermia]])? Is there carbon monoxide risk from how they're heating or cooking ([[a:cold-house]])?
- Visit morning and evening. Agree a sign in the window: a green card for "fine", red for "need help".
- Anyone dependent on powered equipment in a long cut usually needs to move somewhere with power. Get word to the council or emergency services early.

## Pool resources
- **Warmth:** one warm house can host several neighbours. Take turns, and share fuel.
- **Cooking:** a few camping stoves or a wood burner can serve a whole street. Cook in batches ([[a:cooking-without-power]]).
- **Water:** share stored water fairly, with priority for drinking, babies and the sick. Collect rainwater together.
- **Power:** a generator or solar panel can charge everyone's phones, radios and torches. Run generators outside only.
- **Tools and transport:** ladders, saws, a working car with fuel for emergencies.
- Keep it fair and written down: who lent what. That avoids arguments later.

## Skills list
On paper, list who can help with what:
- First aid, nursing, medical or care work.
- Electricians, plumbers, gas engineers, builders.
- Anyone with a radio licence, a 4×4, a chainsaw licence, or other languages.
- Childcare, cooking for groups, organising.

## Communication without phones
- **Noticeboard:** a board, door or window in a central place. Post the date and time on every notice, and take down old ones.
- **Daily meeting:** same place, same time (say 10:00 and 17:00). Short, practical: news, needs, offers, jobs.
- **Runners:** reliable teenagers or adults who carry messages between streets, the council's rest centre or the nearest working phone. Give them written notes, not just verbal messages.
- **Radios:** PMR446 walkie-talkies are licence-free in the UK and work over a few hundred metres to a couple of kilometres. Agree a channel and listening times to save batteries.
- **Battery or wind-up radio:** one person listens to BBC local radio at set times and posts the news on the board.
- Write important messages using a clear format: who, where, what's needed, when. Keep a log in the [[t:notebook]].

## Community emergency plans
- Many towns and parishes in the UK have a **community emergency plan**, often run by a parish, town or community council, with named volunteers and a building used as an emergency hub.
- **Local Resilience Forums** (in Scotland, Regional and Local Resilience Partnerships; in Northern Ireland, Emergency Preparedness Groups) bring councils, emergency services, the NHS and utilities together to plan for emergencies. Their websites publish local risk registers and advice. Find yours once things are back to normal, and consider joining your local plan.
- In a major incident, councils open **rest centres** for people who can't stay at home. Find out where yours is from local radio or the council.

## Keeping things calm
- Agree simple rules early: sharing, quiet hours, where the toilet waste goes ([[a:toilets-fail]]).
- Include everyone; tension grows when some people feel left out.
- Look after volunteers: rest, food and turns. Tired people make mistakes.

=== go-in-stay-in
title: Go in, stay in, tune in
summary: The UK advice for chemical, radiation or toxic smoke incidents: get inside, shut out the air, stay put until told, and listen for official news.

icon: radio
level: easy
related: decontamination, bug-out-or-stay, grab-bag, community
---
!quick
1. **Go in:** get inside the nearest solid building straight away. Bring pets in.
2. **Stay in:** close doors and windows, **turn off ventilation, extractor fans and air conditioning**. Stay away from windows.
3. **Tune in:** listen to **BBC local radio** or official news, and stay put until you are told it's safe.
4. **Don't collect children from school:** schools have plans to keep them safe inside.
!end

## When to do this
Do this if you're told to by the emergency services, an Emergency Alert, or the radio, or if you're near a fire with heavy smoke, a chemical leak, a gas cloud, or an incident at a nuclear site. Buildings give much better protection than being outside or in a car.

## Go in
- Get into the **nearest solid building**: your home, a shop, an office, a school. Don't stay in a car if a building is close.
- Bring pets inside if you can do it quickly.
- If you were outside in the smoke, dust or spray, **take off your outer clothing at the door**, put it in a bag, and wash exposed skin. This removes most of the contamination. See [[a:decontamination]].

## Stay in
- **Close all doors and windows.** Shut curtains or blinds.
- **Turn off** ventilation, extractor fans, air conditioning, tumble dryers vented outside and any warm-air heating. Close fireplace dampers and trickle vents if you can.
- **Chemical cloud or smoke:** go to an **upstairs** room if you can. Many toxic gases are heavier than air and collect low down.
- **Radiation:** go to the **middle of the building**, or a basement if there is one, away from outside walls, windows and the roof.
- Block gaps around doors and windows with damp towels if fumes are getting in.
- **Stay in until you're told it's safe.** This could be a few hours, or longer. Don't go out to look.
- Use stored water and food ([[a:home-water-storage]]). Don't eat garden produce or drink rainwater collected during the incident until authorities say it's safe.

## Tune in
- **BBC local radio** and national BBC radio and TV broadcast official advice in emergencies. Local commercial stations and official websites and social media also carry it.
- **Emergency Alerts** are sent by the government to mobile phones in the affected area. This phone has no SIM and is kept in airplane mode, so **don't rely on it to receive them**. Listen to the radio instead.
- This phone's **FM radio app** works with **wired headphones** plugged in as the aerial. Switch it on at set times (on the hour and half hour, when news bulletins run) to save battery.
- Write down instructions and times in the [[t:notebook]].

## Children at school
Don't go out to collect them. You would put yourself at risk and could block roads the emergency services need. Schools have emergency plans and will keep children inside and cared for until it's safe. Follow official news for when and where to collect them.

## Potassium iodate tablets
- These protect only the **thyroid gland**, and only against **radioactive iodine** from a nuclear reactor incident. They don't protect against other radiation or chemicals.
- In the UK they are kept for people living near nuclear sites and given out by the authorities if needed.
- **Take them only when told to by the authorities**, at the dose they give. Taking them at the wrong time doesn't help and can cause side effects.

## If you're outdoors and can't get inside
- Move **across the wind** (at right angles to it), away from the smoke or cloud, and uphill if it's a gas that hugs the ground.
- Cover your nose and mouth with a cloth. This helps a little with dust and smoke, not with gases.
- Get inside as soon as you can, and remove outer clothing first ([[a:decontamination]]).

## When it's over
- Open windows and doors to air the building when you're told it's safe.
- Follow any advice about cleaning, food, water and gardens.
- Anyone feeling unwell (sore eyes, coughing, breathing problems, skin burning) needs medical advice when you can reach help: see [[a:when-you-reach-help]].

=== decontamination
title: Decontamination: remove, remove, remove
summary: The UK "Remove, Remove, Remove" steps after contact with a chemical or hazardous substance: get away, take off outer clothing, get it off your skin.
tags: decontamination, decon, chemical, contamination, hazardous substance, acid, corrosive, remove remove remove, rinse, clothing, chemical burn, spray, powder, radiation dust
icon: shower-head
time: 10–20 min
level: easy
needs: "scissors", "plastic bags", "kitchen roll or dry cloth", "clean water"
related: go-in-stay-in, burns, eye-injury, poisoning
---
!quick
1. **Remove yourself** from the area: move away to fresh air, upwind, without touching anything.
2. **Remove outer clothing**: cut it off rather than pulling it over your head. Bag it.
3. **Remove the substance** from skin: **blot** with dry absorbent material (kitchen roll, cloth). If skin is **burning or itching**, **rinse with plenty of water**.
4. Get someone to raise the alarm, and get medical help as soon as you can reach it ([[a:when-you-reach-help]]).
!end

## When to use this
Use these steps if someone has been exposed to a **chemical or hazardous substance**: a spray, liquid, powder or vapour from an attack, a spill, a factory or transport accident, or contaminated dust from a fire or radiation incident. The faster you do them, the less harm the substance does. Start straight away, before help arrives.

Protect yourself: wear gloves (washing-up gloves, or plastic bags over your hands) if you help someone else, and don't touch the substance.

## 1. Remove yourself
- Move away from the source, into fresh air. Go **upwind and uphill** if you can.
- Don't touch your face, eat, drink or smoke.
- If you're told to go inside, go in, then do steps 2 and 3 ([[a:go-in-stay-in]]).

## 2. Remove outer clothing
Taking off outer layers removes most of the contamination.
- Take off coats, jumpers, shirts, trousers, shoes. Keep underwear on unless it's soaked.
- **Don't pull clothing over your head.** Cut it off with scissors instead, cutting from the bottom upwards and peeling it away from the face.
- Avoid touching the outside of the clothing. Remove jewellery and watches too.
- Put everything in a **plastic bag**, tie it, then put that bag inside a second bag. Keep it away from people and pets: the emergency services may need it for testing.
- Keep warm afterwards with a blanket or clean dry clothes.

## 3. Remove the substance
- **Dry substance or unknown:** **blot** exposed skin with **dry absorbent material**: kitchen roll, tissues, a clean cloth, cotton wool. **Don't rub**: rubbing spreads it and pushes it into the skin.
- Start with the face and hands, then the rest. Use fresh material for each area and bag the used material.
- **If the skin is burning, itching or red, or it's a corrosive (acid or alkali):** **rinse with plenty of clean water**, continuously, for at least 10–20 minutes. Use a shower, hose, tap or bottles. Let the water run away from the person, not over unaffected skin.
- **Eyes:** remove contact lenses if easy, then rinse the eye with clean running water for at least 20 minutes, holding the eyelids open, from the inner corner outwards. See [[a:eye-injury]].
- **Swallowed:** rinse the mouth with water and spit it out. Don't make them vomit. See [[a:poisoning]].

!warn Corrosive (acid) attacks
For acid or other corrosive liquids, water matters most: **rinse immediately with lots of water** and keep going while you wait for help. Remove contaminated clothing carefully while rinsing. See [[a:burns]].
!end

## Afterwards: wash
Once you're away from the incident and the emergency services haven't told you otherwise:
- **Shower** in lukewarm water with soap or shampoo, from head to toe. Wash hair with shampoo only (no conditioner, which can bind dust and particles to hair). Don't scrub hard or scald the skin.
- Blow your nose, and wipe ears and eyelids gently with a damp cloth.
- Put on clean clothes. Bag the towels you used.
- If there's no running water, wipe down with damp cloths, top to bottom, using a fresh cloth for each part.

## Signs someone needs help urgently
Breathing problems, coughing or wheezing, chest tightness, confusion, drowsiness, fits, burning skin or eyes, or vomiting. Put them in the [[a:recovery-position]] if they're unconscious and breathing, and send someone to get help. Anyone exposed should get medical advice, even if they feel fine: some effects are delayed. Note what happened, when, and what was done in the [[t:casualty]] log.

## The emergency services' role
Ambulance and fire crews have specialist decontamination teams and equipment. If they arrive, follow their instructions. Don't leave the scene before you're told to, or you may spread contamination to your home and family.

!quick
1. **Get out of danger:** away from glass, damaged buildings, crowds and the smell of gas.
2. **Shelter:** home first, then friends or family, then an official **rest centre**.
3. **Head for your agreed meeting point** with family; leave a note if you move on.
4. Navigate with the **offline map** ([[t:map]]) and street signs: phone networks may be down.

## Shelter options
1. **Your own home**, if it's undamaged and safe. Usually the best choice ([[a:bug-out-or-stay]]).
2. **Friends or family** in an unaffected area.
3. **Rest centres:** councils open them in schools, leisure centres and community halls for people who can't go home. They give shelter, warmth, food and information. Local radio announces where they are.
4. **Public buildings:** stations, libraries, churches, shopping centres, hospitals (A&E waiting rooms are for patients). Ask staff.
5. **Last resort:** a doorway, car park stairwell, bus shelter or under a bridge, out of the wind and rain, with a survival bag and insulation from the ground ([[a:insulation-ground]]). Stay where you can be seen by passers-by, not hidden in an alley.

## Dangers in a damaged town

### Glass and falling debris
After explosions, storms or earthquakes, glass and masonry fall from buildings. Walk in the **middle of the street** or well away from tall buildings, and look up. Wear stout shoes, gloves and something on your head.

### Damaged buildings
**Don't go into** buildings with big cracks, leaning walls, sagging roofs or floors, or doors that have jammed in their frames. Aftershocks, fire and water damage can bring them down later. Don't climb on rubble: you can cause collapses.

### Gas leaks
- **Smell of gas (rotten eggs):** don't use light switches, phones, lighters or anything that could spark. Open doors and windows if you're inside, turn off the gas at the meter if you can (the lever across the pipe), and get out.
- Report it to the gas emergency number, **0800 111 999**, when you reach a working phone.

### Flood water
Don't walk or drive through it: 15 cm of moving water can knock you over, and 30 cm can float a car. It's often contaminated with sewage, and manholes may have lost their covers.

## Crowds
- Stay at the **edges** of crowds, near exits, and away from walls, barriers and pinch points.
- If it's getting dangerously packed: **move diagonally** towards the edge, going with the flow, not against it.
- Keep your **arms up in front of your chest** like a boxer to keep space to breathe.
- **Stay on your feet.** If you fall, get up fast. If you can't, curl on your side, protect your head with your arms, and keep your knees up.

!warn Run, hide, tell
If there's an attack with weapons: **Run** if there's a safe route, leaving belongings. **Hide** if you can't: lock yourself in, silence your phone, keep away from the door. **Tell** the police when it's safe, as soon as you can reach someone with a phone.
!end

## Food
- Carry **cash**: card payments and cash machines fail when the power or networks go. Small notes and coins are easiest.
- Shops near the edge of an affected area may still open. Supermarkets often have backup power.
- Rest centres, councils, charities, faith groups and food banks give out food in emergencies.
- Ask and offer: people in towns usually help each other. See [[a:community]].

## Getting around without phone signal
- Use the [[t:map]] (offline) and [[t:gps]]. GPS works without a SIM or signal.
- Street name signs are usually on corner buildings. Bus stops show stop names and routes. Rivers, railway lines, main roads and tall landmarks are good handrails ([[a:handrails-catching]]).
- Write your route and key addresses on paper before you set off.
- Night: streetlights may be out. Use a head torch, walk on lit or main roads where possible, and keep in pairs.

## Keep a low profile
- Dress like everyone else, in plain dark or neutral clothes. Don't show off expensive kit, food or water.
- Carry valuables and the phone in inside pockets. Keep your bag zipped and in front of you in crowds.
- Avoid groups that are angry or drinking, and areas where trouble is starting. Leave early.
- Stay alert: headphones off, phone away.

## Meeting points
Every family should agree two meeting points in advance:
1. **Near home:** a neighbour's house or the end of the street, for a fire or local problem.
2. **Away from home:** a friend's house, a library or a church across town, for when you can't get home.

Agree a time to meet (for example, on the hour, every hour, during daylight) and what to do if nobody comes. Leave a dated note at the meeting point if you move on. See [[a:grab-bag]] for writing contacts on paper.

!quick
1. **First:** water already in buildings (pipes, hot water cylinders, toilet cisterns) and **official bottled-water stations**.
2. **Next:** rain from roofs and gutters, and water butts.
3. **Last resort:** canals, urban rivers and ponds: heavily polluted, **boil** and use only if nothing else.

## Official supplies
If the mains fails, water companies must provide at least **10 litres per person per day**, usually as bottled-water stations or bowsers (tanks on trailers). Local radio and the council will say where. Take containers with lids and help neighbours who can't carry water. Bowser water may need boiling: check the label on the tank.

## Water in buildings
- **Your home and workplace:** pipes, the hot water cylinder, the loft tank and toilet cisterns. See [[a:home-water-storage]] for how to drain them.
- **Offices, schools and flats** often have large cold-water tanks and hot water cylinders. Ask the caretaker or building manager.
- Water from tanks and cylinders isn't always drinking quality. **Boil it.**

## Shops, cafés and public places
- Shops may still sell bottled water, or give it out. Supermarkets often have backup power.
- The **Refill** scheme: cafés, shops and some public buildings with a Refill sticker in the window will fill your bottle with tap water for free.
- **Public toilets**, stations and parks may have taps that still run for a while after the mains pressure drops, because they're fed from storage tanks.
- **Drinking fountains** in parks and stations.

## Outside taps
Many commercial buildings have an outside tap with no handle, called a **sillcock**. It needs a special key (a four-way or sillcock key, sold in DIY shops). Ask the owner or manager first.

!law Taking water
Water from someone else's tap is theirs. Ask permission. Taking water from the mains without the water company's permission, including from **fire hydrants**, is an offence and can damage the network or leave firefighters without water. Never tamper with hydrants.
!end

## Rain
- Rain is usually the cleanest water in a town. Collect from **downpipes** (pull the pipe away from the drain, or cut into it), **gutters**, or a tarp or clean plastic sheet.
- Let the **first few minutes of rain** wash the roof, then collect.
- Roofs collect bird droppings, moss and dust. **Boil** before drinking. See [[a:rain-dew-collection]].
- Avoid water off roofs with old lead, tar or chemical coatings, and water butts with green slime or a chemical smell.

## Swimming pools and hot tubs
- A full swimming pool holds tens of thousands of litres, but it's treated with chlorine and other chemicals, which boiling won't remove ([[a:boiling-limits]]).
- Best used for **washing and flushing toilets**.
- If you have to drink it: take it only from a pool that's been maintained recently, strain it through cloth, **boil it**, and use it for as short a time as possible. Don't drink pool water with a green tinge, algae or a strong chemical smell.
- Hot tubs are poor sources: warm, with bromine or chlorine, and often bacteria.

## Canals, rivers and ponds
- Urban waterways carry **sewage, road runoff, oil, metals** and rat urine, which spreads **leptospirosis** (Weil's disease).
- Only use them **if there is nothing else**. Collect from flowing water, away from drains, outfalls and scum. Settle, strain and boil ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]). Boiling kills germs but doesn't remove chemicals.
- Cover cuts with waterproof plasters and wash your hands after touching the water.
- Never drink from **floodwater**. It's heavily contaminated.

## Water you can't drink, but can use
Bath water, dehumidifier water, aquarium water and radiator water are **not safe to drink**, even boiled (radiators contain corrosion inhibitors). Some can be used for flushing toilets ([[a:toilets-fail]]).

## Using it wisely
Drink first, then cooking, then washing hands, then everything else. A person needs 2–3 litres a day to drink. Keep drinking water separate from washing water, in labelled containers. Use [[t:calc]] to work out how much your group needs.

=== bug-out-or-stay
title: Bug out or shelter in place?
summary: How to decide whether to stay at home or leave in an emergency, the triggers for leaving, where to go, and how to leave safely and early.
tags: bug out, evacuate, evacuation, leave home, shelter in place, stay at home, flood warning, fire, gas leak, plan, family plan, rest centre, leave early
icon: signpost-big
level: easy
related: grab-bag, go-in-stay-in, urban-survival, stay-or-go
---
!quick
1. **Staying at home is usually safer**: you have shelter, water, food and neighbours.
2. **Leave** if your home is unsafe (fire, gas, flooding, structural damage), or **when officials tell you to**.
3. If you're leaving, **leave early**, take your **grab bag**, and go to a planned place.
4. **Tell people** where you've gone: a note inside the door, with neighbours, or at a meeting point.
!end

## Why staying is usually best
Your home gives you shelter, warmth, stored food and water, a toilet, medicines and neighbours who know you. Most UK emergencies (storms, power cuts, cold snaps, water cuts) are best dealt with at home. Moving into the unknown, especially in bad weather or with children or older relatives, brings its own dangers: cold, crashes, flooded roads, getting stranded.

## Triggers to leave
Leave when staying is more dangerous than going:
- **The emergency services tell you to evacuate.** Go when told, and follow their route.
- **Severe Flood Warning** (danger to life), or a Flood Warning where your home has flooded before. Move to higher ground early, before roads are cut off.
- **Fire:** in the house, or a wildfire or large fire heading your way.
- **Gas leak** you can't stop, or a strong smell of gas.
- **Structural damage:** big cracks, a damaged roof, a chimney about to fall.
- **No heat in extreme cold** for vulnerable people, or no water or sanitation for days, with help available elsewhere.
- **Chemical or radiation incident:** usually stay in ([[a:go-in-stay-in]]) unless told to leave.

## Where to go
1. **Friends or family** outside the affected area. Agree this in advance.
2. **Council rest centres:** local radio and council websites announce them.
3. A **second home or caravan**, if you have one, away from the hazard.

Choose two or three options in different directions, in case one route is blocked.

## Make a plan now
- Agree with your household: where to meet if you're split up (near home, and away from home), who collects whom, who checks on elderly relatives.
- Write down addresses and phone numbers **on paper**. Phones die, and this phone can't make calls.
- Plan for pets: carriers, leads, food. Many rest centres can't take pets (assistance dogs are welcome), so plan where they would go.
- Know how to turn off your **gas, electricity and water**.
- Keep a [[a:grab-bag]] packed and ready by the door.
- Keep the car's tank at least half full when warnings are likely.

## Leave early
Leaving early, in daylight, while roads are clear and you can still choose, is much safer than leaving late in a crowd, in the dark or through water.
- **Don't drive through floodwater.** 30 cm of moving water can float a car; 15 cm can knock you off your feet. Turn around.
- Check weather and road news before setting off.
- Take the grab bag, medicines, glasses, chargers, phone, the [[a:solar-panel]], warm and waterproof clothes, and water.

## Before you go
If you have time and it's safe:
- Turn off gas, electricity and water at the mains if told to, or if you're expecting flooding.
- Move valuables and documents upstairs if flooding is likely. Put sandbags or flood boards in place.
- Close and lock windows and doors.
- Take rubbish out, empty the fridge of food that will spoil.

## Telling people where you've gone
- Leave a note **inside**, visible through the letterbox or just inside the door, with where you've gone, who's with you and the date and time. Rescuers and family will look there.
- A note on the outside of the door tells everyone your home is empty, so only do this if the emergency services ask for it.
- Tell a neighbour and leave word at your agreed meeting point.

## If you decide to stay
Prepare for several days on your own: water ([[a:home-water-storage]]), warmth ([[a:cold-house]]), food ([[a:home-food-storage]]), light and information ([[a:grid-down-first]]). Keep your grab bag packed in case things change. Decide in advance what would make you leave, and go as soon as it happens.

=== grab-bag
title: Grab bag
summary: What to pack in an emergency grab bag, based on UK government and British Red Cross lists, plus how to keep it ready.
tags: grab bag, go bag, bug out bag, emergency bag, emergency kit, evacuation kit, 72 hour kit, checklist, documents, medicines, packing list
icon: backpack
time: 1–2 hours to pack
level: easy
needs: this-phone, solar-panel, first-aid-kit, head-torch
related: bug-out-or-stay, kit-checklist, kit-phone, home-food-storage
---
!quick
1. Pack **one bag per person**, light enough to carry for an hour, and keep it **by the door**.
2. Priorities: **medicines, copies of documents, cash, water, torch, radio, warm clothes, contact list on paper**.
3. **Check it every 6 months**: swap food and water, check medicines, charge batteries.
!end

## Why a grab bag
If you have to leave in a hurry because of fire, flood, a gas leak or an evacuation, you may have minutes. A packed bag means you leave with the things that matter, and don't go back into danger for them.

## What bag
A rucksack is best: it leaves your hands free. 25–40 litres for an adult. Waterproof the contents with bin liners or dry bags. Children can carry a small bag with their own comfort items.

!checklist Essentials
- Prescription medicines (at least a week's supply), and a written list of medicines and doses
- Copies of important documents in a waterproof bag: passports, driving licence, insurance policies, bank details, prescriptions
- Cash in small notes and coins
- Contact list on paper: family, friends, doctor, insurance, school, work
- Torch or head torch, with spare batteries
- Battery or wind-up radio
- This phone, charged, with its cable and plug
- Power bank and the solar panel
- Bottled water: at least 2 litres per person
- Snacks that keep: cereal bars, nuts, dried fruit
- Warm layers, a hat and gloves, and a waterproof jacket
- Spare glasses, contact lenses, hearing aid batteries
- Spare house and car keys
!end

!checklist Also useful
- First-aid kit: plasters, dressings, tape, painkillers, hand sanitiser
- Toiletries: toothbrush, soap, sanitary products, toilet paper, wet wipes
- Whistle
- Survival bag or blanket
- Notebook and pencil
- Multi-tool or small folding knife (know the law on carrying one: [[a:knife-safety]])
- Face masks and disposable gloves
- Bin bags (waterproofing, a poncho, rubbish)
- Copies of children's comfort items, games and a book
- Baby supplies: nappies, formula, bottles, wipes
- Pet supplies: food, lead, carrier, vet records
- Map of your area and a compass
!end

## This phone
This phone is useful in a grab bag even without a SIM: it has offline maps ([[t:map]]), GPS ([[t:gps]]), a torch, an FM radio app (with wired headphones) and this guide. Pack wired headphones and its charging cable. Keep it powered off until you need it. See [[a:kit-phone]].

## Documents
- Keep photocopies (not originals) in the bag, in a sealed plastic wallet. Store originals in a safe, fireproof place.
- Write down: NHS number, blood group, allergies, medical conditions, insurance policy numbers, utility account numbers, vehicle registration.
- A list of addresses for your meeting points and places you might go ([[a:bug-out-or-stay]]).

## Keep it ready
- Store it near the main exit, or in the car in winter. Everyone in the house should know where it is.
- Every **6 months** (say, when the clocks change): swap water and snacks, check medicines aren't out of date, test the torch and radio, charge the power bank, update the contact list.
- Add seasonal kit in winter: extra warm layers, hand warmers.
- Don't overpack. If you can't walk a few kilometres with it, take things out.

## Plus a home kit
The grab bag is for leaving. If you're staying, you also need a home store of water, food, light and warmth for at least 3 days: see [[a:home-water-storage]], [[a:home-food-storage]] and [[a:grid-down-first]].

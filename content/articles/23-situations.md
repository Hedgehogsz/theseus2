=== car-snow
title: Stranded in a car in snow
summary: Stay with the car, keep the exhaust clear, run the engine in short bursts, stay warm and make the car easy to find.
tags: car, snow, stranded, stuck, blizzard, snowdrift, vehicle, winter driving, motorway, carbon monoxide, exhaust, breakdown
icon: car-front
level: moderate
needs: warm-layers, survival-bag, head-torch, "blanket", "shovel", "food and drink"
related: hypothermia, be-visible, signalling-rescue, stay-or-go
---
!quick
1. **Stay with the car.** It is shelter, and searchers and ploughs find cars far more easily than people on foot.
2. **Clear snow from the exhaust** before running the engine. Run it about **10 minutes in every hour** for heat, with a downwind window **open a crack**.
3. **Wrap up and insulate:** all spare clothes on, hat on, something between you and the seat; move your arms and legs often.
4. Make the car **visible**: bright cloth on the aerial or door, interior light at night, hazard lights in short bursts.
!end

!danger Carbon monoxide kills quietly
If snow blocks the exhaust, fumes come into the car. You will not smell them. **Dig the exhaust clear every time before you start the engine**, and check it again during heavy snow. Keep a window open about 1 cm on the side away from the wind while the engine runs. Headache, dizziness, nausea or drowsiness: switch off and get fresh air at once.
!end

## Why you stay
People who leave their car in a blizzard become lost, exhausted and hypothermic within a few hundred metres, often on roads they know. Drifting snow hides the road edge, ditches and drops. The car keeps you out of the wind and wet, which is most of the battle ([[a:cold-and-wet]]). On a blocked main road, police, highways crews and ploughs work along the road: they will reach the car.

## Keeping warm
- **Engine:** run it for about 10 minutes an hour with the heater on full, then switch off. This saves fuel and battery, and limits fumes. Never sleep with the engine running.
- **Layers:** put on everything: coats, hats, gloves, spare socks. Tuck trouser legs into socks. A survival bag or blanket over your legs and body traps a lot of heat.
- **Insulate:** the seat and floor draw heat out. Sit on a folded coat, floor mats or a rucksack; put your feet on a bag, not the floor.
- **Share heat:** sit close together in the back seat under shared blankets. Keep children between adults.
- **Move:** clench and relax your muscles, wiggle toes and fingers, and swing your arms every 20–30 minutes. It keeps blood flowing to hands and feet. Don't exercise so hard that you sweat.
- **Eat and drink:** food is fuel. Small amounts often.
- **Electric cars:** there's no exhaust, so no carbon monoxide risk. Use the heated seats and steering wheel (they use far less battery than heating the air), and pre-set a modest cabin temperature. Check the remaining range regularly.

## Water: melt snow, don't eat it
Eating snow chills you from the inside and costs body heat to melt. Pack snow into a bottle and keep it inside your coat, or stand it near a heater vent while the engine runs. Clean, fresh snow from above the ground is best: avoid yellow or grey snow and snow from the road edge.

## Being found
- Tie a **bright cloth** (scarf, hi-vis vest, carrier bag) to the aerial, wing mirror or door handle.
- At night, switch the interior light on for short periods. Use **hazard lights** in bursts when you hear traffic or see lights: they drain the battery.
- Keep the roof and windows clear of snow if safe, so the car is visible from the road and the air.
- Sound the horn in **six blasts** when you hear or see anyone ([[a:signalling-rescue]]).
- If you get out to clear the exhaust or look around, stay within sight of the car and hold onto it in poor visibility. Wear hi-vis on a road.

## When leaving is right
Leave only if:
- you can **clearly see** a building, a manned vehicle or people close by, and the weather allows you to get there and back; or
- the car is in danger (fire, deep water, or stuck where traffic could hit it, in which case move well away from the road behind a barrier and wait).

Before you go, leave a **note** on the dashboard saying who you are, when you left and where you went. Walk in pairs if you can, and never in a whiteout or after dark. See [[a:stay-or-go]].

!checklist Winter car kit (AA/RAC advice)
- Ice scraper and de-icer
- Torch with spare batteries, or a head torch
- Warm coat, hat, gloves, boots and a blanket or sleeping bag
- Survival bag or foil blanket
- Small shovel
- Bottled water and high-energy snacks
- First-aid kit
- Hi-vis vest and warning triangle
- Phone power bank and charging cable
- Jump leads
- Sacks or old carpet for grip under the wheels
- Fuel tank at least a quarter full in winter, ideally more
!end

## Before you set off in snow
- Check the Met Office forecast and warnings, and travel news. If it says don't travel, don't.
- Tell someone your route and when you expect to arrive.
- Stick to main roads, which are gritted and ploughed first.

=== cold-water-immersion
title: Cold water: float to live
summary: Fell into cold water? Fight the urge to swim. Lean back and float until the gasping stops, then swim or signal.
tags: cold water, cold water shock, float to live, fall in, drowning, sea, river, lake, reservoir, swimming, capsized, RNLI, HELP position, huddle
icon: waves-horizontal
level: hard
related: water-rescue, hypothermia, cpr-adult, river-crossing
---
!quick
1. **Don't swim yet.** The first minute or two of gasping is the killer. **Lean back, ears in the water, arms and legs spread out**, and float.
2. **Breathe.** Wait until your breathing is under control. This usually takes **60–90 seconds**, sometimes up to 3 minutes.
3. **Then act:** swim to safety if it's close, or keep floating and **shout and wave** for help.
4. **Keep your clothes on**: they trap air and help you float.
!end

## What cold water does to you
Water under 15 °C counts as cold, and UK seas, rivers and lakes are colder than that for most of the year. Even in summer, reservoirs and rivers are cold a metre below the surface.

| Stage | When | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold water shock** | First 1–3 minutes | Uncontrollable gasping, fast breathing, panic, a jump in heart rate and blood pressure. Gasping underwater is how most people drown. |
| **Swimming failure** | About 10–30 minutes | Arms and legs cool, muscles weaken and stop working. Even strong swimmers can't keep their head up. |
| **Hypothermia** | Usually after 30 minutes or more | Core temperature falls; confusion, then unconsciousness. |

The point: the first minutes are about **breathing**, not warmth. You have more time than you think if you float.

## Float to live (the RNLI method)
1. **Lean back** and tilt your head back, ears in the water.
2. **Spread your arms and legs** out like a starfish. Gently scull with your hands if you need to.
3. It's normal for your legs to sink a bit. Everyone floats differently.
4. **Fight the instinct** to thrash or swim hard to the bank. It makes the gasping worse and you go under.
5. Once your breathing is steady, decide: swim to safety if it's near, or stay floating and **shout, wave and blow a whistle**.

**In moving water** (a river): float on your back, **feet first downstream** and up near the surface to fend off rocks, and angle towards the slower water at the bank. Don't try to stand up in fast water: your foot can trap under a rock.

## Clothing and lifejackets
- **Keep clothes and shoes on** unless they are dragging you down. They trap air and slow heat loss.
- A lifejacket or buoyancy aid does the floating for you: you can spend all your energy on breathing and signalling.

## Waiting to be rescued
- **HELP position** (Heat Escape Lessening Posture), with a lifejacket: draw your knees to your chest, cross your ankles, press your arms against your sides. This protects the armpits, chest and groin, where you lose most heat.
- **Huddle**, for two or more with lifejackets: face each other in a tight circle, arms around each other, legs together. It saves heat, keeps people together and is easier for rescuers to spot.
- Without a lifejacket, keep still and float. Swimming burns heat fast.
- If you capsized, **stay with the boat** or anything that floats and climb as far out of the water as you can.

## Getting out
- Climb out where the bank is low and firm: steps, a slipway, a beach. Steep, muddy or concrete banks are very hard to climb with cold arms.
- Once out, get **out of the wind**, strip wet layers, and put on anything dry ([[a:hypothermia]]).

## After a rescue
- **Keep them roughly horizontal** while lifting them out and afterwards, if you can. Pulling a very cold person upright can make their blood pressure crash.
- Treat for [[a:hypothermia]]: shelter, dry clothes, insulate from the ground, warm the core gently.
- If they are not breathing normally, start CPR with **5 rescue breaths** first ([[a:cpr-adult]], [[t:cpr]]).
- **Anyone who breathed in water** needs to be seen by a doctor, even if they seem fine. Watch them closely for the next day.

!warn Signs of trouble after inhaling water
A cough that won't stop, breathlessness or fast breathing, chest pain, foam or froth at the mouth, confusion, or unusual sleepiness. These can appear hours later. **Send for help** or get them to a hospital at once.
!end

=== water-rescue
title: Helping someone in the water
summary: Shout, reach, throw: how to help someone in the water without becoming the second casualty.
tags: rescue, drowning, someone in water, throw line, throw bag, lifebuoy, reach, ice, frozen pond, river, sea, lake
icon: hand-helping
level: hard
needs: cord, "throw bag or lifebuoy"
related: cold-water-immersion, recovery-position, cpr-adult, hypothermia
---
!quick
1. **Don't go in.** Many people drown trying to save someone else.
2. **Shout** to them: "**Float on your back! Kick to me!**" Send someone for help now.
3. **Reach** with a stick, branch or coat while lying down on the bank, or **throw** anything that floats.
4. Keep them in sight. If they go under, note **exactly where**, using a landmark on the far bank.
!end

## The order: shout, reach, throw, row, go
Work down this list. Only move to the next step if the one above won't work.

### 1. Shout
- Tell them to **float**: "Lean back, arms out, float!" Cold water shock makes people thrash and gasp ([[a:cold-water-immersion]]). If they float for a minute, their breathing settles and they can help themselves.
- Tell them to **kick towards you** or to the nearest low bank or steps.
- Shout for others to help. **Send someone for help**: at the coast ask for the **Coastguard**, inland for the **Fire service** ([[a:when-you-reach-help]]). Look for a public **lifebuoy or throw line** on a post nearby.

### 2. Reach
- **Lie down** on the bank or jetty so you can't be pulled in. Have someone hold your legs if possible.
- Reach out with a long stick, branch, paddle, fishing rod, scarf, belt or coat. Hold it out, let them grab, then pull in steadily.
- Never reach with your hand alone unless they're right by the edge and you're braced.

### 3. Throw
- Throw something that floats: a **lifebuoy**, throw bag, ball, cool box, empty plastic bottle (a 2 litre bottle with a little water in it throws further), a lifejacket, or a log.
- A **throw bag**: hold the end of the line (don't tie it to yourself), throw underarm **beyond** them so the line falls across them. Tell them to hold the line on their chest and roll on their back. Pull in steadily, or in a river let the current swing them to the bank downstream of you.
- If they can't hold a line, throw floats as close to them as you can so they have something to hold.

### 4. Row
Only if you have a boat, know how to use it, and wear a lifejacket. Approach from downwind or downstream, and pull them in over the back of the boat, not the side.

### 5. Go (last resort)
Only if you are a strong swimmer, the water is calm, they are close, and you **take something that floats** to give them. Never let them grab you: a panicking person will push you under. Hand them the float from arm's length. If they grab you, go under: they let go.

## Ice
- **Don't go onto the ice.** If someone falls through, shout to them to **keep still**, spread their arms on the ice and kick to stay up.
- **Reach** from the bank, lying flat: a branch, pole, ladder, rope or scarf.
- If you **must** go out: lie flat and spread your weight, pushing a ladder, plank or branch ahead of you. Never stand up.
- **Self-rescue, if you go through:** turn back towards where you came from (that ice held you). Spread your arms on the ice, kick your legs to get horizontal, and slide forwards onto the ice like a seal. Then **roll** away from the hole. Don't stand until you're on thick ice or the bank.

## After you get them out
- **Breathing?** Check for 10 seconds. If they're breathing but not responding, put them in the [[a:recovery-position]] and watch their breathing all the time: they may vomit water.
- **Not breathing normally?** Start CPR with **5 rescue breaths** first ([[a:cpr-adult]], [[a:cpr-child]], [[t:cpr]]).
- Keep them roughly horizontal, out of the wind, and treat for [[a:hypothermia]].
- **Everyone** who was rescued from water should be checked by a doctor: breathing problems can appear hours later.
- Look after yourself too: you're probably wet and cold.

=== tidal-cutoff
title: Cut off by the tide
summary: Trapped by a rising tide or stuck in mud: get above the high-tide line, don't try to swim round, and signal.
tags: tide, cut off, trapped, beach, cove, headland, sandbank, high tide, strandline, quicksand, mud, estuary, coastguard
icon: waves-arrow-up
level: moderate
related: coast-and-tides, signalling-rescue, cold-water-immersion, hypothermia
---
!quick
1. **Get high:** climb above the **strandline** (the line of seaweed and rubbish from the last high tide), well above it if waves are big. Don't climb cliffs.
2. **Don't swim or wade** round a headland or across a channel. The water is deeper, faster and colder than it looks.
3. **Signal:** wave, whistle six blasts, shine a torch, shout to anyone on the cliff path or a boat.
4. **Stay together and keep warm** until the tide drops or help comes. High water passes; you may only wait a few hours.
!end

## Why the UK catches people out
Britain has some of the biggest tides in the world. The **Severn Estuary** range can exceed 14 m, and many coasts rise 5–10 m between low and high water. Big, flat bays like **Morecambe Bay** and the Wash flood across the sand faster than you can walk, and channels fill behind you first. The water rises fastest in the **middle hours** of the tide ([[a:coast-and-tides]]).

**Spring tides** (around full and new moon) come higher and go lower than neap tides. An onshore gale or low pressure pushes the sea higher again.

## Before you go
- **Check tide times** for that exact place (tide tables are posted at many harbours and beaches, and in local shops).
- Start back along a beach or across sand **while the tide is still going out**, or at least 2–3 hours before high water.
- Look at the cove or causeway at low tide: where does the sea reach? Is there a path up?
- Watch the time as well as the water.

## If you're cut off
1. **Stop.** Don't panic and don't try to beat the tide round the point.
2. **Find the highest safe ground:** a ledge, a raised beach, rocks above the strandline. Look for wet rock and fresh seaweed: those get covered.
3. **Keep away from the cliff base** (rockfalls) and from spots where waves funnel in.
4. **Don't climb cliffs** unless you have a clear, easy route all the way to the top. Many coastal rescues are people stuck halfway up.
5. **Signal for help** ([[a:signalling-rescue]]): six whistle blasts or torch flashes a minute, waving both arms. Anyone who sees you can get the **Coastguard** ([[a:when-you-reach-help]]).
6. **Stay together.** Never send anyone to swim for help.
7. **Get warm:** sit on a rucksack or coat, put on all layers, huddle in the lee of a rock. The wind off the sea is cold even in summer ([[a:hypothermia]]).

If you're swept in: [[a:cold-water-immersion|float to live]].

## Stuck in mud or quicksand
Estuaries and bays (the Severn, Dee, Morecambe Bay, the Wash) have soft mud and quicksand that grips your legs. The danger isn't sinking out of sight: it's being held while the tide comes in.
- **Don't struggle** or pull hard on one leg. That works you deeper.
- **Lean back** and spread your arms to spread your weight. Lying back on the mud stops you sinking further.
- **Wriggle your legs slowly** to let water and air in around them, then work them up a little at a time, rolling towards firmer ground.
- **Others: don't go in after them.** Throw them a coat, branch or board to lie on, and send someone for the Coastguard.
- Avoid mudflats, and if you must cross wet sand, test with a stick and keep to firm, ribbed sand.

## Guided crossings
Some bays (Morecambe Bay, Lindisfarne causeway) are only safe with an official guide or within published crossing times. Obey the safe crossing times and the signs.

=== flood-outdoors
title: Caught in a flood outdoors
summary: Fast rising water outdoors: get to high ground, never walk or drive through flood water, and watch for flash floods.
tags: flood, flash flood, flooding, rising water, heavy rain, river burst, flooded road, ford, driving, spate, contamination
icon: cloud-drizzle
level: moderate
related: river-crossing, stay-or-go, flood-home, water-hygiene
---
!quick
1. **Get to high ground now**, away from rivers, streams, gullies and low roads. Go up the slope, not along the valley floor.
2. **Never walk or drive through flood water.** **15 cm** of fast water can knock you over; **30 cm** can move a car.
3. **Stay out** of the water: it hides open manholes, ditches and debris, and is full of sewage.
4. Wait it out on high ground. Floods on small rivers often drop within hours.
!end

## How strong flood water is
| Depth of moving water | What it can do |
|---|---|
| **15 cm** (6 in) | Knock an adult off their feet |
| **30 cm** (1 ft) | Move or float a car |
| **60 cm** (2 ft) | Float most cars, including 4x4s |

You can't tell the depth or the speed from the surface. Roads under water may have been washed away beneath. Most flood deaths in the UK are people who walked or drove into the water.

## Flash floods
- Happen in **steep, narrow valleys** after heavy downpours or thunderstorms, sometimes miles away and out of sight upstream. The Boscastle flood in Cornwall (2004) rose in hours from a summer storm.
- Signs: rain rising fast, a **roar** upstream, water turning muddy and full of sticks, the level rising quickly.
- **Act at once:** climb the valley side straight away. Don't try to get to the car or pack up camp.
- Don't camp right beside streams or on low gravel banks in hill country, especially with storms forecast. Pick a site well above the stream ([[a:site-selection]]).

## On foot
- Go **uphill** and away from the water. Bridges, embankments and riverbanks can collapse.
- If water is rising around you, stand on the highest thing you can safely reach and **signal** ([[a:signalling-rescue]]).
- If you're swept in: **float on your back, feet first downstream**, and steer to the bank ([[a:cold-water-immersion]]).
- Don't cross a swollen stream to get "home": wait, or go round by a bridge ([[a:river-crossing]]).

## In a vehicle
- **Turn round.** Don't drive into flood water or through a flooded ford. Even if others have, you don't know what's under it.
- If water rises around the car and it starts to float, **get out quickly** through a window or the door and onto the roof. Open the window early: electric windows may stop working.
- If it's moving water and you're stuck, stay on the roof and signal.

## Flood water is dirty
Flood water is mixed with sewage, farm slurry, fuel and chemicals.
- Don't drink it, and don't let children play in it. Treat any water you drink ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]).
- Cover cuts with waterproof plasters. Wash hands well before eating ([[a:water-hygiene]]).
- **Leptospirosis** (Weil's disease) spreads in water contaminated by rat urine. Flu-like illness 1–2 weeks after contact needs a doctor: say you were in flood water.

## UK flood warnings
The Environment Agency (England), SEPA (Scotland) and Natural Resources Wales use three levels:
- **Flood alert:** flooding is possible. Be prepared.
- **Flood warning:** flooding is expected. Act now.
- **Severe flood warning:** danger to life. Follow the emergency services.

If you hear these on the radio or from others, move to high ground early.

## After the water drops
- Keep out of flooded areas until told it's safe: there may be more rain to come, and structures may be weak.
- Watch for washed-out paths, undercut banks and landslips on slopes.
- Wash yourself and your kit with clean water.

=== flood-home
title: Flooding at home
summary: Protect people first, then property: flood kit, upstairs, utilities off, flood barriers, and cleaning up safely afterwards.
tags: flood, flooding, house, home, sandbags, flood boards, flood kit, flood plan, electricity, gas, clean up, insurance, EA, SEPA
icon: house-plug
level: moderate
needs: "torch", "battery radio", "rubber gloves", "wellies", first-aid-kit
related: flood-outdoors, grab-bag, grid-down-first, cold-house
---
!quick
1. **People and pets first:** move everyone **upstairs** with a flood kit, warm clothes, food, water and medicines.
2. **Switch off gas, electricity and water** at the mains, **only if safe**. Never touch switches or sockets while standing in water.
3. **Block water** with flood boards, airbrick covers and sandbags; move valuables and documents upstairs.
4. **Don't walk or drive through flood water.** Leave when the emergency services tell you to.
!end

## When a flood warning is issued
The Environment Agency, SEPA and Natural Resources Wales issue **flood alerts**, **flood warnings** and **severe flood warnings** (danger to life). Act on the alert, not when water reaches the door.

## Flood kit
!checklist Keep in one waterproof bag or box
- Torch with spare batteries, or a wind-up torch
- Battery or wind-up radio
- Warm clothes, waterproofs and wellies
- Rubber gloves
- First-aid kit and a week of prescription medicines
- Bottled water and food that needs no cooking
- Important documents (insurance, ID, bank details) in a sealed plastic bag
- Phone charger and power bank
- Baby and pet supplies
- Cash
!end

See [[a:grab-bag]] for a kit you can leave home with.

## Protecting the house
- **Flood boards** across doors, **airbrick covers**, and non-return valves on drains, if you have them.
- **Sandbags:** half-fill them (about two-thirds) with sand or soil, lay them like bricks with joins overlapping, and tread each one down. Lay a plastic sheet under the front row facing the water. No sandbags? Use pillowcases, bin bags or compost bags filled with soil.
- **Plug sinks, baths and showers** and weigh the plugs down. Put a bag of soil in the toilet bowl: sewage can come up through drains.
- **Move valuables, electrics, photos and documents** upstairs or high up. Roll up rugs. Raise furniture on bricks if you can't move it.
- **Move the car** to higher ground before the water comes.
- **Turn off** the gas, electricity and water at the mains if it is safe to reach them. Know where they are **before** a flood.

## During the flood
- **Stay upstairs.** If water keeps rising, be ready to signal from a window or the roof with a torch, a bright sheet or by shouting.
- **Never walk through flood water.** Lifted manhole covers, drains and hidden debris are deadly, and the water is full of sewage.
- Don't try to drive out through water: 30 cm of flowing water can float a car ([[a:flood-outdoors]]).
- **Evacuate** when the emergency services tell you to. Take the flood kit, lock up, and leave by the safest route.
- Don't use gas or electric appliances that have been in water.

## After the flood
!warn Before you switch anything back on
Have the **electrics checked by a qualified electrician** and **gas appliances checked by a Gas Safe registered engineer** before using them. Don't use anything that has been under water.
!end

- **Health:** wear rubber gloves, wellies and a face mask when cleaning. Wash hands often, especially before eating. Cover cuts. Keep children and pets away from mud and water.
- **Food:** throw away any food that touched flood water, including tins that are dented, and anything from a fridge or freezer that lost power.
- **Water:** if the water company or council says the supply is unsafe, boil it ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]). Private wells and springs must be tested before drinking.
- **Never** run a **generator, barbecue or patio heater indoors** or in a garage to dry out or keep warm: carbon monoxide kills ([[a:house-fire]]).
- **Clean:** shovel out mud, wash hard surfaces with hot soapy water, then disinfect. Dry out with heating and ventilation or dehumidifiers. It can take weeks or months.
- **Insurance:** photograph everything and the water line before you clean, and keep damaged items until the assessor has seen them.
- **Mental health:** floods are stressful and the clean-up is long. Talk to people, accept help, and check on neighbours.

=== severe-weather-now
title: Severe weather now
summary: Quick actions for thunderstorms, gales, snow and whiteout, extreme heat and heavy rain, with links to the full advice.
tags: storm, thunderstorm, lightning, gale, wind, snow, blizzard, whiteout, heat, heatwave, rain, flood, severe weather, weather warning
icon: cloud-alert
related: lightning-safety, storm-survival, wind-chill, heatwave
---
!quick
1. **Decide now:** stop, shelter or turn back. Don't push on into worse weather.
2. **Get off high and exposed ground** in thunder or gales; **get to high ground** in floods.
3. **Shelter early**, while you can still use your hands and think clearly.
4. Pick your situation below.
!end

## Thunderstorm
- Thunder within **30 seconds** of the flash: you're in range. **Get off summits, ridges and open ground**, away from lone trees, fences, metal and water.
- Caught in the open: **crouch on your rucksack or mat**, feet together, head down. Don't lie flat.
- **Spread a group out**, at least 5–10 m apart.
- Wait **30 minutes** after the last thunder.
- Full advice: [[a:lightning-safety]].

## Gale
- Get **off ridges, summits and cliff edges**: gusts knock people over. **Crawl** or crouch if you're being blown about.
- **Drop to lower, sheltered ground** on the side away from the wind.
- Keep **away from trees and woods** (falling branches) and from the coast and sea walls (waves).
- See [[a:storm-survival]] and [[a:wind-chill]].

## Snow and whiteout
- **Shelter early**: behind a wall, boulder, bank or in a dug snow hollow ([[a:wind-snow-shelter]]).
- In a whiteout, **stop**. Cornices and drops are invisible. Only move on a compass bearing and with pacing ([[a:walking-on-bearing]]).
- Don't push on to "make it down". Stay together, insulate from the ground, and stay put until it clears.
- In a car: [[a:car-snow]].

## Extreme heat
- **Rest in shade** from **11:00 to 15:00**. Walk early and late.
- **Drink** regularly; cover your head and neck.
- Watch for headache, dizziness, confusion: cool them at once ([[a:heat-illness]]).
- See [[a:heatwave]].

## Heavy rain and flood
- **Get to high ground** away from streams. Flash floods rise in minutes in steep valleys.
- **Never walk or drive through flood water.** 15 cm of moving water can knock you over.
- Don't cross swollen streams: wait ([[a:river-crossing]]).
- See [[a:flood-outdoors]].

## Cold and wet (most days in the hills)
- Waterproofs on **before** you get wet; hat and gloves on; eat and drink.
- Shivering and clumsiness are the warning: stop and get warm ([[a:hypothermia]]).

=== storm-survival
title: Storms and gales
summary: What UK storms do, how to stay safe from falling trees, flying debris and waves outdoors and at home, and what to do after.
tags: storm, gale, wind, high winds, named storm, hurricane, weather warning, fallen trees, power cut, power lines, Beaufort scale, debris
icon: tornado
level: moderate
related: severe-weather-now, wind-chill, mountain-weather, grid-down-first
---
!quick
1. **Get indoors or to sheltered low ground** before the worst of the wind. Don't travel unless you must.
2. **Keep away from trees, woods, cliff edges, sea fronts and loose roofs** in strong winds.
3. On hills: get **off ridges and summits**, crouch or **crawl** in gusts, and descend on the sheltered side.
4. After the storm: **stay clear of fallen power lines** (assume they're live) and damaged trees.
!end

## UK storms
Most of Britain's severe gales come from deep Atlantic lows between **October and March**. The Met Office, with Irish and Dutch forecasters, **names storms** likely to cause amber or red warnings. Gusts of **110–160 km/h (70–100 mph)** hit exposed coasts and hills in big storms; **Storm Eunice** (2022) gusted to 196 km/h (122 mph) on the Isle of Wight, England's highest on record.

**Warnings:** yellow (be aware), amber (be prepared: disruption, risk to life possible), red (take action: dangerous, risk to life likely).

Most deaths in UK storms come from **falling trees and branches**, people on **sea fronts swept by waves**, vehicles hit by debris or blown over, and people trying to repair roofs during the storm. Long **power cuts** follow: Storm Arwen (2021) left some homes without power for over a week.

## How strong is the wind?
| Wind (Beaufort) | Speed | What you notice |
|---|---|---|
| 6 Strong breeze | 39–49 km/h (25–31 mph) | Large branches moving; walking feels tiring on hills |
| 7 Near gale | 50–61 km/h (32–38 mph) | Whole trees moving; hard to walk against the wind |
| 8 Gale | 62–74 km/h (39–46 mph) | Twigs break off; walking difficult; balance affected on hills |
| 9 Severe gale | 75–88 km/h (47–54 mph) | Slates and chimney pots blow off; people blown over in gusts |
| 10 Storm | 89–102 km/h (55–63 mph) | Trees uprooted; serious damage. Standing on hills is impossible |
| 11–12 Violent storm, hurricane force | 103 km/h+ (64 mph+) | Widespread damage |

Hill summits often have **twice the wind** of the valley ([[a:mountain-weather]]). Wind plus rain chills you fast ([[a:wind-chill]]).

## Outdoors
- **Trees:** keep out of woods in a gale, and don't shelter under single trees. Branches fall without warning, and trees with full summer leaf or waterlogged roots topple. Don't pitch a tent under trees: check overhead for dead limbs ("widowmakers").
- **Hills:** get off ridges, summits and cliff edges. Turn back early; descend on the lee side. In gusts, **crouch low** with your poles or hands on the ground, or **crawl**. Groups hold onto each other.
- **Coast:** keep off sea fronts, piers, harbour walls and cliff tops. Big waves come in sets and wash people off promenades and rocks. Don't go to watch the waves.
- **Camp:** pitch in the lee of a wall, bank or hill, door away from the wind; add guylines and weigh pegs with rocks. If your shelter fails at night, pack it down and get into a survival bag behind a wall ([[a:emergency-night-out]]).
- **On the road:** high-sided vehicles, caravans, cyclists and motorbikes get blown over, especially on bridges and open stretches. Watch for fallen trees round bends.

## At home
- **Before:** tie down or bring in bins, garden furniture, trampolines and anything loose. Close and latch windows, doors and gates. Park away from trees and walls.
- **During:** stay indoors, away from windows on the windward side. **Don't go outside to fix a roof or fence.**
- **Power cuts:** have torches, spare batteries and a battery radio ready; never use candles near curtains, or generators and barbecues indoors ([[a:grid-down-first]], [[a:cold-house]]).

## After the storm
- **Fallen power lines:** stay at least **10 m** away from any wire on the ground, and from anything it touches (fences, puddles, cars). Keep others back and report it when you reach a phone.
- **Damaged trees:** hanging branches and partly-fallen trees can drop days later. Keep out of storm-damaged woods until they've been made safe.
- **Buildings:** watch for loose slates and chimneys before you walk under them.
- Check on neighbours, especially older people and anyone without power.

=== heatwave
title: Heatwave
summary: UKHSA heat-health advice: keep your home cool, avoid the midday sun, drink, and check on people most at risk.
tags: heatwave, heat, hot weather, high temperature, heat health alert, sun, hot house, overheating, dehydration, sunburn, car, UKHSA
icon: fan
related: heat-illness, heat-and-sun, water-needs, cold-water-immersion
---
!quick
1. **Stay out of the heat 11:00–15:00.** Shade, indoors, or rest. Do anything hard early or late.
2. **Keep your home cool:** curtains and blinds closed on sunny windows by day; windows open when it's cooler outside, especially at night.
3. **Drink water regularly**, before you feel thirsty. Limit alcohol.
4. **Check on others** at least once a day: older people, babies, the ill and those who live alone.
!end

## Why it matters
British homes and bodies aren't built for heat. The UK hit **40.3 °C** in July 2022, and UKHSA estimated nearly **3,000** heat-related deaths in England that summer, mostly older people in their own homes. Heat-health alerts (yellow, amber, red) are issued by **UKHSA with the Met Office**.

## Who is most at risk
- People over 65, babies and young children.
- Anyone with heart, lung or kidney disease, diabetes, dementia, Parkinson's or serious mental illness, and people on certain medicines (some for blood pressure, mental health and water tablets: don't stop them, but drink well).
- People who live alone, can't get about, or work or exercise outdoors.

## Keep your home cool
- **Close curtains and blinds** on windows that get the sun. Light-coloured ones reflect more.
- **Open windows** when the air outside is cooler than inside, usually evening, night and early morning. Shut them when it's hotter outside. Make a through draught if you can safely.
- Turn off lights and electrical things you're not using: they make heat.
- Find the **coolest room** (often north-facing or downstairs) and spend the hot part of the day there. Sleep there too.
- Fans help when the air is below body temperature. Above about 35 °C, a fan alone can blow hot air: wet your skin as well.
- Cool yourself: cool (not cold) showers, damp cloths on the neck and wrists, feet in cool water.

## Drink and eat
- Drink regularly through the day. Pale straw-coloured urine means you're drinking enough ([[a:dehydration-signs]]).
- Eat cold, light meals with fruit and salad. Salty snacks help if you're sweating a lot.
- Alcohol, and lots of strong tea and coffee, make dehydration worse.

## Heat illness
**Heat exhaustion** (headache, dizziness, heavy sweating, cramps, nausea, intense thirst) needs cooling, rest and fluids now. **Heatstroke** (hot skin, confusion, fitting, collapse, sweating may stop) is an emergency: cool them hard and **send for help**. Full guide: [[a:heat-illness]].

## Outdoors
- **Shade and timing:** walk early and late; rest in shade through the middle of the day.
- **Water:** in heat, walking can take **0.5–1 litre an hour** or more. Work out your needs with [[t:calc]] and [[a:water-needs]]. Carry more than you think; streams dry up in drought.
- **Sun:** hat with a brim, long loose clothes, sunglasses, sunscreen SPF 30+ with 4 or 5 stars, reapplied every 2 hours. UV is high even when it's hazy ([[a:heat-and-sun]]). Sunburn stops you sweating properly.
- **Swimming:** rivers, lakes and quarries stay cold below the surface even in a heatwave. Cold water shock drowns strong swimmers: enter slowly and [[a:cold-water-immersion|know how to float]].
- **Wildfire risk** is high in hot dry spells: no fires or barbecues on moorland, heath or in woods ([[a:wildfire]]).

!danger Never leave anyone in a parked car
A closed car in sunshine becomes dangerously hot within minutes, even with a window open a little. Never leave a child, a vulnerable adult or a pet in a parked vehicle. If you see one in distress, act and get help.
!end

## Night time
Hot nights are the hardest on the body. Sleep in the coolest room, use a sheet only, keep a cool damp cloth handy, and drink water by the bed.

- Met Office: Heatwave; Heat-health alerts; UK temperature records (metoffice.gov.uk)
- NHS: Heatwave: how to cope in hot weather; Heat exhaustion and heatstroke (nhs.uk)
- RNLI: Cold water shock (rnli.org)

=== wildfire
title: Wildfire (moorland and forest fire)
summary: What to do if you see or are caught near a moorland, heath or forest fire, and how to avoid starting one.

!quick
1. **Move away early**, **across the slope or to the side** of the fire and **upwind** of it. **Never uphill** above a fire: it runs uphill fast.
2. Head for **bare ground**: a road, wide track, ploughed field, water, rock, or ground that has **already burnt** and cooled.
3. **Smoke:** keep low, cover your nose and mouth with a cloth, and get out of it.
4. **Report it** when you reach a phone or a person: where, how big, which way it's moving.
!end

## UK wildfires
Wildfires in Britain mostly burn **moorland, heathland, grassland and forest**, especially in the Peak District, Pennines, Scottish Highlands, south Wales valleys, and southern heaths. The main season is **spring (March–May)**, when last year's dead grass, bracken and heather are dry and new growth hasn't come through, and again in **summer droughts** like 2018 and 2022. Most are started by people: barbecues, campfires, cigarettes, discarded glass, and deliberate burning.

Gorse and heather fires can spread **faster than you can run**, especially in wind and on slopes. **Peat** can burn underground for weeks, with hidden hot spots that collapse under your feet.

## How a wildfire moves
- **Wind** drives the fire front and throws embers ahead of it, starting new fires (spotting) tens of metres or more downwind.
- **Slope:** fire runs **uphill** much faster than on the flat, because flames lean into the fuel above. Gullies and narrow valleys act like chimneys.
- **Fuel:** dry heather, gorse, bracken, long grass and conifer plantations burn hottest. Short grazed grass, bare earth, rock, wet ground and green deciduous woods burn less.

## If you see a wildfire
1. **Don't approach it** to get a look or a photo. Smoke and wind shifts trap people.
2. Note **where it is** (grid reference or landmarks: [[a:grid-references]]), its size, and which way the smoke is blowing.
3. **Move away** along paths and tracks, ideally **upwind** and **downhill or across the slope**, never up a gully or a slope above the fire.
4. **Report it** as soon as you reach a phone or a person: they should ask for the **Fire service** ([[a:when-you-reach-help]]).
5. Only tackle a fire if it's **tiny** (a dropped barbecue or a campfire spreading a metre or two) and you can easily put it out with water or by smothering. Otherwise leave.

## If the fire is near you
- **Get to the side.** A fire front pushed by the wind is narrowest at its flanks. Move at right angles to its direction of travel, then work round behind it.
- **Look for a refuge:** a road, wide forest track, river or loch shore, rocky area, ploughed field, car park, or **burnt ground that has stopped smoking**. Short grass is better than heather or bracken.
- **Avoid** gullies, saddles, steep slopes above the fire, dense plantations and dead-end valleys.
- **Protect yourself:** cover bare skin with wool or cotton (synthetic fabrics melt onto skin). Keep a cloth over your nose and mouth: wet it if you have water.
- **Last resort**, with no escape: get into the lowest bare spot you can find (a ditch, a stream, behind a rock), lie face down with your feet towards the fire, and cover yourself with anything non-flammable or with earth.
- **In a car:** don't drive into smoke you can't see through. Turn round early. If trapped, park on bare ground away from vegetation, engine on, windows and vents shut, and get down below the windows under a wool blanket or coat until the front has passed.

## Smoke
Smoke kills and disables more people than flames. It's hot, full of carbon monoxide and fine particles, and blinds you.
- Get **out of the smoke** and stay **low**.
- People with asthma, heart or lung disease should leave smoky areas early and keep their inhalers or medicines handy.
- After smoke exposure: watch for a cough, breathlessness, headache or confusion. Get help if they don't clear in fresh air ([[a:burns]] for burns to the face or airway).

## Don't start one
- No fires or **disposable barbecues** on moorland, heath, peat or in woods. Many land managers and national parks ban them outright, and Public Spaces Protection Orders apply in some areas.
- Take all glass and litter home. Put cigarettes out properly.
- If you need a fire to survive, follow [[a:fire-safety]]: a mineral soil or stone base, cleared ground, water to hand, never on peat.

!quick
1. **Fire indoors:** **get out, stay out**. Don't stop for belongings. Close doors behind you.
2. **Smoke:** get **low** and **out of it**. Move **upwind** outdoors.
3. **Flood:** get **high** and **stay out of the water**.
4. Once you're safe, get help: shout, knock on doors, **send someone**, or report it when you reach a phone ([[a:when-you-reach-help]]).

## House or building fire
- **Shout "Fire!"** and wake everyone. Don't investigate.
- **Get out by the safest route**, closing doors behind you to slow the fire.
- **Feel doors with the back of your hand** before opening. Warm? Don't open it: use another way.
- **Smoke:** crawl on hands and knees, where the air is cleaner.
- **Never go back in**, for anyone or anything. Tell firefighters who's missing.
- **Can't get out?** Get into a room with a window, shut the door, block the gap with bedding, open the window and shout for help.
- Clothes on fire: **stop, drop and roll**.
- Full advice: [[a:house-fire]].

## Smoke outdoors
- Move **upwind** and to the side of the smoke, towards open, bare ground.
- Stay **low**; cover your nose and mouth with a cloth.
- Thick smoke from a big industrial or chemical fire: **go in, stay in, tune in**. Get indoors, shut windows and doors, and listen to local radio ([[a:go-in-stay-in]]).

## Wildfire
- Move **across the slope and away**, ideally **upwind**. **Never uphill** above the fire.
- Head for roads, tracks, water, rock, or ground that has already burnt.
- Full advice: [[a:wildfire]].

## Rising flood water
- **Get to high ground**, or **upstairs** at home.
- **Don't walk or drive through it**: 15 cm of moving water knocks you over; 30 cm moves a car.
- **Switch off** gas and electricity at the mains only if you can do it without touching water.
- Signal from a window or roof if trapped.
- Full advice: [[a:flood-outdoors]], [[a:flood-home]].

## Afterwards
- Burns: cool under cool running water for **20 minutes** ([[a:burns]]).
- Anyone who breathed smoke, or was in the water, needs checking by a doctor, even if they seem fine.
- Keep warm and dry ([[a:hypothermia]]).

=== house-fire

## Smoke is the killer
Most people who die in house fires are killed by **smoke**, not flames, often while asleep. A few breaths of thick smoke can knock you out. Smoke rises, so the cleanest air is near the floor.

## Before a fire: smoke alarms and a plan
- **Smoke alarms** on every floor, in the hallway and on landings; a **heat alarm** in the kitchen. **Test them monthly** by pressing the button.
- **Carbon monoxide alarm** in every room with a gas, oil, coal or wood burner.
- **Escape plan:** the best route is usually the normal way in and out. Pick a second route. Keep both clear.
- Keep **door and window keys** where everyone can find them in the dark, near the door.
- **Close internal doors at night.** A closed door can hold back fire and smoke for many minutes.
- Know how to get anyone who needs help (children, older relatives, pets) out.

## If there's a fire
1. **Shout "Fire!"** and wake everyone. Don't stop to investigate or pick up belongings.
2. **Check doors before opening:** touch the door and the handle with the **back of your hand**. If warm, don't open it: the fire is behind. Use another route.
3. **Through smoke:** get down and **crawl**, keeping your face near the floor.
4. **Close doors** behind you as you go.
5. Once out, **stay out** and meet at a set point. Get the fire service called by a neighbour or anyone with a phone ([[a:when-you-reach-help]]).
6. **Never go back in**, even for a person or a pet. Tell firefighters who is inside and where.

## If you're trapped
- Get everyone into **one room**, ideally with a **window**, and **close the door**.
- **Seal the gaps** at the bottom of the door with towels, bedding or clothes. Wet them if you can.
- **Open the window** and shout **"Help, fire!"**. Wave a sheet or bright cloth; use a torch at night.
- If the room fills with smoke, keep low near the open window.
- **Ground or first floor only, and only as a last resort:** drop bedding or cushions below to land on. Get out feet first, lower yourself to arm's length from the window sill, then drop. Don't jump from higher floors.

## Flats
In many purpose-built blocks the advice is to **stay put** in your flat unless fire or smoke reaches it, because each flat is built to contain a fire. **Follow your building's fire plan.** If the fire is in your flat, or smoke reaches you, get out, close the door, and use the stairs, never the lift.

## Clothes on fire: stop, drop, roll
**Stop** where you are (running fans the flames), **drop** to the ground, cover your face with your hands, and **roll** over and over to smother the flames. Smother someone else's with a heavy coat, blanket or rug. Then cool the burn.

## Burns
Cool under cool running water for **20 minutes**, remove jewellery and clothing near the burn (unless stuck), then cover loosely with cling film or a clean plastic bag. Full guide: [[a:burns]].

## Kitchen fires
- **Chip pan or oil fire:** **never use water**: it explodes into a fireball. Turn off the heat only if it's safe to reach, don't move the pan, and get out, closing the door. A **fire blanket** works if you know how to use it.
- **Electrical fire:** never use water. Switch off at the mains if safe, and get out.

## Carbon monoxide
Carbon monoxide (CO) has no smell, colour or taste. It comes from **anything that burns** with poor ventilation.
- **Never** use a **generator, barbecue, patio heater, camping stove or charcoal** indoors, in a garage, a tent or a caravan, especially in a power cut.
- **Signs:** headache, dizziness, nausea, tiredness, confusion, breathlessness. Symptoms often ease when you go outside, and pets or several people in the house may be ill at once.
- **What to do:** open doors and windows, switch off appliances if you can, **get everyone outside** into fresh air, and get medical help. Don't go back in until the source is fixed.
- Have gas appliances serviced every year by a **Gas Safe** registered engineer, and chimneys swept at least yearly.

# Fire. Your kit: 1/2" x 6" ferro rod, flint & steel (high-carbon striker + found flint + char cloth), glass magnifier, rechargeable arc lighter.

=== ferro-rod
title: Ferro rod technique
summary: Throw a shower of 3,000 °C sparks into fine tinder by pulling the rod back, not by pushing the striker forward.
tags: ferro rod, ferrocerium, firesteel, sparks, striker, tinder
wiki: Ferrocerium
---
!quick
1. Prepare **everything first**: tinder bundle, a pile of pencil-lead-thin kindling, then thumb-thick, then wrist-thick sticks.
2. Put the rod tip **into or touching** the tinder. Hold the striker (or knife spine) still at about **45°**.
3. **Pull the rod back** firmly and steadily along its length while the striker stays still, so sparks land in the tinder without scattering it.
4. Once it catches, cup it, blow gently from below, and feed it kindling.
!end

## Technique
- **New rods** have a black protective coating: scrape it off with a few firm strokes first.
- Use the **full length** of the rod and press hard: firm pressure and a slow-to-medium stroke give fat, long-burning sparks. Fast, light strokes give a few weak sparks.
- Strike **close** to the tinder (within 2–3 cm). Sparks cool quickly.
- Scrape a little **powder** onto the tinder first (a few gentle scrapes), then throw a big spark into it. The powder flares instantly.
- In wind, kneel with your back to the wind and shelter the tinder with your body.

## What catches a ferro rod spark
Excellent: cotton wool (with a smear of petroleum jelly), [[s:silver-birch]] bark scrapings, fatwood shavings ([[s:scots-pine]]), [[s:reedmace]] down, char cloth, a dry [[s:cramp-balls]] crumb, and fine dry grass. See [[a:natural-tinders]].

## Care
- About 12,000 strikes from a ½″ × 6″ rod. It wears faster with heavy strokes, so use the whole length evenly.
- Ferrocerium **corrodes** in damp storage (a grey crust forms): keep it dry; a quick scrape restores it.
- Spark temperature is around 3,000 °C: never strike towards eyes or close to fuel stoves.

=== flint-and-steel
title: Flint and steel
summary: Strike a high-carbon steel against a sharp flint edge to make sparks that land on char cloth, then blow the ember into flame.
tags: flint, steel, striker, char cloth, ember, traditional fire, sparks, chert, quartz
wiki: Fire striker
---
!quick
1. Hold a **sharp-edged flint** in your non-dominant hand with a piece of **char cloth** pinched on top, just behind the edge.
2. Strike the **steel** down glancingly against the flint edge, shaving off tiny steel particles: these are the sparks.
3. A spark lands on the char cloth and makes a **glowing dot**. Blow on it gently to spread it.
4. Fold the glowing char cloth into a **tinder bundle** and blow it into flame.
!end

## What you need
- **Steel:** your high-carbon, hardened striker. Stainless steel and soft iron don't spark well.
- **Stone:** a hard stone with a **sharp edge**: flint, chert, quartz or quartzite. Flint is common in the **chalk country** of southern and eastern England: ploughed fields, beaches, and river gravels. Elsewhere look for white quartz pebbles.
- **Char material:** [[a:char-cloth]], charred punk wood, [[s:tinder-fungus]] (amadou), or [[s:cramp-balls]]. Steel sparks are **much cooler** than ferro rod sparks; they will not light birch bark or cotton wool directly.
- **Tinder bundle:** a nest of fine dry grass, shredded [[s:honeysuckle|honeysuckle]] or clematis bark, or dry bracken. See [[a:natural-tinders]].

## Technique
- Grip the flint so its edge sticks out slightly, with the char cloth on top of it, held under your thumb and close to the edge.
- Swing the steel down and slightly away from you, hitting the edge at a shallow angle. It's a scraping strike, not a hammer blow. Keep your knuckles clear!
- When the edge dulls (no sparks), knock off a flake to make a fresh sharp edge.
- Blow the ember in the bundle: hold it up, blow long and gentle, increasing as it smokes heavily, until it bursts into flame.

## Care
- Keep the striker dry and lightly oiled (any cooking oil or fat) to stop rust. Rust stops it sparking: clean it with sand.
- Store char cloth in an airtight tin: it absorbs moisture.

=== char-cloth
title: Making char cloth in a tin
summary: Turn 100% cotton into char cloth that catches the faintest spark, using a tin with a small hole in the lid.
tags: char cloth, charcloth, cotton, tin, charring, flint and steel, ember
wiki: Char cloth
---
!quick
1. Cut **100% cotton or linen** into squares (5 cm) and pack them loosely into a **metal tin** with a lid.
2. Make **one small hole** (nail-sized) in the lid.
3. Put the tin **in hot embers**. Smoke (and sometimes a small flame) jets from the hole.
4. When the smoke **stops**, take it out, **plug the hole** (a twig or soil), and let it **cool completely** before opening.
!end

## Getting it right
- Good char cloth is **evenly black**, flexible, and doesn't crumble. Brown means under-cooked (cook longer); grey ash means air got in (seal better).
- Opening the tin while hot lets air in and the cloth **bursts into ember**: wait.
- Use cotton you can spare. Keep your straining cloth for water. Old cotton T-shirts, jeans, flannel and cotton wool pads all work.
- Natural alternatives to char in the same tin: rotten "punk" wood, dry [[s:cramp-balls]] slices, [[s:reedmace]] heads, and amadou from [[s:tinder-fungus]].

## Using it
- Catches a spark from flint and steel, a ferro rod, or the focus of a magnifier ([[a:magnifier-fire]]).
- An ember on char cloth smoulders slowly. Place it inside a tinder bundle and blow.
- Store it in the airtight tin, kept dry. A small square gives one ember.

## Sources
- Traditional tinderbox methods (English Heritage, Museum of London learning resources)
- Bushcraft UK / Woodlore techniques

=== magnifier-fire
title: Sun fire with a magnifier
summary: Focus sunlight on char cloth or dark tinder. Possible on clear UK days from spring to autumn around midday.
tags: magnifier, magnifying glass, lens, burning glass, sun, solar fire
wiki: Burning glass
---
!quick
1. Needs **direct, bright sun**, usually 10:00–15:00 from March to September. Haze and cloud stop it.
2. Hold the lens square to the sun; move it until the bright spot is **as small as possible** on **dark** tinder (char cloth, charred wood, a [[s:cramp-balls|cramp ball]]).
3. Hold **perfectly still**. Brace your hands on your knees or a stick. Smoke within seconds to a minute.
4. Transfer the glowing ember into a tinder bundle and blow it to flame.
!end

## Tips
- **Dark absorbs, light reflects.** Blacken pale tinder with charcoal first. Char cloth works best of all.
- Bigger lenses work faster. Your magnifier is for an ember, not flame directly.
- No fuel or batteries are used: save the arc lighter and ferro rod for when the sun isn't out.

## Safety
- **Never look at the sun through the lens**, or at the focus spot for long. Wear sunglasses if you have them.
- Never leave a lens lying in the sun: it can start a fire in dry vegetation or melt kit. Keep it in its case.

## Care
- Glass scratches: keep it wrapped in cloth. Clean it with a soft cloth and clean water.

=== arc-lighter
title: Arc lighter: use and care
summary: Your rechargeable plasma arc lighter: windproof and fuel-free, but battery-dependent and sensitive to cold and wet.
tags: arc lighter, plasma lighter, USB, rechargeable, battery, electric lighter
---
!quick
1. Prepare tinder and kindling **first**. The arc is small; touch it to the **edge** of birch bark, a feather stick's curls, paper or a candle wick.
2. Hold it for 2–5 seconds at a time; let it rest between goes.
3. Keep it **warm** (inside your clothing) and **dry**. Charge it from the solar panel whenever the sun is out.
4. Lock it when packed. Don't touch the electrodes or hold it near fuel or gas.
!end

## Strengths and limits
- Windproof, no fuel, thousands of lights per lifetime. It works in rain if the head is shielded.
- **Battery:** lithium cells lose capacity in cold. Below about 0 °C it may fail until warmed in a pocket. Charge it at least monthly, ideally when the sun is out, using the panel ([[a:solar-panel]]).
- It lights **solid** tinder well (birch bark curls, feather stick curls, fatwood shavings, paper, cotton wool). It is poor at lighting loose fluffy material, which blows away in the arc.

## Care
- Clean the electrodes gently with a dry cotton bud. Ash and grit can short the arc.
- If it gets wet: dry it thoroughly (open the charging flap) before charging or using it.
- Don't leave it fully discharged for long. Store it around half charged if not used for months. See [[a:batteries-electronics]].
- Keep the charging port clean and the cap closed.

=== natural-tinders
title: UK natural tinders
summary: Where to find birch bark, cramp balls, dead bracken, clematis and honeysuckle bark, thistle and reedmace down, and fatwood.
tags: tinder, birch bark, cramp balls, King Alfred's cakes, bracken, clematis, honeysuckle, thistle down, reedmace, fatwood, pine
wiki: Fatwood
---
!quick
1. **Birch bark:** oily, burns even when damp. Peel thin curls from dead or fallen birch.
2. **Fatwood:** resin-soaked Scots pine stumps and branch bases. Shave it to fine scrapings.
3. **Fine fibres:** shredded dead **clematis** (old man's beard) or **honeysuckle** bark, dry **bracken**, dry grass, made into a bird's nest.
4. **Ember extenders:** dry **cramp balls** (King Alfred's cakes), amadou, char cloth.
!end

## The tinders
| Tinder | Where | Use |
|---|---|---|
| [[s:silver-birch]] bark | Birch woods, heaths, everywhere | Ferro rod, arc lighter. Curls burn hot with black smoke. |
| Fatwood ([[s:scots-pine]]) | Old pine stumps, knots and branch bases; smells of turpentine | Scrape shavings; lights from a ferro spark; burns in rain. |
| Dead [[s:bracken]] | Hillsides, autumn to spring | Crush to fine fibres; a good nest and kindling. |
| Old man's beard ([[s:clematis]]) bark | Hedges on chalk and limestone | Rub shredded stringy bark between your palms into a fluffy nest. |
| [[s:honeysuckle]] bark | Woodland and hedges | Peeling papery strips: shred it, a perfect nest for an ember. |
| [[s:thistle]] and [[s:reedmace]] down | Field edges; pond margins (autumn and winter) | Flash tinder: mix with heavier tinder. |
| [[s:cramp-balls]] | Dead ash branches | A dry one takes a spark and smoulders: an ember extender and carrier. |
| [[s:tinder-fungus]] (amadou) | Birch (north), beech (south) | Catches flint and steel sparks; smoulders for hours. |
| Dry grass, dead pine needles | Under trees, sheltered banks | Nest material; burns fast. |

## Collecting and drying
- Collect tinder **as you walk**, all day, and keep it in an inside pocket or bag to dry with your body heat.
- Dead material that is still attached to the plant (standing dead) is drier than anything on the ground.
- Make far more than you think you need: a fist-sized nest plus extra.
- Only take dead material, and don't strip bark from living trees.

=== feather-sticks
title: Feather sticks
summary: Carve a dry stick into a cluster of thin curls that light easily, even when the outside of the wood is wet.
tags: feather stick, carving, kindling, knife, wet wood, curls
wiki: Feather stick
---
!quick
1. Choose **dead, dry, straight-grained** wood about thumb to wrist thick: pine, birch, hazel, ash. Split wet wood to reach the **dry inner core**.
2. Brace the stick on a log or the ground. Lock your wrist and keep your arm straight. **Push the blade down the stick**, using your body weight.
3. Shave **long thin curls** and stop each one before it falls off. Rotate and repeat until a dense bunch forms at the bottom.
4. Make **two or three**. Light the curls with the ferro rod or arc lighter, and feed the fire with the rest of the stick.
!end

## Tips
- The thinner the curls, the easier they catch. Keep the blade angle shallow.
- Softwoods with resin (pine, spruce) light most easily. Birch is excellent.
- Always carve **away** from your body and legs, kneeling with the stick out to the side. See [[a:knife-safety]].
- A fine curl or two scraped to "dust" catches a ferro rod spark on its own.

## Sources
- Woodlore / Ray Mears: Feather sticks
- Scouts UK: Fire lighting and knife skills (scouts.org.uk)

=== fire-lays
title: Fire lays: teepee, log cabin, star and Dakota hole
summary: Which fire structure to build for lighting, boiling, cooking, warmth or keeping a low profile.
tags: teepee, tipi, log cabin, star fire, Dakota hole, fire lay, cooking fire
wiki: Dakota fire hole
---
!quick
1. **Teepee:** lights easily, burns hot and fast. Best for starting a fire and boiling water.
2. **Log cabin:** stable, builds a bed of coals. Best for cooking and a long fire.
3. **Star:** 4–6 logs pushed into the centre as they burn. Economical, good overnight.
4. **Dakota hole:** a fire in a hole with an air tunnel. Wind-resistant, efficient, low glow. Only where digging is allowed.
!end

## Teepee
Lean kindling around the tinder bundle in a cone, leaving a gap on the windward side to light it. Add larger sticks around it as it catches. It collapses into a good base for anything else.

## Log cabin
Two logs parallel, two more across the top, and so on, with a teepee or tinder in the centre. Burns down to an even bed of coals: ideal for a pot or for cooking on embers.

## Star
Logs radiate from the centre like spokes. Push them inwards as the ends burn. Little wood is used, and the fire is easily controlled and easy to bank ([[a:banking-fire]]).

## Dakota fire hole
- Dig a hole about 30 cm wide and 30 cm deep. Dig a second, narrower hole 20–30 cm away on the **upwind** side, and join them at the bottom with a tunnel: this is the air feed.
- Light the fire in the main hole. The draught makes it burn hot with little smoke, and the pot sits across the top.
- **Don't dig** in peat (it can smoulder underground for weeks), among roots, or without the landowner's permission. Fill in the holes and replace the turf afterwards.

## Reflector
A wall of stacked green logs or rocks behind the fire throws heat towards your shelter. See [[a:lean-to]].

## Sources
- Scouts UK: Fire lays
- Woodlore / Ray Mears: Cooking fires
- Forestry England: Campfire safety

=== wet-weather-fire
title: Lighting a fire in wet weather
summary: How to get a fire going in typical UK rain: standing dead wood, splitting to the dry core, a platform, and three times more kindling.
tags: wet weather, rain, damp wood, standing dead, splitting, platform, fire
---
!quick
1. **Find dry fuel:** standing dead branches that **snap** cleanly; split them to reach the dry core.
2. **Prepare 3×** the kindling you think you need, graded from matchstick to thumb to wrist thickness, before you strike a spark.
3. Build on a **platform** of dry sticks or bark off the wet ground, sheltered from the rain.
4. Use your **best tinder** (birch bark, fatwood, feather sticks), and light from the windward side.
!end

## Where dry wood hides
- **Standing dead** wood and dead branches still on trees: dead twigs on the lower trunks of conifers (the "witch's whisker" twigs on spruce and pine) are often dry in the worst rain.
- Wood that snaps with a crack is dry enough; if it bends, it's wet or green.
- **Under** dense evergreens (holly, yew, conifers) and overhangs.
- Inside rotten logs: dry hearts of split wood.

## Keep it going
- Feed the fire before it dies back: add small fuel steadily. Dry the next batch of wood around the fire (not on it).
- In heavy rain, rig a tarp **high** over the fire at an angle (not low: sparks melt and burn tarps).
- **Dead holly, birch and pine** burn well when damp. Ash famously burns well even green.

## Sources
- Woodlore / Ray Mears: Fire in wet conditions
- Forestry England: Fire safety guidance

=== banking-fire
title: Banking a fire overnight
summary: Keep embers alive until morning so you don't have to relight from scratch.
tags: banking, embers, overnight, coals, ash, morning fire
---
!quick
1. Before bed, rake the **embers into a tight pile**.
2. Lay one or two **thick hardwood logs** (oak, ash, beech) on or against the embers.
3. **Cover with ash**, leaving a little air. This slows the burn.
4. In the morning, rake the ash off, add tinder and small sticks, and blow.
!end

## Notes
- Hardwood coals last much longer than softwood. A star fire is easy to bank: push the ends together.
- In wind, protect the banked fire with a ring of stones or a bank of earth on the windward side.
- **Safety:** only bank a fire in a safe, clear, legal site, well away from your shelter, dry grass and peat. Never leave a fire unattended in dry, windy conditions ([[a:fire-safety]]).

## Sources
- Traditional hearth practice ("smooring" the fire, Scottish and Irish tradition)
- Forestry England: Campfire safety

=== carrying-embers
title: Carrying embers
summary: Move fire from camp to camp with a smouldering ember in a tin or a fungus, saving your ignition kit.
tags: ember, carry fire, fire horn, tinder fungus, amadou, cramp balls, punk wood
---
!quick
1. Use a **slow-burning** material: [[s:tinder-fungus]] (amadou), dry [[s:cramp-balls|cramp balls]], or rotten "punk" wood.
2. Light it from a coal and place it in a **tin with a few small holes**, packed with dry moss or more punk.
3. Check every 30–60 minutes and blow gently if it fades.
4. To relight, put it into a tinder bundle and blow it into flame.
!end

## Notes
- A whole dry hoof fungus can smoulder for many hours. Prehistoric people carried fire this way.
- Wrap the tin in a sock or green leaves to protect your hands. Keep it away from your fuel and clothing.
- Always carry your ferro rod as a backup.

## Sources

=== fire-safety
title: Fire safety and the law
summary: Where you can light a fire in the UK, how to keep it safe, carbon monoxide, and putting it out properly.
tags: fire safety, law, permission, wildfire, moorland, peat, extinguish, carbon monoxide, Leave No Trace
---
!quick
1. **Permission:** in England, Wales and Northern Ireland you need the **landowner's** permission to light a fire. In Scotland small fires are allowed responsibly under the Access Code, but **not** near woods, peat or farmland in dry weather.
2. **Site:** clear a 1–2 m circle to bare soil or stone, away from trees, overhanging branches, tents and tarps. **Never on peat or moorland.**
3. **Never leave a fire unattended.** Keep water ready.
4. **Put it out:** drown, stir, drown again, and feel for heat with the back of your hand. Leave no trace.
!end

## Wildfire risk
- Spring (March–May) is the UK's worst wildfire season: dead grass, bracken and heather are tinder-dry before new growth comes.
- **Peat** burns underground for days or weeks and destroys habitat. Never light fires on moorland or peat soils.
- Wind carries sparks a long way; don't light fires in strong winds.
- Gorse and heather fires spread faster than you can run.

## Carbon monoxide
- **Never** burn a fire, stove, BBQ or charcoal inside a tent, bothy room without ventilation, snow hole or enclosed shelter. CO is invisible and kills while you sleep.
- Symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, drowsiness. Get into fresh air.

## Leave no trace
- Use existing fire sites where they exist; use small fires; burn wood to ash.
- Scatter cold ash, replace turf, and carry out anything unburnt.

## Sources
- Scottish Outdoor Access Code (outdooraccess-scotland.scot)
- Countryside Code (gov.uk)
- Forestry England / Forestry and Land Scotland: Fire safety
- NHS: Carbon monoxide poisoning

=== firewood-uk
title: Firewood: which UK woods burn best
summary: Which dead wood to collect for heat, cooking and long burns, and what to avoid.
tags: firewood, ash, oak, birch, hazel, softwood, fuel, burn
---
!quick
1. **Best all-rounders:** ash (burns well even damp), beech, oak (long and hot once going), birch (lights easily), hazel.
2. **Fast starters:** birch, pine, spruce, dead holly, gorse. Hot and quick, but they spit.
3. **Avoid:** green wood, rotten wood, [[s:yew]] (toxic smoke), [[s:cherry-laurel]] and [[s:rhododendron]] (toxic smoke), painted or treated timber.
!end

## Notes
- Collect only **dead wood** (with the landowner's permission). Standing dead and fallen branches off the ground are best.
- Thickness matters more than species: gather plenty of each size before lighting.
- Willow and poplar burn fast with little heat; alder and elder burn poorly.
- Sweet chestnut and larch spit sparks: keep them away from your tarp.

- Woodland Trust: Tree identification
- The traditional poem "Logs to Burn" (Celia Congreve) and Forestry Commission firewood guidance

=== cooking-on-fire
title: Cooking on a fire
summary: Use embers, not flames; how to boil, roast and cook safely with one steel pot.
tags: cooking, embers, coals, pot, roast, hygiene
---
!quick
1. Cook over **embers**, not flames: let a log cabin fire burn down for 20–30 minutes.
2. Support the pot on two green logs or stones; don't balance it on burning wood.
3. Cook wild meat and fish **thoroughly** (no pink); boil wild greens.
4. Keep raw and cooked food apart; wash hands with boiled water.
!end

## Methods
- **Boiling:** the safest way to cook wild food, and it uses your one pot. Stews and soups don't waste nutrients.
- **Roasting:** on a green-wood skewer of a **non-toxic** tree (hazel, willow, birch). Never yew, laurel, rhododendron or holly.
- **In embers:** wrap fish or roots in large leaves or clay and bury them in hot coals.

## Sources
- Food Standards Agency: Cooking safely (food.gov.uk)
- Scouts UK: Backwoods cooking

=== fire-reflector
title: Using fire for warmth
summary: Get the most warmth from a small fire with a reflector wall, the right distance, and a long, steady burn.
tags: warmth, reflector, heat, lean-to, night, fire
---
!quick
1. Build a **reflector**: stacked logs or rocks behind the fire, facing your shelter.
2. A **long fire** (the length of your body) warms you evenly; keep it about **1 m** from your shelter edge.
3. Burn hardwood for steady heat; add fuel little and often.
!end

## Notes
- Keep a stack of fuel within reach so you can feed it without leaving your sleeping spot.
- Stones from rivers can **explode** when heated: avoid wet or layered stones near the fire.
- A warm (not glowing) stone wrapped in cloth warms a sleeping bag.

- Woodlore / Ray Mears: Fire and shelter
- Forestry England: Fire safety

# Self-rescue, walking out, surviving days to weeks, and relocating when lost.

=== follow-water-downhill
title: Follow water downhill
summary: Why streams lead to valleys, farms and roads in Britain, how to follow one safely, and when water leads you into danger.

!quick
1. **Walk beside the stream, not in it**, on the open slope a few metres above the bank.
2. If the stream drops into a **gorge, waterfall or crag**, climb away from it and go round on easy ground. **Never** down-climb wet rock.
3. **Don't cross** it unless you must; it grows bigger and harder to cross as you go down ([[a:river-crossing]]).
4. Stop and shelter **before dark** ([[t:sun]]). Water is not a reason to keep going at night.
!end

## Why it works in Britain
Water always takes the easiest way down. Small streams join into bigger ones, and bigger ones run into valleys. In Britain people have farmed, built roads and lived in valley bottoms for centuries, so a valley floor almost always has a track, a farm, a road or a reservoir with a dam keeper within a few kilometres.

- In England and Wales, and in most of southern and eastern Scotland, following water down will usually bring you to a lane or farm in a few hours at most.
- In the **Highlands** and some **islands**, a glen can run for many kilometres with nothing in it but a path or a bothy. It is still the right direction, but it may take a day. Look for any track, fence or power line leading off it ([[a:linear-features]]).
- Streams also give you **water to drink** on the way. Boil it if you can ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]).

## How to follow a stream safely
- **Stay on the open slope**, 5–20 m above the bank, where the ground is firmer and you can see ahead. Banks are often undercut, slippery and overgrown.
- **Look ahead and listen.** A sudden roar, a line of trees closing in, or the ground ahead disappearing all mean a drop. On a map, contour lines crowding together across a stream mean a gorge or falls ([[a:contours-terrain]]).
- **Short steps on steep grass.** Wet grass on a steep slope is one of the commonest places for walkers to slip in the UK hills. Face into the slope on the steepest bits and use a stick.
- **Take the side with the easier ground**, and stay on it. Crossing back and forth wastes energy and adds risk.
- **Tick off junctions.** Each time a side stream comes in, note it. It helps you match the map later ([[a:relocating]]) and tells rescuers your route.

## The dangers
### Gorges, waterfalls and crags
Streams in the Lake District (gills or ghylls), Snowdonia, the Pennines and the Highlands often cut deep, steep-sided ravines with waterfalls. They look like a way down and are not.
- Leave the stream **before** the sides steepen. Climb up and out to open ground, then follow the line of the gorge from above at a safe distance from the edge.
- Never go down a gully because it "looks like it goes": many end at a drop you can't see from above.
- In mist, stop if you hear falling water ahead and can't see where the ground goes.

### Bogs and wet ground
- Stream sources and flat valley floors are often bog, peat hags or wet rushes. Walk on the slightly higher, drier ground at the edge.
- **Bright green moss** (sphagnum) and **cotton grass** mean deep wet ground. Test with a stick. If you sink, lean back, spread your weight and move slowly towards firm ground.

### Limestone country
In the Yorkshire Dales, the Peak District and parts of South Wales, streams can **vanish underground** into shake holes, sinks and potholes. Don't follow water into a hole or a cave. Go round and pick up the valley below.

### Rising water
After heavy rain, streams can rise by a metre or more in an hour. Keep well above the bank, and **never camp on a flat gravel bank** or low ground beside water ([[a:site-selection]]).

### The coast and estuaries
Water may lead you to a tidal estuary with mudflats and fast tides. Stay on the landward edge; never cross sand or mud to save time ([[a:coast-and-tides]]).

## When not to follow water
- **Someone knows your route** and will raise the alarm: walking down into a different valley takes you away from where they will search ([[a:stay-or-go]]).
- **Darkness, thick fog on steep ground, or a storm.** Shelter and wait.
- **The stream leads into a gorge you can't get round**, or onto a crag-ringed slope. Go back up and find another way, such as a path or ridge.
- **Anyone is injured or very cold.** See [[a:moving-with-injury]] and [[a:hypothermia]].
- **It leads into a big, empty glen** late in the day. A known shelter or path back up may be better than a long night walk-out.

!tip Leave a trail
Before you leave a shelter or car, leave a note saying which stream you are following and when you left. Build stone arrows at junctions ([[a:be-visible]]).
!end

!quick
1. Find a **line made by people**: a path, track, wall, fence or power line. Follow it, **downhill when in doubt**.
2. **Small wooden power poles** usually lead to farms and houses. **Big pylons** cross country but have access tracks.
3. **Never walk on a railway.** Follow it from outside the fence to a station, road or crossing.
4. Listen and look for **traffic, lights, smoke, dogs and farm noise**, and note their direction with the [[t:compass]].
!end

## Why lines help
Almost every line people have made in the landscape joins up with something: a farm, a road, a village. Following one is far easier than navigating cross-country, and searchers check these lines first. It is the same idea as a navigation handrail ([[a:handrails-catching]]).

## The best lines, in order
### Paths and tracks
- A path with obvious wear, signposts, stiles or waymarks leads to a car park, road or village.
- At a junction, choose the **more worn** branch and the one going **downhill**.
- In mist, paths can split and fade. If the path disappears, go back to where it was clear.

### Forestry tracks
- Wide gravel forest roads lead to a gate and a public road, but plantations can hold many kilometres of tracks that loop and end in dead ends.
- Follow tracks that **go downhill** and carry **fresh tyre marks**. Grassed-over tracks often lead nowhere.
- Avoid straight **rides and firebreaks** between trees: they are often bog and ditches.
- Forestry operations: keep clear of machines and stacked timber, which can roll.

### Walls, fences and field boundaries
- **Drystone walls and fences** run down from the open hill to the in-bye fields around farms. Higher up they may follow a parish or estate boundary over a summit, so check you are going downhill.
- Once you reach **enclosed fields**, you are close to a farm. Head for gates; field gates usually line up along a track to the farmyard.
- **Use gates and stiles.** Don't climb drystone walls: they collapse, injure you and cost the farmer money. Climb a fence only at a strong post.
- Livestock: walk calmly round **cows with calves**; if cattle threaten you, let go of any dog and move away steadily.

### Power lines
- **Wooden poles** carrying two or three wires are the local supply. They almost always end at houses, farms or a road within a few kilometres.
- **Steel pylons** are the long-distance grid. They don't head for houses, but each pylon is reached by a maintenance track, and lines run to substations next to roads.
- **Never touch** a fallen or low wire, or anything touching it, and keep at least **10 m** away. Power can arc through wet ground.

### Roads
- Even a minor road gets traffic within an hour or two. Walk **facing oncoming traffic** on the right (unless on a blind right-hand bend), and wear or carry something bright. At night use a torch.
- Wave vehicles down where drivers have a long view and a safe place to stop ([[a:finding-help]]).

### Railways
!danger Never walk on the line
Trains are fast and almost silent until they are on you. Much of southern England has an electrified **third rail** (750 volts) and many main lines have **25,000-volt overhead wires**. Walking on a railway is also an offence.
!end
- Follow the line **from outside the fence**, on a road or path alongside it, to the next **station, level crossing or bridge**. Stations and crossings lead to roads and often have help points.

## Sounds, lights and smoke
- **Traffic noise** carries a long way in still air, especially at night and early morning. A steady hum is a main road or motorway.
- **Dogs, sheep, cattle, tractors, chainsaws, church bells** all mean people.
- **Lights at night:** take a bearing on them with the [[t:compass]] and write it down. Walk to them **in daylight** unless the ground between is easy and known. Lights look closer than they are.
- **Smoke** from a chimney or bonfire on a calm morning marks a house.
- Sound bounces off valley sides. Get a few bearings from different spots before you trust a direction.

## Using the phone
The [[t:map]] shows roads, tracks, walls and power lines around your GPS position without any signal. Check it, plan the shortest line to a road, then switch the phone off to save battery ([[a:phone-navigation]]).

!law Access
In **Scotland** you have a right of responsible access to most land, including crossing farmland. In **England and Wales** you have a right to walk on public rights of way and open access land; in an emergency, cross land by the least damaging way, use gates, and leave them as you find them.
!end

=== pacing-yourself
title: Pacing yourself on a long walk out
summary: How fast to go, when to rest, eat and drink, and how to set a turnaround time so a long walk out doesn't end in exhaustion and darkness.
tags: pace, pacing, rest, energy, exhaustion, turnaround time, daylight, Naismith, group, slowest person, sweating
icon: gauge
time: hours
level: moderate
needs: warm-layers, waterproofs, head-torch, "food and water"
related: naismith-pacing, stay-or-go, hypothermia, water-needs
---
!quick
1. Walk at a pace where you can **talk in full sentences**. The group goes at the **slowest person's** pace.
2. **Rest 5–10 minutes every hour**: sit on your pack, put a layer on, eat and drink.
3. **Vent before you sweat**: take a layer off or open zips before you overheat.
4. Set a **turnaround time** that leaves at least **1–2 hours of daylight** ([[t:sun]]). At that time, stop and shelter.
!end

## How long will it take?
Use Naismith's rule ([[a:naismith-pacing]]): **1 hour per 5 km** plus **10 minutes per 100 m of climb**. Then be honest:
- Rough ground, heather, bog or deep snow: add **50–100%**.
- Tired, cold, carrying a heavy pack, or with an injured person: **halve your speed** or worse.
- In the dark, it can take **twice as long**, and it is far more dangerous ([[a:night-navigation]]).
- Work it out in Tools → [[t:calc]], and check how much daylight is left with [[t:sun]].

## The right pace
- **Steady beats fast.** A pace you can keep for hours costs far less energy than rushing and stopping. If you are gasping or can't talk, slow down.
- **Small steps uphill**, like going up stairs one at a time. Rest-step on steep ground: lock your back leg straight for a moment between steps.
- **Short, careful steps downhill.** Most slips, sprains and falls happen on the way down, late in the day, when people are tired and hurrying.
- **Don't stop for long on a cold day.** Short regular rests are better than one long one, because you cool fast.

## Rest, eat and drink
- **Every hour:** stop for 5–10 minutes. Put a warm layer on as you stop, take it off before you start.
- **Eat little and often:** a handful of food every hour keeps your energy steady. Sugary food works fast; fat and protein last longer. Don't save all your food for later and collapse on the way.
- **Drink regularly**, even in cold weather. Aim for pale urine. Wild water: boil if you can ([[a:settle-strain-boil]], [[a:water-needs]]).
- **Check each other** at each stop: feet (hot spots become blisters, [[a:blisters]]), cold hands, mood, and whether anyone has gone quiet.

## Sweat is the enemy in the cold
- Wet clothes lose much of their warmth. Sweat that soaks your base layer chills you as soon as you stop.
- **Be bold, start cold:** set off feeling a little cool. You warm up within 10 minutes.
- Open zips and take your hat off on climbs; put them back on at the top, before you feel cold.
- **Exhaustion and cold together are dangerous.** Running out of energy means your body can't make heat. Stumbling, slowness, silence and irritability are early signs of [[a:hypothermia]]: stop, shelter, eat, and warm them.

## Walking as a group
- **The slowest person sets the pace.** Put them near the front, behind the leader, where they don't feel left behind.
- Keep the group **in sight of each other**, and stop at every junction until all are together.
- Share the load: take weight from the weakest walker.
- Never let anyone walk ahead alone or drop behind alone.

## Turnaround time
Decide before you start: "**At [time], wherever we are, we stop and make shelter**" (or turn back).
- Set it at least **1–2 hours before sunset**. In a UK winter that may be as early as 14:00; the sun can set before 16:00 in December, and earlier in the north.
- Making a shelter, finding water and gathering wood takes 1–2 hours. Do it in daylight ([[a:emergency-night-out]]).
- Keep to it. "Just another half hour" is how people end up on steep ground in the dark.

!tip Signs to stop early
Someone is stumbling, shivering, very quiet or confused; the weather is worsening; you are behind your time plan by more than an hour; or you no longer know exactly where you are ([[a:relocating]]).
!end

=== moving-with-injury
title: Moving with an injury
summary: Decide whether an injured person can walk out, how to strap ankles and knees, improvise crutches and poles, and when to stop and shelter instead.

icon: person-standing
time: 15–30 min to prepare
level: hard
needs: first-aid-kit, cotton-cloth, knife, cord
related: sprains-strains, fractures-splinting, carrying-casualty, leaving-casualty

!quick
1. **Check first:** can they take **four steps** on it unaided? If not, or a bone looks deformed, **don't walk**: shelter and get help.
2. **Strap and support:** keep the **boot on** and lace it firm; add figure-of-eight strapping and **two walking sticks**.
3. **Go slowly**, with someone beside them on the downhill side. Expect a third of normal speed.
4. **Stop and shelter** if pain is getting worse, they are getting cold, or it will get dark first.
!end

## Should you move at all?
Moving an injured person is a trade-off: getting nearer to help against making the injury worse or causing a second accident. Stay put and get help ([[a:leaving-casualty]], [[a:signalling-rescue]]) if:
- They **can't take four steps** on the leg, or there is **deformity**, a bone through the skin, or severe swelling straight away. Treat it as broken ([[a:fractures-splinting]]).
- There may be a **head, neck, back or pelvis injury**: a fall from height, a tumble down a slope, neck or back pain, numbness or tingling. Don't move them unless they are in danger.
- They are **confused, drowsy** or have been knocked out ([[a:head-injury]]), or have chest pain or breathing trouble.
- The walk out is long, steep, dark or in bad weather, and someone knows your route.

You can usually try walking out with:
- A **sprained ankle or knee** that takes weight (with pain).
- An **arm, wrist, hand or collarbone injury** in a sling.
- Cuts and grazes that have stopped bleeding.

## Ankle injuries
- **Leave the boot on.** It splints the ankle, and once swelling starts you may never get it back on. Loosen it only if the toes go pale, numb or cold.
- **Lace it firm**, especially round the ankle. Skip the top hooks and tie a reef knot below them if that is more comfortable.
- **Figure-of-eight strapping over the boot:** take a triangular bandage, scarf or strip of cotton cloth folded into a long band. Put the middle under the instep (in front of the heel), cross it over the top of the foot, wrap behind the ankle, cross again over the front, and tie off at the front. It should feel snug, not tight.
- Rest, cool and elevate it at each stop if you can ([[a:sprains-strains]]).

## Knee injuries
- Wrap a bandage or cloth firmly from mid-calf to mid-thigh for support. Check feeling and colour below it at each stop.
- **Avoid deep bends**: step up and down with the good leg first ("up with the good, down with the bad").
- On a steep descent, go down **sideways** or even **backwards**, which spares the knee.
- A knee that locks, gives way repeatedly or swells hugely in the first hour needs a splint ([[a:fractures-splinting]]) and is a reason to stop.

## Sticks, poles and crutches
- **Two sticks** are better than one. Cut or find straight, sound dead wood about chest height and 3–4 cm thick. Use them like walking poles to take weight off the injured leg.
- **Improvised crutch:** a forked branch at armpit height, the fork padded with a rolled hat, glove or cloth. Take the weight on your **hands** through a side grip, not on the armpit, which can damage nerves. Crutches are slow and tiring on rough ground; poles are usually better.
- **A person as a crutch:** the helper stands on the injured side, the casualty's arm over their shoulders, the helper's arm round the waist. Change helpers every 20–30 minutes.

## Lower body vs upper body injuries
- **Leg injuries** slow everything down. Plan for a third to a half of normal speed and more rests. Carry the injured person's pack.
- **Arm injuries:** support the arm in a sling (a triangular bandage, a jacket hem pinned up, or a cord loop round the wrist and neck). The walker's legs work, but their **balance** and ability to catch themselves are gone. Keep off scrambles, and have someone walk on the downhill side.
- **Rib injuries:** walking is possible, but breathing hurts. Shallow breathing that worsens or breathlessness is a reason to stop.

## Going downhill
- Descents hurt injured legs more than climbs, and slips happen when tired.
- Choose the **gentlest line**, even if longer: zig-zag across steep slopes rather than straight down.
- On steep grass, the casualty can **sit and shuffle** down on their bottom, with someone below them, controlling speed. Never let anyone slide on wet grass, snow or above a drop.

## When to stop and shelter instead
- **Pain or swelling is getting worse**, or the leg starts giving way.
- They are getting **cold**, slow or quiet: injury plus cold leads to [[a:hypothermia]] and [[a:shock]].
- You will not reach a road or shelter **before dark** ([[t:sun]]).
- Then: shelter, insulate, signal, and send two people for help if you can ([[a:leaving-casualty]], [[a:emergency-night-out]]).

!tip Painkillers
If you carry paracetamol or ibuprofen, follow the dose on the packet. They help someone walk, but they also hide warning pain: go just as carefully.
!end

!quick
1. **Only carry** someone to get them **out of danger**, to **shelter close by**, or a **short distance** to a road. Otherwise make them warm where they are and send for help.
2. **Suspected neck, back or pelvis injury:** don't move them unless they are in danger. Keep head and body in line.
3. Short distances: **drag** on a jacket or tarp, or a **two-person seat**.
4. Longer: an **improvised stretcher** needs **at least 4 carriers, ideally 6–8**, swapping every few minutes.
!end

## Is it worth it?
Carrying a person over British hill ground is extremely hard. Mountain rescue teams use a purpose-built stretcher and often **a dozen or more people** taking turns, and it still takes hours to move a few kilometres.

**Carry when:**
- The person is in **immediate danger**: rising water, rockfall, a cliff edge, a road, fire.
- **Shelter** (a wall, a bothy, trees out of the wind) is a few hundred metres away and they are getting cold.
- A road or building is **very close**, the ground is easy, and you have enough fit people.

**Don't carry when:**
- It is far, steep or rough, or it will get dark first.
- There are only one or two of you: you will exhaust yourselves and may end up with two casualties.
- They may have a **spinal, pelvic or thigh (femur) injury**, or are seriously ill. Rough handling can kill.
- Someone knows your route and help will come. Make them warm and **signal** instead ([[a:signalling-rescue]], [[a:leaving-casualty]]).

!danger Possible spinal injury
Think spine if they fell from a height, tumbled down a slope, were hit on the head, or have neck or back pain, tingling, numbness or weakness. Leave them where they are unless there is danger. If you must move them, keep the **head, neck and body in a straight line**: one person holds the head with both hands at all times, and roll or slide them as one unit.
!end

## Before any carry
- Treat life threats first ([[a:primary-survey]]): bleeding, airway, breathing.
- Splint fractures ([[a:fractures-splinting]]) and **insulate and wrap them** before moving. A casualty being carried doesn't generate heat and chills fast.
- Plan the route, clear obstacles, and agree on commands: "**Ready, lift**", "**Ready, lower**". Everyone lifts with **legs bent and back straight**.

## Dragging (one rescuer, short distances)
- **Clothes drag:** grip the shoulders of their jacket, support their head on your forearms, and walk backwards, pulling them along the line of the body.
- **Tarp or jacket drag:** roll them onto a tarp, survival bag or coat and pull it by the head end. Works well on wet grass and snow; protects the body.
- Drags are the safest one-person way to move someone out of danger, including when you suspect spinal injury (pull along the line of the body, never sideways).

## One-person carries
- **Piggyback:** for a conscious casualty with a leg injury who can hold on. Practical for a few hundred metres on easy ground. Use sticks or walking poles for balance.
- **Supported walk:** the casualty's arm over your shoulders, your arm round their waist (see [[a:moving-with-injury]]).
- **Fireman's lift** (casualty over your shoulders): it is rarely recommended now. It risks injuring the carrier's back, presses on the casualty's chest and belly, and puts their head down. Use it only to get someone out of immediate danger when nothing else works.

## Two-person seats (conscious casualty)
- **Four-handed seat:** each carrier grips their own right wrist with their left hand, then grips the other carrier's left wrist with their right hand, making a square seat. The casualty sits on it with arms round both carriers' shoulders.
- **Two-handed seat:** for someone who can't hold on. The carriers crouch either side, reach one arm behind the casualty's back and grip each other's shoulders or clothes, and link the other hands (fingers hooked, or holding a pad) under the thighs.
- **Pole seat:** a stout pole, padded with a jacket, held between two carriers for the casualty to sit on.
- Two-person seats work for **50–200 m** on even ground. Swap carriers often.

## Improvised stretcher
**Jackets and poles:**
1. Find **two strong, straight poles** 2–2.5 m long and 4–6 cm thick (dead but sound wood, or walking poles taped together).
2. Zip up **two or three jackets** with the **sleeves turned inside**. Thread the poles through the sleeves so the bodies of the jackets form the bed.
3. Test it with an uninjured person before using it on the casualty.

**Tarp or blanket:**
1. Lay the tarp flat. Put one pole about a third of the way across and fold the tarp over it.
2. Put the second pole on the folded part, leaving a hand's width, and fold the rest of the tarp back over it.
3. The casualty's weight pins the folds; tie or knot the corners if it slips.

**Carrying it:**
- Carry **feet first** on the level and downhill; **head uphill** on steep slopes and when going up.
- **Four carriers** minimum, one at each corner; with **six to eight**, swap every **5–10 minutes**. A third person goes ahead to find the line.
- Strap the casualty on with cord or slings over a jacket, not directly over injuries.
- Lift and lower on command, together.

## Look after the carriers
- Carrying causes sweating, then chilling as soon as you stop. Carriers vent layers while working and add them at rests.
- Eat and drink at each change. Exhausted carriers drop stretchers and slip.
- If progress drops below a few hundred metres an hour, stop. Make a shelter and send for help ([[a:finding-help]]).

1. **Shelter and insulate** them: off the ground, in a survival bag, every spare layer, hat on. Food, water, whistle and torch **within reach**.
2. **Unconscious and breathing?** Recovery position, and ideally **someone stays with them**.
3. **Mark the spot**: GPS waypoint ([[t:gps]]) and bright kit on open ground nearby.
4. **Leave a note** (time, injuries, treatment, where you've gone) and **take a written [[a:location-report]]** with you.
!end

## The decision
Leaving someone injured is one of the hardest decisions you will face. It is often the right one.
- **Best:** two people go for help together, at least one stays with the casualty. Never send someone alone if you can avoid it.
- **If there are only two of you**, decide between staying and signalling ([[a:signalling-rescue]]) or going. Go if: no one knows where you are, nobody is likely to pass, signalling has failed, and help is within reach in daylight.
- **An unconscious casualty** may block their airway, vomit or roll into danger. Leave them alone only if there is no other way to get help.

## Make them safe before you leave
### Shelter and insulation
- Move them only if needed and safe ([[a:carrying-casualty]]). Get them **out of the wind** behind a wall, boulder or bank.
- **Insulate underneath** with packs, rope, heather, bracken or spare clothing. The ground takes heat faster than the air ([[a:insulation-ground]]).
- Put them in a **survival bag** or wrap them in a tarp, with every spare layer, a **hat** and gloves. Leave them your warm layers if you will be moving and generating heat.
- If you have a tarp, rig it low over them ([[a:tarp-rigging]]).

### Position
- **Unconscious and breathing normally:** recovery position, on their side with the airway open ([[a:recovery-position]]). Put padding under the head and along the back so they can't roll onto their face or back.
- **Conscious:** whatever position is most comfortable, usually half sitting against a pack. Keep injured limbs splinted and supported.

### Within reach
- **Water** (a full bottle), **food** (easy to eat: sweets, bars), **whistle**, **torch** (a spare if you have one), and any medicines they need, such as an inhaler.
- Tell a conscious casualty: **stay put**, keep warm, eat and drink a little often, and blow **six blasts** on the whistle when they hear voices, vehicles or a helicopter.

## Mark the spot
- Save a **waypoint** with [[t:gps]] and write the grid reference down. Check it twice.
- Spread **bright kit** on open ground near them: a tarp, orange bag, jacket. Tie something bright high on a pole or tree if they are hidden ([[a:be-visible]]).
- Note a **named feature** (stream junction, wall corner, summit) and the distance and direction from it.
- As you leave, look back and notice landmarks. **Tick off features** on your way out so you can lead rescuers back.

## Write a note (leave it with the casualty)
Put it in a pocket or tie it to their pack, in a plastic bag if you have one. Write clearly in pencil:

!checklist The note
- **Name** of the casualty, age, and any medical conditions or allergies you know.
- **Time** of the accident and the **time you left**.
- **What happened** and the **injuries** you found.
- **Treatment given**, with times. A tourniquet: write the time it went on.
- **Their condition** when you left: conscious, breathing, pulse, cold.
- **Where you have gone**, by which route, and when you expect to return or send help.
- Your **name**.
!end

Start a casualty log with [[t:casualty]] to keep timed notes of their condition before you go.

## Take a copy with you
- Fill in the [[a:location-report]]: **grid reference, what happened, how many, their condition, kit, and how they will signal.** Take it with you on this phone and on paper.
- When you reach a person or a working phone, hand it over and ask them to raise the alarm on 999, asking for Police then Mountain Rescue ([[a:finding-help]], [[a:when-you-reach-help]]).

## On your way
- **Don't become the second casualty.** Move steadily, not fast. Stay on safe ground ([[a:pacing-yourself]]).
- Leave **arrows** or markers at junctions so you and the rescuers can find your way back.
- If you can't reach help before dark, stop, shelter, and set off again at first light. Rescuers can't use a messenger who is lost too.

## Sources
- Mountain Rescue England and Wales: What to do in an emergency; Giving your location (mountain.rescue.org.uk)
- Scottish Mountain Rescue: What to do in an emergency (scottishmountainrescue.org)
- St John Ambulance: The recovery position (sja.org.uk)
- Mountain Training: Hill and Moorland Leader: emergency procedures (mountain-training.org)

=== daily-routine
title: The daily routine
summary: A concrete day-by-day schedule for surviving in the open: water, firewood, shelter, food, signals, hygiene, rest and preparing before dark.
tags: routine, daily, schedule, camp chores, water, firewood, signals, hygiene, log, day, morale
icon: calendar-clock
level: moderate
needs: steel-pot, steel-bottle, knife, this-phone
related: rationing, sleeping-warm, long-term-shelter, signalling-rescue
---
!quick
1. **First light:** check everyone, restart the fire, **boil water**, check signals are in place.
2. **Morning:** collect water and **three times the firewood you think you need**; repair the shelter.
3. **Midday:** eat, rest out of the sun or rain; do light jobs.
4. **Two hours before dark:** wood stacked, water boiled, dry clothes ready, everything in reach. Write the day's log ([[t:notebook]]).
!end

## Why routine matters
After the first night, the danger is slow: getting a little colder, wetter, more dehydrated and tired each day, and missing rescuers. A fixed routine makes sure the vital jobs happen while you still have the energy, and it keeps your mind steady. Write it down and stick to it.

Check the day length with [[t:sun]]. In a British winter you may get only **7–8 hours of light** (less in the north of Scotland); in midsummer, **16 hours or more**.

## A sample day (adjust to daylight)
| Time | Job |
|---|---|
| First light | Wake, toilet, check everyone (cold, injuries, feet). Revive the fire from banked embers. |
| +30 min | Boil water for the day. Hot drink and food. Air the bedding. |
| +1 hour | **Signal check:** ground signal, flag, signal fire ready. Whistle and torch to hand. |
| Morning | Water run. **Firewood**: gather, sort, stack under cover. |
| Late morning | Shelter work: repair, thicken, drain. Dry clothes and boots. |
| Midday | Eat. Rest in the shade in summer or in the shelter in cold rain. |
| Afternoon | Light jobs: food, tools, cordage, washing. Second wood run. |
| 2 hours before dark | **Evening prep** (below). |
| Dusk | Eat the main meal. Hot water bottle. Log. Bank the fire. |
| Night | Sleep. Check the fire. Signal with the torch if you see lights. |

## Morning checks
- **People:** anyone shivering, confused, quiet or limping? Feet: white, wrinkled, numb or swollen? ([[a:cold-injuries]]). Wounds: redder, hotter, smelly? ([[a:wound-care]]).
- **Urine:** pale straw is good. Dark means drink more ([[a:dehydration-signs]]).
- **Kit:** knife, fire kit, torch, whistle, phone in the same place every time. Losing the ferro rod is a disaster.
- **Weather:** what is the sky doing? Plan wet-weather jobs for later if it is dry now ([[a:mountain-weather]]).

## Water
- Collect enough for the whole day **in the morning**: at least **2–3 litres a person** in cool weather, more in heat or with hard work ([[a:water-needs]]).
- **Boil it** ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]). Use the [[t:boil]] timer. Keep boiled and unboiled water in separate, marked containers.
- **Never ration water you have**: drink it. Ration sweat instead.

## Firewood: gather three times what you think
- People always underestimate. A fire that burns through a long British winter night can need **a big armful every hour**.
- Collect **dead wood off the ground and standing dead branches** ([[a:firewood-uk]]). Sort into tinder, kindling, wrist-thick and leg-thick.
- **Stack it under cover** and keep the next day's tinder dry inside the shelter.
- Gather while you are fresh, in daylight. Collecting in the dark is how people get lost and injured.

## Shelter maintenance
- Every day, **add insulation**, patch leaks, clear the drainage ditch and refresh the bed ([[a:long-term-shelter]]).
- Air the bedding on dry days.
- Check overhead for **dead branches**, especially after wind.

## Food
- Eat **at regular times**, a little and often, more on hard-working days ([[a:rationing]]).
- Forage or fish only when it gains more than it costs; do it near camp, in the warm part of the day ([[a:food-priorities]]).

## Signals
- **Keep ground signals clear and visible** ([[a:be-visible]]). Re-lay stones and cloth that have moved; clear snow and leaves.
- Keep a **signal fire** built and covered, ready to light with green leaves for smoke ([[a:signalling-rescue]]).
- **Listen and look** at set times: on the hour, for a few minutes. Blow six blasts on the whistle.

## Hygiene
- Toilet **at least 30 m from water and camp**, and dig it in ([[a:latrines]]).
- **Wash hands** after the toilet and before food, even with just water and ash ([[a:water-hygiene]]).
- Dry and check feet every evening. Clean teeth with a frayed twig if nothing else.
- Keep food sealed and away from the sleeping area.

## Rest
- Rest during the **warmest part of the day** in summer, and in the middle of the day if it is wet.
- Sleep is part of survival. Short naps are fine.

## Evening prep (start 2 hours before dark)
!checklist Before dark
- Wood stacked for the whole night, within reach of the bed
- Water boiled; bottles full; a hot water bottle ready ([[a:sleeping-warm]])
- Dry sleeping clothes and socks set aside
- Torch, whistle, knife, fire kit in the same pocket
- Boots and wet kit placed to dry safely ([[a:drying-clothes]])
- Toilet visited
- Log written
!end

## Keep a log
Write a short entry each evening in [[t:notebook]] or on paper: **day number, weather, water and food used and left, everyone's health, signals sent, aircraft or people seen or heard**, and your plan for tomorrow. It helps you spot trends (you are getting weaker; the food won't last), keeps track of days, and tells rescuers what happened if you are found.

- NHS: Water, drinks and your health; Dehydration (nhs.uk)
- UK Health Security Agency: Preventing infection and hand hygiene (gov.uk)

=== rationing
title: Rationing food and energy
summary: How to make food last: never ration water, eat little and often, don't burn more energy getting food than it gives, and why lean meat alone isn't enough.
tags: rationing, food, calories, energy, starvation, rabbit starvation, protein, fat, hunger, ration
icon: chart-pie
level: moderate
related: food-priorities, water-needs, daily-routine, foraging-rules
---
!quick
1. **Never ration water you have.** Drink it. Without water, eat little, especially protein.
2. Divide your food into **daily portions** for the time you expect to wait, then eat **a little and often**.
3. **Save energy**: rest, keep warm and dry. Don't spend hours hunting or foraging for less food than it costs.
4. **Lean meat alone is not enough**: eat fat and carbohydrate with it.
!end

## Water first, food second
- Dehydration kills in days and makes you weak, cold and muddled within hours. **Drink the water you have** and find more ([[a:finding-water-uk]], [[a:water-needs]]).
- Digesting food, especially **protein**, uses water. With little or no water, eat mostly carbohydrate in small amounts, or nothing.
- A healthy adult can survive **weeks** without food, but feels it within a day or two: weakness, cold, irritability and poor decisions ([[a:food-priorities]]).

## How much do you need?
The NHS guide is roughly **2,000 kcal a day** for women and **2,500 kcal** for men doing ordinary activity. In the field:
- **Resting in shelter** in mild weather uses not much more than that.
- **Cold** raises your needs a lot: your body burns fuel to make heat, and shivering is hard work.
- **Walking in the hills with a pack** can double your needs.

| Activity (rough guide) | Energy use |
|---|---|
| Lying or sitting, warm | Low |
| Light camp jobs | Low to moderate |
| Shivering through a cold night | Moderate to high |
| Gathering firewood, building shelter | High |
| Walking uphill with a pack | Very high |

## Making food last
1. **Count it.** Lay out all the food. Estimate how many days until rescue or until you can walk out, and add a margin.
2. **Divide it into daily portions** and bag them up. Seeing the day's ration stops you nibbling tomorrow's.
3. **Eat perishable food first** (sandwiches, fruit, anything opened), keep sealed, dense foods for later.
4. **Eat a little and often**, through the day. It keeps energy and warmth steady and stops the crash of one big meal.
5. **Save the fatty, slow food for the evening**: nuts, cheese, chocolate, oily food. It helps you sleep warm ([[a:sleeping-warm]]).
6. **Eat more on hard days**: when you walk out, build shelter or carry someone. Going weak into a hard day is dangerous.
7. **Share fairly in a group**. Children, the injured and the cold may need more. Arguments over food destroy morale.

## Energy is a resource too
- Every calorie you save is one you don't have to find. **Rest**, stay in the shelter in bad weather, and do heavy jobs in one efficient go.
- **Stay dry and out of the wind.** Being cold and wet burns energy faster than almost anything else ([[a:cold-and-wet]]).
- Use a **fire for warmth** ([[a:fire-reflector]]) and wood instead of your body's stores.

## Food that costs more than it gives
Wild food in Britain is often low in energy for the effort it takes:
- **Leafy greens** (nettles, wild garlic) give vitamins and morale but very few calories.
- **Berries** in season give some sugar, but you need a lot of picking for a meal.
- **Hunting and trapping** usually take days of skill and luck, burn a lot of energy, and are tightly restricted by law ([[a:trapping-law]]).
- **Fish** may give a better return if you can fish near camp ([[a:fishing-basics]]). Shellfish carry a real poisoning risk ([[a:shellfish-shore]]).

Rule of thumb: forage close to camp, in daylight and in the warm part of the day, only for **certain, safe, energy-rich food** ([[a:foraging-rules]]). Never eat a plant or fungus you can't identify for certain ([[a:edibility-test-myth]]).

## Rabbit starvation (protein poisoning)
Lean meat such as rabbit, if eaten almost alone for days, can make people ill: nausea, diarrhoea, weakness and a strong craving for fat, even though they are eating plenty. The body can't use large amounts of protein as its main fuel.
- **Eat the fat** on any meat, including fat round the kidneys and the organs (cooked well).
- Combine meat with **carbohydrate**: berries, nuts, roots you are certain of, or carried food.
- With little water, avoid large amounts of protein.

## Signs you are running low
- Feeling cold all the time, dizziness on standing, clumsiness, poor sleep, and irritability or apathy.
- **Low blood sugar** feels like sudden weakness, shaking and confusion. Eat something sugary and rest.
- Write food used and left in your daily log ([[t:notebook]]), so you see the trend in good time ([[a:daily-routine]]).

## Sources
- NHS: Understanding calories (nhs.uk)
- British Nutrition Foundation: Energy and nutrients (nutrition.org.uk)
- Mountain Training: Food and energy on the hill (mountain-training.org)
- Mountain Rescue England and Wales: Hill safety (mountain.rescue.org.uk)

=== sleeping-warm
title: Sleeping warm
summary: The full method for a warm night out: insulation underneath, dry sleeping clothes, food before bed, a safe hot water bottle, warm stones, and sharing warmth.
tags: sleep, night, warm, cold night, hot water bottle, insulation, sleeping bag, condensation, warm rocks, huddle, bedtime
icon: bed-single
level: easy
needs: steel-bottle, cotton-cloth, survival-bag, warm-layers
related: sleeping-warm, insulation-ground, hypothermia, drying-clothes
---
!quick
1. **Thick insulation under you** first: at least a hand's depth when squashed. It matters more than what's on top.
2. Change into **dry clothes** kept just for sleeping; **hat on**; **pee** before bed.
3. **Eat** something fatty or sugary and **drink** something warm before you lie down.
4. A **hot water bottle**: steel bottle filled with hot (not boiling) water, lid tight, **wrapped** in cloth.
!end

## Below beats above
The ground steals heat far faster than still air. Most cold nights out are cold because of what's under you, not what's over you.
- Build a bed of **dry bracken, heather, leaves, grass or conifer needles**, at least **20–30 cm** deep before you lie on it. It squashes down to a fraction of that ([[a:insulation-ground]]).
- Add your **empty rucksack, rope, map case, spare clothes** under the hips and shoulders, where your weight presses down.
- Raise yourself off wet ground with logs or a platform if you can ([[a:long-term-shelter]]).
- Top up the bed every day: it compresses and gets damp.

## Clothes for sleeping
- **Dry clothes** keep you far warmer than damp ones. If you have a spare base layer or socks, keep them **only for sleeping**, sealed in a bag.
- Damp clothes: wear them in the bag only if you have nothing dry; your body heat can dry thin synthetic layers overnight, but cotton stays wet ([[a:drying-clothes]]).
- **Hat and neck covering**. A head and neck left out lose a lot of warmth. Pull the hood or hat down over your ears.
- **Loose, not tight.** Tight clothing and too many socks squeeze out insulation and blood flow, making feet colder.

## Fuel up
- Eat **fat and carbohydrate** before bed: nuts, cheese, chocolate, oats. Your body burns it slowly through the night.
- Drink something **warm** (and keep a bottle in reach). Dehydrated people get colder.
- Keep a **snack in your pocket** for when you wake cold at 3 am.

## Get into bed warm
- Do 5 minutes of brisk movement (press-ups, squats, star jumps) before getting in, **without sweating**.
- Once in, warm your **hands and feet** first.
- Wake cold? Eat, clench and relax big muscles (thighs, buttocks) for a minute, and turn over.

## Pee before bed
A full bladder takes heat to keep warm and wakes you in the night. Go just before you settle. If it's cold or wet out, a marked wide-necked bottle as a pee bottle saves leaving the shelter (never confuse it with a drinking bottle).

## Hot water bottle (steel bottle)
The single-walled steel bottle makes a good hot water bottle, carefully.
1. Heat water until steaming hot, then let it stand off the boil for a minute or two.
2. Fill the bottle, screw the lid on **tight**, and test the seal by holding it upside down over the fire or pot for 10 seconds.
3. **Wrap** it in a sock, cotton cloth or spare layer. A steel bottle gets far hotter on the outside than a rubber one.
4. Put it at your **core**: between the thighs, at the belly, or under the armpits. These warm the blood. On the feet it warms only the feet.

!warn Burns
Never put a hot bottle against bare skin, especially on anyone who is numb, very cold, a child, or asleep: they may not feel a burn. Don't fill it with boiling water. Check the lid seal every time: a leak at night soaks your bedding.
!end

## Warm stones
- Heat dry, solid stones (fist-sized) **beside** the fire for an hour, not in it.
- **Never use stones from rivers, streams or wet ground**: water inside can turn to steam and make them **burst** ([[a:fire-reflector]]).
- Let them cool until you can hold one in your hand for a few seconds, wrap in cloth, and put them at your feet or core. A layer of warm stones under the bed (covered with soil and then bedding) gives gentle heat for hours.

## Breath and condensation
- **Don't breathe into your sleeping bag or survival bag.** Each breath adds moisture that dampens the insulation and makes it colder night after night.
- Keep mouth and nose out, with a hat or scarf over the face if needed.
- Survival bags and plastic sheets trap **condensation** from your body. Expect the inside to be damp by morning; turn it inside out and air it during the day.
- Leave some **ventilation** in any shelter.

## Sharing warmth
- Sleep **close together**, back to back or spooning, under shared covers. Put the coldest person **in the middle**.
- For someone who is hypothermic, body-to-body warmth inside a sleeping bag or survival bag is a recognised way to rewarm ([[a:hypothermia]]).

## Fire at night
- A fire outside an open-fronted shelter, with a reflector, can keep you warm all night ([[a:fire-reflector]]). Bank it before you sleep ([[a:banking-fire]]).
- **Never bring a fire, stove, charcoal or embers inside an enclosed shelter**: carbon monoxide kills silently.

- Mountaineering Scotland: Winter skills: staying warm overnight (mountaineering.scot)
- NHS: Hypothermia (nhs.uk)
- British Red Cross: First aid for hypothermia (redcross.org.uk)

=== drying-clothes
title: Drying clothes and boots
summary: How to dry clothes with body heat and a fire without ruining them, keep boots wearable, and look after your feet over days.
tags: drying, wet clothes, boots, socks, fire, synthetic, melting, foot care, trench foot, damp
icon: heater
time: 1–3 hours
level: easy
needs: cord, firewood, cotton-cloth
related: cold-injuries, sleeping-warm, layering, cold-and-wet
---
!quick
1. **Thin synthetic or wool layers** dry fastest **on you**, under a waterproof, while you move.
2. By a fire: hang clothes on a line **at the side** of the fire, where you can hold your hand for 10 seconds. **Never** over the flames.
3. **Boots:** take out insoles, stuff with dry grass or paper, change it every hour or two, and keep them **away** from flames.
4. **Damp socks** dry overnight inside your clothes on your chest. Dry and air your feet every night.
!end

## Why it matters
Wet clothes lose most of their insulation and pull heat from your body many times faster than dry ones. Wet feet for days cause **non-freezing cold injury** (trench foot), which can cripple a walker ([[a:cold-injuries]]). Getting dry is a daily survival task, not a comfort.

## Body drying
- Your body is a gentle, safe dryer. Thin **synthetic** and **wool** layers dry on you in an hour or two if you are **warm and active**, with a windproof or waterproof on top.
- Wring out wet layers hard first. Put them back on under a dry layer and a shell.
- **Cotton** (jeans, t-shirts) holds water and barely dries this way. Take it off and dry it by the fire, or wear it only when warm and dry.
- **Don't body-dry when you're already cold.** If someone is shivering, put them in dry clothes, or the warmest you have, and dry the wet things another way ([[a:hypothermia]]).

## Fire drying
- Rig a **drying line** of cord between two sticks or trees **beside** the fire, about **1–1.5 m** from the flames, and not downwind where sparks and smoke blow.
- **The hand test:** hold your hand where the clothes hang. If you can't keep it there for **10 seconds**, it is too hot for the clothes.
- **Turn** things every 10–15 minutes. The side facing the fire dries; the other stays wet.
- **Stay with them.** Clothes fall off lines into fires. A burnt jacket in the British hills is a serious problem.
- Hang **smaller items** (gloves, hats, socks) on sticks pushed into the ground at an angle near the fire.
- Steam rising means it's working. Smoke or a hot plastic smell means move it back now.

!warn Synthetics melt
Fleece, nylon, polyester, polypropylene, waterproof membranes and down jackets can **shrink, glaze, melt or catch a spark** well before they look scorched. A tiny spark hole lets the down out of a jacket. Keep them further back than cotton and wool, and never drape them over hot stones or a reflector.
!end

## Boots
Boots that are damaged by heat can end your ability to walk out. Dry them slowly.
1. **Take out the insoles** and dry them separately. Open the laces wide and pull the tongue forward.
2. **Stuff the boots** with dry grass, dry bracken, moss squeezed dry, newspaper or a dry cloth. It wicks water out. **Change the stuffing every hour or two** while it keeps coming out damp.
3. Stand them **upside down on sticks**, or on their sides, a good distance from the fire, **toes away from the heat**. They should feel just warm to touch, never hot.
4. **Never put boots right by the flames** or on hot stones. Leather shrinks and cracks, glue fails, rubber soles peel, and synthetic uppers melt.
5. **At night**, put boots in a bag inside the shelter or at the foot of your sleeping bag, so they don't freeze stiff.

If you can't dry them, **put on dry socks** with a plastic bag over each (a vapour barrier) inside the wet boots for short periods. Take the bags off at night: they make skin soft and sore.

## Socks and small items overnight
- Put **damp (not soaking) socks**, gloves or a buff **inside your clothes on your chest or belly**, or between the sleeping bag and your base layer. Body heat dries them by morning, and they are warm when you put them on.
- Wring them out first. Don't do this with soaking items, or if you are cold.

## Rotation
- Keep at least **two pairs of socks** in rotation: one on, one drying. Keep a **dry pair just for sleeping** ([[a:sleeping-warm]]).
- In wet weather it is better to walk in wet clothes and keep a dry set for camp than to have nothing dry.

## Foot health
!checklist Every evening
- Take boots and socks off. **Dry, air and warm** the feet for at least 20–30 minutes.
- Look for **white, wrinkled, numb, swollen or blotchy** skin, and for blisters and hot spots ([[a:blisters]]).
- **Massage** the feet gently to bring blood back (not if they are frozen).
- Put on **dry socks** for the night, loose enough to keep the circulation good.
!end

- **Wet, cold feet for hours and days** cause trench foot: feet go numb, white or blotchy, then swollen, red and painful when they warm up. It can take weeks to heal. Prevent it by drying feet daily and never sleeping in wet socks ([[a:cold-injuries]]).

## Sources
- NHS: Hypothermia; Frostbite (nhs.uk)
- Mountain Training: Expedition skills (mountain-training.org)
- Mountaineering Scotland: Winter skills: clothing and staying dry (mountaineering.scot)
- British Army Medical Services: Non-freezing cold injury guidance (gov.uk)

=== long-term-shelter

summary: Turn an emergency shelter into one you can live in for days or weeks: raised bed, thicker roof, drainage, windbreak, reflector, door, and safe ventilation.
tags: long-term shelter, improve shelter, raised bed, thatch, debris, drainage, windbreak, door, ventilation, carbon monoxide, widowmaker, flooding
icon: house-plus
time: a day or more
level: hard
needs: knife, cord, tarp
related: debris-hut, site-selection, insulation-ground, fire-reflector
---
!quick
1. **Check the site** again: no **dead branches or trees** overhead, not in a **flood** line, out of the wind.
2. **Raise the bed** and thicken the **roof and walls** to keep out cold and rain.
3. Dig a **drainage ditch** on the uphill side, add a **windbreak** and a **door**.
4. **Never** have a fire, stove or charcoal inside an enclosed shelter: **carbon monoxide** kills.
!end

## From emergency to long-term
The first night's shelter is about surviving the night ([[a:shelter-priorities]]). If you will be there for days, improve it a little every day, in this order: **safety, dryness, warmth, comfort**.

## 1. Re-check the site
You chose it in a hurry. Look again in daylight ([[a:site-selection]]).
- **Widowmakers:** dead trees and dead branches above you can fall without warning, especially in wind. **Ash dieback** has left many ash trees in Britain brittle and dying: look for bare crowns and dead tops. Beech and birch also drop heavy limbs. If in doubt, move.
- **Flooding:** look for lines of debris caught in bushes, flattened grass and silt on the ground. These show how high water has been. Stay well above them and away from flat ground beside streams.
- **Cold hollows:** cold air drains into hollows at night; a spot a little up the slope is often warmer.
- **Exposure:** prevailing winds in Britain are from the **south-west**. Face the opening away from it, usually to the east or south-east.
- **Rockfall, livestock paths, ant nests, and midges** by still water in Scotland in summer.
- **Water** within a few minutes' walk, and **firewood** nearby.

## 2. A raised bed
Lying on the ground, even on a thin bed, slowly drains heat and gets damp.
- Make a **frame** of two logs about 20–30 cm high either side of where you lie, pegged so they can't roll.
- Fill it with **dry bracken, heather, grass, leaves or dead conifer twigs**, packed hard, at least **30 cm** deep.
- Or build a platform of straight poles across two logs, then the bedding on top.
- **Fluff it up and add more every day**; replace it when it gets damp or smells.

## 3. Thicker roof and walls
- A natural roof needs to be **thick**: for debris shelters, at least **60 cm** of leaves and litter keeps out wind and rain ([[a:debris-hut]]). Thin is cold and leaky.
- **Thatch from the bottom up**, like roof tiles: each layer of bracken, grass or leafy branches overlaps the one below, tips pointing down.
- Steeper roofs shed rain better. Aim for at least **45°**.
- Lay **branches over the debris** so the wind can't strip it.
- With a tarp, pitch it **taut** with a steep side to the weather, and add debris on top for insulation ([[a:tarp-rigging]]).

## 4. Drainage
- Dig a **ditch about a spade's width and 10–15 cm deep** round the uphill side, leading the water away round the shelter.
- Raise the floor a little above the ground around it.
- Where the tarp or roof drips, make sure water runs away, not into the shelter.

## 5. Windbreak, reflector and fire
- Build a **wall** of logs, turf, stones or woven branches on the windward side, or across the opening, to stop draughts.
- Put the fire **outside** the open front, about **1 m** from the edge, with a **reflector wall** of logs or stones behind it to throw heat back at you ([[a:fire-reflector]]).
- Keep fuel stacked within reach, under cover.

## 6. A door
- Close the entrance at night with a **plug** of debris in a bag or jacket, a woven hurdle of branches, or a hanging tarp.
- Make it easy to open from inside, quickly.

## 7. Ventilation and carbon monoxide
!danger Carbon monoxide
Burning anything in an enclosed space (a shelter, tent, snow hole, car or bothy without a working chimney) can fill it with **carbon monoxide**, which you can't see or smell. It causes **headache, dizziness, nausea, drowsiness, confusion** and then death, often in sleep. Never bring a fire, stove, charcoal, embers or a barbecue into an enclosed shelter. Keep fires outside open-fronted shelters. If anyone gets a headache or feels sick near a fire, get everyone into fresh air at once.
!end
- Even without a fire, leave a **gap at the top** of a closed shelter. Your breath fills a sealed shelter with moisture that soaks the insulation.
- Condensation dripping inside means you need more airflow.

## 8. Living in it
- **Keep it tidy:** kit in the same place, wet things outside or near the door, food sealed.
- **Toilet** at least 30 m away and downhill of water ([[a:latrines]]).
- **Air the bedding** on dry days and check the roof after every storm.
- **Keep your signals** visible from the shelter ([[a:be-visible]]).

!law Landowners and the law
Building shelters, cutting live trees and digging are not covered by access rights in **Scotland** or **England and Wales**. In a genuine emergency, use dead material, cause as little damage as you can, and put things back when you leave.
!end

!quick
1. **Stop.** Turn on the phone, open [[t:gps]] for a **grid reference**, write it down, and check it on the [[t:map]]. Then switch off.
2. No GPS? Work out your **last known point**, the **time** since then and the **direction** you've walked: that gives an **area** you must be in.
3. Match the ground to the map **inside that area**: slope direction, streams, contours, walls.
4. Still unsure? **Retrace**, or head for a **catching feature** you can't miss. In fog or darkness near crags, **stay put**.
!end

## Start with the phone
- The phone's GPS works **without signal or SIM**: it listens to satellites. In the open it usually gets a fix in under a minute; in deep valleys or forest it can take longer or be less accurate.
- Open [[t:gps]], wait until the accuracy figure is small (tens of metres), and **write down the grid reference** ([[a:grid-references]]).
- Open the [[t:map]] to see where that is, and note nearby features and the way out.
- **Switch the phone off** afterwards. Check again only at decision points ([[a:phone-navigation]]).
- Keep your paper map and compass skills going: phones fail in cold, wet and at low battery.

## Area of uncertainty
Without GPS, don't guess a point; work out an **area**.
1. **Last known point**: where were you last *certain*? A summit, a path junction, a bridge.
2. **Time since** you left it, and your speed. At about 4–5 km/h on paths and 2–3 km/h rough ground ([[a:naismith-pacing]]), 30 minutes is roughly 1–2 km.
3. **Rough direction**: which way was the sun, the wind, the slope?
4. Draw a rough shape on the map in that direction and distance. You are almost certainly inside it. Now look for features only within it.

## Read the land
### Slope aspect
This is one of the most reliable techniques in the hills.
1. Stand facing **straight down the slope** (the way a ball would roll).
2. Point the compass that way and read the bearing.
3. On the map, look in your area of uncertainty for slopes facing that direction (contour lines run at right angles to it; downhill is the direction of lower numbers).
4. Usually only one or two places fit. Combine with height or a nearby stream to pick the right one.

### Contours and shape
- Is the ground steep or gentle? Rising on the left or right? Are you on a spur (ground falls away on three sides) or in a valley (rises on both sides)? ([[a:contours-terrain]])
- Match the shape around you to the contour pattern: "steep on my left, stream below me, slope easing ahead".

### Streams
- **Water flows downhill**. The direction a stream flows tells you which way is down and which catchment you are in. A stream flowing the "wrong" way means you have crossed a watershed into the next valley: a very common mistake.
- Count **junctions**: two streams meeting is a precise point on the map.

### The sun
- Use [[t:sun]] for the sun's bearing at this time of day. In Britain the sun is roughly **south at about 13:00** in summer (British Summer Time) and about **12:00** in winter.
- Shadows and the sun's position give a direction check if you have no compass ([[a:finding-north]]).

### Features made by people
- Walls, fences, forest edges, tracks, power lines, buildings, dams and masts are on OS maps and easy to match ([[a:linear-features]]).

## Resection with a compass
If you can see **two or three features** you can identify on the map (a summit, a mast, a lake end, a building):
1. Take a **compass bearing** to the first feature.
2. Turn the bearing into a **back bearing** by adding 180° (or subtracting 180° if over 180°).
3. Put the compass on the map with the edge touching the feature, and turn the whole compass until the orienting lines match the grid lines (north to north). Draw a line along the edge **from the feature back towards you**.
4. Repeat for a second feature, ideally **60–120°** away from the first. Where the lines cross is your position.
5. A third bearing makes a small triangle: you are inside it.

!tip The quick version
If you are **on a linear feature** (a path, stream, wall or ridge), one bearing to one identified feature is enough: where the back-bearing line crosses your path, you are there.
!end

Magnetic variation in Britain is now small (a couple of degrees at most), so for resection over a few kilometres you can usually ignore it ([[a:map-compass-basics]]).

## Catching features
If you still can't pin your position, head for a **long, unmissable line**: a road, a big river, a forest edge, a lake shore, a wall or a power line. Choose one you can't miss whichever way you've drifted, then follow it to a known point ([[a:handrails-catching]]).

## Retrace
- Going back the way you came, to the last known point, is often the safest choice if the ground was easy and it is light.
- Footprints in soft ground or snow show the way. Note features as you go.

## When to stop trying
- In **fog or darkness on steep or unknown ground**, stop. Shelter and wait for better visibility ([[a:lost-person-actions]], [[a:emergency-night-out]]).
- If you're cold, hungry or panicking, eat and put on a layer first. Low blood sugar makes navigation mistakes worse.

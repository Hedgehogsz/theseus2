# First aid. Aligned with NHS, St John Ambulance, British Red Cross and Resuscitation Council UK public guidance.

=== when-to-call-999
title: When to call 999 (and when 111)
summary: Which situations need an ambulance or rescue now, and what to say. Grid references, not what3words.
tags: 999, 112, 111, ambulance, emergency, what3words, grid reference
---
!quick
1. **Call 999/112 now** for: unresponsive, not breathing normally, chest pain, severe bleeding, severe breathing difficulty, stroke signs, seizure, severe allergic reaction, serious head injury, suspected broken spine/thigh/pelvis, hypothermia with confusion, anyone who can't walk out.
2. **NHS 111** (needs signal) for urgent but not life-threatening problems.
3. Give a **grid reference**, not what3words (it doesn't work offline): [[a:location-report]].
!end

## Call 999 or 112 immediately if someone
- Is unresponsive, or not breathing normally: start [[a:cpr-adult]].
- Has **severe bleeding** that won't stop: [[a:severe-bleeding]].
- Has chest pain or pressure, especially spreading to the arm, jaw or back: [[a:heart-attack-stroke]].
- Shows **FAST** stroke signs: Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech problems, Time to call.
- Has a **seizure** for the first time, or one lasting over 5 minutes: [[a:seizures]].
- Has signs of **anaphylaxis**: swollen lips or throat, wheeze, collapse: [[a:stings-anaphylaxis]].
- Has a **head injury** with knocked out, vomiting, confusion or drowsiness: [[a:head-injury]].
- May have eaten a **poisonous plant or fungus**, even if they feel fine: [[a:poisoning]].
- Is **hypothermic** (confused, stopped shivering, drowsy), or has heatstroke.
- Cannot walk out because of injury or exhaustion, or is lost: [[a:calling-999]].

## What the call handler needs
1. **Where:** grid reference plus a description.
2. **What:** what happened and when.
3. **Who:** how many casualties, their age, whether they are conscious and breathing.
4. Stay on the line and follow the instructions. They will talk you through CPR if needed.

- NHS: Stroke symptoms, FAST
- Ofcom and BT: Calling 999 / 112

=== primary-survey
title: Primary survey: DRABC
summary: The first-aider's checklist for anyone hurt or collapsed: Danger, Response, Airway, Breathing, Circulation.
tags: DRABC, DR ABC, primary survey, unresponsive, assessment, first aid basics
---
!quick
1. **D**anger: make sure you, the casualty and bystanders are safe. **R**esponse: "Can you hear me? Open your eyes." Tap the shoulders.
2. **A**irway: tilt the head back and lift the chin.
3. **B**reathing: look, listen and feel for 10 seconds. Not breathing normally → **call 999 and start CPR**. Breathing → [[a:recovery-position]].
4. **C**irculation: look for and stop **severe bleeding**.
!end

## Step by step
### Danger
Check for traffic, water, falling rocks, fire, electricity and dangerous animals. Don't become a second casualty.

### Response
Kneel by them. Speak loudly, and gently tap their shoulders. Use **AVPU** to describe their level of response: **A**lert, responds to **V**oice, responds to **P**ain, **U**nresponsive.

### Airway
With the person on their back, put one hand on the forehead and two fingers under the chin, and tilt the head back to open the airway. Remove any obvious obstruction from the mouth.

### Breathing
Look at the chest, listen at the mouth and feel for breath on your cheek for **up to 10 seconds**. Occasional gasps are **not** normal breathing: treat this as cardiac arrest.
- **Not breathing normally:** call 999 (speaker on), send someone for a defibrillator (AED) if there is one, and start [[a:cpr-adult]] or [[a:cpr-child]].
- **Breathing:** check for other injuries, then put them in the [[a:recovery-position]] unless you suspect a spinal injury and they are safe where they are.

### Circulation
Look for severe bleeding, and treat it straight away with firm direct pressure ([[a:severe-bleeding]]). Catastrophic bleeding comes first, even before the airway.

## Secondary survey (once life threats are dealt with)
- Check head to toe for injuries; ask about pain, medical conditions, medicines, allergies, and the last meal.
- Note the time and any changes in their response or breathing every few minutes.
- Keep them warm and insulated from the ground.

=== cpr-adult
title: CPR for adults
summary: What to do when an adult is unresponsive and not breathing normally: call 999, push hard and fast, 30:2.
tags: CPR, cardiac arrest, chest compressions, rescue breaths, AED, defibrillator, resuscitation
---
!quick
1. Unresponsive and not breathing normally → **call 999** (speaker on) and get an **AED** if available.
2. Heel of hand in the **centre of the chest**, other hand on top; arms straight.
3. Push down **5–6 cm**, **100–120 times a minute**; let the chest come back up fully.
4. After **30 compressions**, give **2 rescue breaths** (if trained and willing), then repeat. Otherwise do **hands-only CPR** non-stop.
!end

## Doing CPR
1. Kneel beside them. Place the heel of one hand in the centre of the chest (the lower half of the breastbone) and the other hand on top, fingers interlocked.
2. Lean over with your shoulders above your hands and **arms straight**.
3. Press down hard, 5–6 cm, then release. Aim for 2 compressions per second (to the beat of "Stayin' Alive").
4. **Rescue breaths:** tilt the head back, lift the chin, pinch the nose, seal your mouth over theirs and blow steadily for about 1 second until the chest rises. Give 2 breaths, then go back to compressions immediately.
5. If you can't or won't give breaths, do continuous compressions. It still saves lives.
6. Swap with another person every 2 minutes if you can, with minimal pauses.

## Defibrillator (AED)
Switch it on and follow the spoken instructions. Keep doing CPR while the pads are attached, and stand clear when it says so. There are rarely AEDs in the wilds.

## When to stop
- They start to wake, move, open their eyes **and** breathe normally: put them in the [[a:recovery-position]] and monitor.
- Help arrives and takes over.
- You are too exhausted to continue.

## In the wilds
- Outdoors, far from help, CPR is unlikely to restart a heart unless an AED arrives. It is still worth doing while help comes. Drowning and hypothermia casualties have the best chance; for them give **5 rescue breaths first**.
- **Hypothermia:** continue CPR during long evacuations. "Nobody is dead until they are warm and dead."

- British Heart Foundation: How to do CPR (bhf.org.uk)

=== cpr-child
title: CPR for children and babies
summary: CPR for a child (1 year to puberty) or a baby (under 1): five rescue breaths first, then 30:2.
tags: CPR, child, baby, infant, resuscitation, rescue breaths
---
!quick
1. Unresponsive and not breathing normally → shout for help; ask someone to **call 999**. If you are alone, do **1 minute of CPR first**, then call.
2. Open the airway and give **5 rescue breaths**.
3. **Child:** one hand (or two), centre of chest, press **one third of chest depth (about 5 cm)**. **Baby:** two fingers, centre of chest, about **4 cm**.
4. **30 compressions : 2 breaths**, at 100–120 a minute. Continue until help arrives or they recover.
!end

## Child (1 year to puberty)
1. Tilt the head back gently and lift the chin. Pinch the nose and give 5 rescue breaths, watching the chest rise.
2. Place the heel of one hand in the centre of the chest. Press down a third of the depth of the chest, then release.
3. Give 30 compressions, then 2 breaths. Repeat.

## Baby (under 1 year)
1. Keep the head in a **neutral** position (don't tilt it back far). Seal your mouth over the baby's **mouth and nose** and give 5 gentle puffs.
2. Use two fingertips in the centre of the chest; press a third of the chest depth (about 4 cm).
3. Give 30 compressions, then 2 breaths. Repeat.

=== recovery-position
title: Recovery position
summary: How to keep the airway open in someone who is unresponsive but breathing normally.
tags: recovery position, unconscious, breathing, airway, vomit
---
!quick
1. Kneel beside them. Put the **near arm** out at a right angle.
2. Bring the **far arm** across the chest and hold the back of that hand against their near cheek.
3. Bend the **far knee** up and pull it towards you, rolling them onto their side.
4. Tilt the head back so the airway stays open. Call 999 and **check breathing** continually.
!end

## Details
- Adjust the upper leg so the hip and knee are at right angles: this stops them rolling.
- Keep them warm and insulated from the cold ground. Outdoors, put a mat, rucksack or dry bracken underneath **before** you roll them if you can.
- If they stop breathing normally, roll them onto their back and start [[a:cpr-adult]].
- Turn them to the other side every 30 minutes if waiting a long time.
- **Suspected spinal injury:** if the airway is clear and they are breathing, keep the head and neck still in line. Use the recovery position only if you have to (they are vomiting or you must leave them), and keep the head in line with the body as you roll.
- **Babies:** hold the baby face down along your forearm, head lower than the body, supporting the head.

## Sources
- St John Ambulance: Recovery position (sja.org.uk)
- NHS: Recovery position
- British Red Cross: Unresponsive and breathing

=== choking
title: Choking
summary: Coughing, back blows, abdominal thrusts. For adults, children and babies.
tags: choking, back blows, abdominal thrusts, Heimlich, airway obstruction
---
!quick
1. **Encourage coughing** if they can cough, speak or breathe.
2. If they can't: up to **5 back blows** between the shoulder blades (heel of hand), leaning them forward.
3. Then up to **5 abdominal thrusts**: fist above the belly button, pull sharply **in and up**. (Not for babies.)
4. Repeat. Call **999** if it hasn't cleared. If they become **unresponsive**, start [[a:cpr-adult]].
!end

## Adults and children over 1
- **Mild choking** (can cough, speak, breathe): encourage coughing. Don't slap their back.
- **Severe choking** (can't cough, speak or breathe):
  - Stand behind them, lean them forward, support the chest, and give up to 5 sharp back blows with the heel of your hand. Check the mouth between blows.
  - If still blocked: stand behind them, place a fist between the belly button and the bottom of the breastbone, grab the fist with your other hand, and pull sharply **inwards and upwards** up to 5 times.
  - Repeat back blows and abdominal thrusts. Call 999 if it hasn't cleared after 3 cycles.
- Anyone who has had abdominal thrusts must be checked by a doctor afterwards (internal injury is possible).

## Babies under 1
- Lay the baby face down along your thigh or forearm, supporting the head, and give up to 5 back blows.
- Then turn the baby face up and give up to 5 **chest thrusts**: two fingers in the centre of the chest, pushing down sharply. **Never** give abdominal thrusts to a baby.
- Call 999 if not cleared.

## Sources
- NHS: Choking (nhs.uk)
- St John Ambulance: Choking adult, child and baby (sja.org.uk)
- Resuscitation Council UK: Choking algorithm

=== severe-bleeding
title: Severe bleeding and tourniquets
summary: Stop life-threatening bleeding with firm direct pressure, packing, and, for limbs, a tourniquet.
tags: bleeding, haemorrhage, tourniquet, wound packing, pressure, catastrophic bleeding, blood
---
!quick
1. **Press hard** directly on the wound with a pad or cloth (or your hand). Don't let go.
2. **Call 999.** Lie them down.
3. Blood soaking through? Add another pad **on top** and press harder. Pack deep wounds tightly with cloth, then press.
4. **Life-threatening limb bleeding** that pressure can't control: apply a **tourniquet** 5–7 cm above the wound (not on a joint). Note the time. Never loosen it.
!end

## Direct pressure
- Put on gloves if you have them (or a plastic bag), but don't delay.
- Press firmly on the wound with a clean pad, your cotton cloth, or clothing, for at least **10 minutes** without peeking.
- For a deep wound with an opening (thigh, armpit, groin, neck): **pack** cloth firmly into the wound to fill it, then press on top with both hands.
- Secure with a firm bandage once it is controlled. Check fingers or toes beyond it stay pink and warm.
- If there is an **object embedded** in the wound, don't remove it: press either side of it and pad around it.

## Tourniquet (arms and legs only)
Use for life-threatening bleeding from a limb, such as an amputation or spurting blood that firm pressure cannot stop, or when you can't apply pressure (several casualties, you need your hands).
1. Place it **high and tight** on the limb, 5–7 cm above the wound, never over a joint.
2. Tighten until the **bleeding stops**. It will hurt; that is expected.
3. Note the time it went on (write it on the skin or tourniquet).
4. **Never loosen or remove it**; leave that to hospital. Add a second tourniquet above the first if bleeding continues.

### Improvised tourniquet
- A **wide** band (at least 4 cm: triangular bandage, folded cloth, belt). Thin cord cuts the skin and doesn't work.
- Tie it round the limb, put a strong stick (windlass) through the knot, twist until the bleeding stops, then tie the stick in place.
- Commercial tourniquets work far better. Training is strongly recommended.

## Then
- Treat for [[a:shock]]: lie them down, keep them warm and insulated, and give nothing to eat or drink.
- Watch for signs of shock: pale, cold, clammy skin, a fast weak pulse, fast breathing, confusion.

## Sources
- St John Ambulance: Severe bleeding (sja.org.uk)
- British Red Cross: Bleeding heavily
- NHS: Cuts and grazes
- UK Citizen Aid / Resuscitation Council UK guidance on catastrophic haemorrhage and tourniquets

=== shock
title: Shock
summary: Recognise and manage shock (dangerously low blood flow), most often caused by bleeding or burns.
tags: shock, blood loss, pale, clammy, faint, legs raised
---
!quick
1. Treat the cause: stop bleeding, cool burns.
2. **Lie them down**; raise and support the legs if no leg, pelvis or spinal injury.
3. **Keep them warm**: insulate underneath, cover them. Give nothing to eat or drink (moisten the lips only).
4. **Call 999** and monitor breathing and response.
!end

## Signs
- Pale, grey, cold and clammy skin; sweating.
- Fast, weak pulse; fast, shallow breathing.
- Thirst, nausea, dizziness, anxiety, restlessness, confusion, then drowsiness and collapse.

## What to do
- Loosen tight clothing at the neck, chest and waist.
- Reassure them and keep them still.
- Monitor breathing and level of response every few minutes. If they become unresponsive and stop breathing normally, start CPR.
- Don't let them walk about, and don't give food, drink or alcohol (they may need surgery).

## Sources
- St John Ambulance: Shock (sja.org.uk)
- British Red Cross: Shock
- NHS: First aid

=== burns
title: Burns and scalds
summary: Cool with running water for 20 minutes, cover loosely with cling film or a clean plastic bag, and know when it needs hospital.
tags: burns, scalds, fire, cool, cling film, blisters, chemical burn
---
!quick
1. **Cool the burn with cool or lukewarm water for 20 minutes** (pour clean water from a bottle if no tap or stream). No ice.
2. Remove jewellery and clothing near the burn, unless stuck to it.
3. **Cover loosely** with cling film, a clean plastic bag, or a clean non-fluffy cloth.
4. Keep the rest of the person **warm** (cooling a big burn can cause hypothermia). Call 999 for serious burns.
!end

## Call 999 / get to hospital for
- Burns larger than the person's palm, or deep burns (white, charred or leathery, or painless).
- Burns to the face, hands, feet, joints or genitals, or burns all the way round a limb.
- Electrical or chemical burns, burns with smoke inhalation (sooty mouth or nose, hoarse voice, coughing), and burns on children, older people, or anyone with other injuries.

## Don'ts
- Don't use ice, iced water, creams, butter or toothpaste.
- Don't burst blisters or peel off skin.
- Don't use fluffy dressings (cotton wool) that will stick.

## In the field
- Water is precious, but cooling the burn matters. Use a stream if it is clean, or pour slowly from a bottle. Boiled water must be cool.
- Change dressings only if they become wet or dirty; watch for infection (see [[a:wound-care]]).
- **Chemical burns:** brush off dry powder, then rinse with lots of water for at least 20 minutes.

## Sources
- NHS: Burns and scalds (nhs.uk)
- St John Ambulance: Burns (sja.org.uk)
- British Red Cross: Burns

=== fractures-splinting
title: Fractures and splinting
summary: Recognise a broken bone, support it, improvise a splint, and check circulation.
tags: fracture, broken bone, splint, sling, immobilise, open fracture
---
!quick
1. **Don't move** the injured part. Support it in the position you found it.
2. **Open fracture** (bone or wound over the break): cover the wound with a clean pad and press **around** the bone, not on it.
3. **Immobilise** with padding and a splint to the body or a rigid object, including the joints above and below.
4. Check **circulation** beyond the injury (colour, warmth, feeling) every 15 minutes. Call 999.
!end

## Signs of a fracture
- Pain, swelling, bruising; deformity or an unnatural angle; grating; inability to use the limb or put weight on it.
- If unsure, treat it as a fracture.

## Improvised splints
- **Arm:** support it in a sling made from a triangular bandage, a scarf, the bottom of a jacket pinned up, or a belt. Pad between the arm and the chest.
- **Lower leg:** tie the injured leg to the good leg with padding between (knees and ankles), using broad bands, avoiding the injury. Or use a rolled foam mat, sticks or trekking poles as splints.
- **Fingers:** "buddy strap" to the next finger with padding between.
- Pad all gaps and bony points with clothing, moss or grass.
- Tie knots on the uninjured side; tight enough to hold, loose enough to fit a finger under.

## Serious injuries: call 999 and don't move them
- **Suspected spinal injury** (fall from height, neck or back pain, tingling, weakness): keep the head and neck still and in line; only move them if they are in danger.
- **Thigh or pelvis fracture:** risk of major internal bleeding and shock. Keep them still, treat for [[a:shock]], keep them warm, and wait for rescue.

## Sources
- St John Ambulance: Broken bones and fractures (sja.org.uk)
- British Red Cross: Broken bones
- NHS: Broken arm or wrist; Broken leg

=== sprains-strains
title: Sprains and strains
summary: Rest, ice, compression, elevation, and getting moving safely afterwards.
tags: sprain, strain, ankle, PRICE, twisted ankle, swelling
---
!quick
1. **P**rotect and **R**est the injury.
2. **I**ce: wrap a cold pack (or cloth soaked in cold stream water) and apply 20 minutes every 2–3 hours.
3. **C**ompress with a bandage (firm, not tight) and **E**levate above the heart when resting.
4. Can't take any weight, or there's a deformity? Treat it as a [[a:fractures-splinting]].
!end

## Details
- For the first 3 days avoid **HARM**: Heat, Alcohol, Running (or activity), Massage. These increase swelling.
- A twisted ankle can often walk out with good boot support, tight lacing, and poles or a stick. But if you can't take four steps at all, assume it is broken.
- After 2–3 days, start gentle movement as pain allows.

## Sources
- NHS: Sprains and strains (nhs.uk)
- St John Ambulance: Strains and sprains

=== hypothermia
title: Hypothermia
summary: The UK's biggest outdoor killer: recognise the "umbles", stop heat loss, rewarm gently and call for help.
tags: hypothermia, cold, wet, wind, shivering, rewarm, umbles, exposure
---
!quick
1. **Stop heat loss now:** get out of the wind and wet; insulate from the ground; replace wet clothes with dry; cover the head.
2. **Wrap** them in dry layers, a sleeping bag, or a survival bag. Huddle to share body heat.
3. If **fully alert**: give warm, sweet drinks and food. **No alcohol.**
4. **Call 999** if they are confused, drowsy, or have stopped shivering. Handle gently, keep them lying flat, and don't rub limbs.
!end

!danger Cold and wet is the real UK killer
Hypothermia usually happens at 0–10 °C with **wind and rain**, not in deep snow. Wet clothing loses most of its insulation, and wind strips heat. See [[a:cold-and-wet]].
!end

## Recognise it early: the "umbles"
- **Mild:** shivering, cold pale skin, fumbles (clumsy hands), grumbles (irritable), mumbles.
- **Moderate:** stumbles, confusion, slurred speech, poor decisions, apathy, slow breathing. Shivering may become violent.
- **Severe:** **shivering stops**, drowsiness, unresponsiveness, a slow weak pulse. A life-threatening emergency.

## What to do
- Treat the whole group: if one person is hypothermic, others are probably cold too.
- Shelter: tarp, survival bag, the lee of a wall or boulder. Get them **off the ground** onto a mat, rucksack or bracken.
- Dry clothes on, wet off (one item at a time to limit exposure), hat and gloves on.
- Warm (not hot) drinks and high-energy food if they can swallow safely.
- **Warm the core, not the limbs:** warm water bottles wrapped in cloth in the armpits and on the chest (not directly on the skin), or body-to-body contact inside a sleeping bag.

## Severe hypothermia
- Call 999. **Handle very gently** and keep them horizontal: rough movement can trigger cardiac arrest.
- Check breathing for a full minute (it may be very slow). If not breathing, start [[a:cpr-adult]].
- Don't give drinks to anyone who is drowsy. Don't rub limbs, apply direct heat or put them in a hot bath.

## Prevention
- Layers, a hat, a waterproof and windproof shell; eat and drink regularly; turn back early in bad weather. See [[a:layering]].

## Sources
- NHS: Hypothermia (nhs.uk)
- St John Ambulance: Hypothermia (sja.org.uk)
- Mountain Rescue England and Wales: Hypothermia advice
- Royal Life Saving Society UK / RNLI: Cold water shock

=== cold-injuries
title: Frostnip, frostbite and trench foot
summary: Cold injuries to fingers, toes, ears and feet, including non-freezing "trench foot" from days of wet boots.
tags: frostbite, frostnip, trench foot, non-freezing cold injury, feet, cold
---
!quick
1. **Frostnip** (white, numb skin): rewarm with body heat: hands in armpits, feet on a friend's belly.
2. **Frostbite** (hard, waxy, white or grey): don't rub it; don't rewarm it if it might refreeze. Get medical help.
3. **Trench foot** (cold, wet, numb, swollen feet for many hours): dry the feet, elevate, warm slowly, and keep them dry.
!end

## Frostbite
- Rewarm only when there is no chance of refreezing (refreezing causes much worse damage). The best way is warm water at about 37–39 °C, but only in shelter with help nearby.
- Don't rub with snow, don't use direct heat (fire, stove), and don't burst blisters. Painkillers help, as rewarming is very painful.
- Protect the area with loose dry dressings between the fingers or toes.

## Non-freezing cold injury (trench foot)
Common in wet UK conditions after hours or days of cold, wet feet.
- Signs: cold, numb, white or blotchy feet, then red, swollen and painful after warming.
- Prevent it by drying feet each night, changing to dry socks, airing boots, and never sleeping in wet socks.

## Sources
- NHS: Frostbite (nhs.uk)
- British Mountaineering Council: Cold injuries
- Ministry of Defence guidance on non-freezing cold injury (public summaries)

=== heat-illness
title: Heat exhaustion and heatstroke
summary: Recognise and cool heat exhaustion, and spot heatstroke, which is a 999 emergency.
tags: heat exhaustion, heatstroke, hot weather, dehydration, cooling, sun
---
!quick
1. Move them to a **cool, shaded** place. Lie them down with the feet slightly raised.
2. Give plenty of water (or rehydration drink: [[a:diarrhoea-ors]]).
3. **Cool the skin:** wet it with water, fan it; wrap cold wet cloths around the neck and armpits.
4. Not better within **30 minutes**, or confused, not sweating, fitting or unresponsive → **heatstroke: call 999** and keep cooling.
!end

## Heat exhaustion signs
Headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, loss of appetite, heavy sweating with pale clammy skin, cramps in the arms, legs or stomach, fast breathing or pulse, a temperature of 38 °C or more, and intense thirst.

## Heatstroke signs (emergency)
Hot skin that may be **dry** (sweating stops), confusion, loss of coordination, seizures, unresponsiveness, and a temperature above 40 °C.

## Prevention
Rest in the shade from 11:00 to 15:00, drink regularly, wear a hat, and travel early and late in the day. Long, hard days on UK hills in summer cause real cases.

## Sources
- NHS: Heat exhaustion and heatstroke (nhs.uk)
- St John Ambulance: Heat exhaustion; Heatstroke

=== wound-care
title: Cleaning wounds and spotting infection
summary: How to clean and dress minor wounds outdoors, and the warning signs of infection and sepsis.
tags: wound, cut, graze, infection, sepsis, clean, dressing, tetanus
---
!quick
1. Stop the bleeding with pressure first.
2. **Irrigate** with plenty of clean water (boiled and cooled), pouring it hard over and into the wound.
3. Remove visible dirt with clean tweezers. Cover with a clean dressing; change it daily or when wet.
4. Watch for infection: **spreading redness, swelling, heat, pus, red streaks, fever**. Sepsis signs mean **999**.
!end

## Cleaning
- Wash your hands. Use **boiled, cooled water** ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]). A pierced bottle cap or plastic bag with a pinhole makes a pressure jet.
- Clean around the wound with soap and water if you have them; keep soap out of the wound itself.
- Don't close dirty or deep wounds with tape; let them drain, covered. Small clean cuts can be closed with adhesive strips.
- Animal bites and deep puncture wounds have a high infection risk: get them seen by a doctor.

## Signs of infection
- Increasing pain, swelling, warmth and redness spreading out from the wound.
- Pus or a bad smell; **red streaks** tracking up the limb; swollen glands; feeling hot and shivery.

## Sepsis: call 999
Sepsis can follow any infection. Call 999 if someone has: slurred speech, confusion; extreme shivering or muscle pain; passing no urine all day; severe breathlessness; feeling that they are going to die; skin that is mottled, bluish or pale.

## Tetanus
Tetanus lives in soil. Anyone with a dirty wound who isn't sure they have had 5 tetanus doses should get medical advice when they can.

## Sources
- NHS: Cuts and grazes; Sepsis; Tetanus (nhs.uk)
- St John Ambulance: Cuts and grazes
- UK Sepsis Trust: Signs of sepsis (sepsistrust.org)

=== head-injury
title: Head injuries
summary: Which head injuries need 999, how to watch someone after a knock, and the danger signs.
tags: head injury, concussion, knocked out, skull, confusion, vomiting
---
!quick
1. **Call 999** if they were knocked out, are confused, drowsy, vomiting, have a seizure, clear fluid or blood from the nose or ears, weakness, or problems with speech or vision.
2. Assume a **neck injury** too after a fall: keep the head and neck still.
3. Minor knock: rest, cold compress, and **watch them closely for 24 hours**. Never leave them alone.
!end

## Watching someone after a head injury
- Check every 15–30 minutes at first: can they answer simple questions (name, where they are, what day it is)?
- Danger signs: worsening headache, repeated vomiting, increasing drowsiness, confusion, unequal pupils, weakness on one side, and fits.

## Scalp wounds
These bleed heavily. Press firmly with a pad; don't press on an obvious dent or soft area in the skull. Press around it.

- St John Ambulance: Head injury

=== adder-bite
title: Adder bite
summary: The UK's only venomous snake: what to do, what not to do, and why it still needs hospital.
tags: adder, snake bite, venom, viper, swelling
---
!quick
1. **Call 999** (or 112). Most bites are not life-threatening but everyone needs hospital assessment.
2. Keep the person **still and calm**; keep the bitten limb still and roughly at heart level.
3. **Remove rings, watches and tight clothing** near the bite before it swells.
4. **Don't** cut, suck, apply ice, a tourniquet, or a tight bandage. Note the time and the snake's appearance; don't try to catch it.
!end

## Signs
- Pain, swelling and bruising spreading from the bite; nausea, vomiting, faintness; in severe cases, swelling of the lips and face, difficulty breathing, and collapse (anaphylaxis or severe envenoming).
- Up to half of bites inject little venom, but swelling can take hours to develop: don't be reassured early.

## While waiting
- Carry the person, or have them walk slowly only if there is no other choice.
- Monitor breathing and response. If they collapse and stop breathing normally, start CPR.
- Paracetamol is fine for pain. Avoid aspirin and ibuprofen (they increase bleeding).

## Prevention
- Wear boots and long trousers on heaths in spring and summer; watch where you put your hands; leave snakes alone. See [[s:adder]].

- St John Ambulance: Snake bites
- Froglife / Amphibian and Reptile Conservation: Adders

=== tick-removal-lyme
title: Ticks and Lyme disease
summary: Remove ticks correctly and recognise the rash and symptoms of Lyme disease.
tags: tick, Lyme disease, borrelia, rash, bullseye, deer, removal
---
!quick
1. **Check your whole body** daily after being in long grass, bracken or woodland (backs of knees, groin, waist, armpits, hairline, behind ears).
2. Remove ticks ASAP with **fine-tipped tweezers or a tick tool**: grip **as close to the skin as possible** and pull **steadily upwards**. Don't squeeze the body or burn it.
3. Clean the bite. Note the date.
4. **See a doctor** if a spreading red rash or flu-like illness appears within a few weeks.
!end

## Lyme disease
- A **circular red rash** spreading out from the bite, sometimes like a bullseye, appearing 3–30 days after the bite. It can be faint or look like a bruise. It is not usually itchy or painful.
- Flu-like symptoms: fever, headache, aches, tiredness.
- Treated with antibiotics. The earlier, the better.

## Prevention
- Long trousers tucked into socks; light colours make ticks easier to see.
- Walk in the middle of paths; avoid sitting in bracken and long grass.
- Prompt removal (within 24 hours) greatly reduces the risk of infection.

=== stings-anaphylaxis
title: Stings, bites and anaphylaxis
summary: Treat bee and wasp stings and insect bites, and recognise a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis).
tags: sting, bee, wasp, hornet, allergy, anaphylaxis, adrenaline, EpiPen, swelling
---
!quick
1. **Bee sting:** scrape the sting out sideways with a fingernail or card.
2. Wash, apply a **cold compress** for 10 minutes, raise the area, and take painkillers or an antihistamine.
3. **Anaphylaxis** (swollen lips, tongue or throat, wheeze, faintness, collapse, rash) → **call 999**, use their **adrenaline auto-injector** in the outer thigh, and lie them down with legs raised (or sit them up if breathing is difficult).
4. No improvement after 5 minutes → give a **second** adrenaline dose if available. Start CPR if they stop breathing.
!end

## Normal reactions
Pain, redness and swelling up to several centimetres, lasting a few days. Don't scratch. Seek advice if the swelling spreads widely, there are signs of infection, or the sting is in the mouth or throat.

## Anaphylaxis
- Starts within minutes. Signs: swelling of the lips, face or throat, difficulty breathing or swallowing, wheeze, a hoarse voice, fast heartbeat, dizziness, clammy skin, confusion, collapse; often a raised itchy rash.
- Do not make them stand or walk. Remove the trigger if possible.
- Stings to the mouth or throat can swell and block the airway even without allergy: call 999.

=== poisoning
title: Poisoning from plants or fungi
summary: What to do if someone may have eaten a poisonous plant, berry or mushroom. Keep a sample; don't wait for symptoms.
tags: poisoning, mushroom, fungi, berries, plants, toxic, sample, 999, 111, hemlock, death cap
---
!quick
1. **Call 999** if they have symptoms (vomiting, drowsiness, fits, breathing difficulty, irregular heartbeat), or if a **deadly species** might be involved, even with no symptoms. Otherwise call **NHS 111**.
2. **Don't** make them vomit. Rinse the mouth; give sips of water only.
3. **Keep a sample**: the whole plant or mushroom (with its base), leftovers, or vomit, in a paper bag or cloth. Photograph it.
4. Note **what, how much, and when** it was eaten. Watch breathing and response.
!end

## Why not wait?
- **Deadly fungi act late.** [[s:death-cap]], [[s:destroying-angel]] and [[s:funeral-bell]] cause no symptoms for 6–24 hours, then vomiting and diarrhoea, then apparent recovery while the liver fails. Webcaps ([[s:deadly-webcap]]) cause kidney failure **days** later.
- Plant poisons such as [[s:hemlock-water-dropwort]], [[s:hemlock]], [[s:yew]], [[s:foxglove]] and [[s:monkshood]] can cause seizures, paralysis or cardiac arrest within an hour or two.
- Hospitals can get specialist advice from the National Poisons Information Service only if they know what was eaten. **The sample matters.**

## If they become unresponsive
- Breathing: put them in the [[a:recovery-position]], and check breathing continually.
- Not breathing: CPR. Use a face shield or cloth for rescue breaths if the poison may be on their lips.

## Skin and eye contact
- Sap from [[s:giant-hogweed]] or [[s:common-hogweed]]: wash with soap and water, and keep the skin covered from sunlight for 48 hours.
- Eyes: rinse with clean water for at least 10 minutes.

- NHS: Poisoning (nhs.uk)
- National Poisons Information Service / TOXBASE public guidance
- St John Ambulance: Poisoning
- British Mycological Society: Fungi and safety

=== blisters
title: Blisters and foot care
summary: Prevent and treat blisters, which can turn a walk-out into a crawl.
tags: blisters, feet, hot spots, boots, socks, plaster
---
!quick
1. **Stop at the first hot spot.** Cover it with tape or a plaster before it blisters.
2. **Don't burst** a blister. Cover it with a padded dressing or plaster with a hole cut around it.
3. If it bursts: let it drain, **leave the skin on**, clean it, and cover it.
4. Watch for infection (spreading redness, pus, heat).
!end

## Prevention
- Dry, well-fitting boots worn in; wool or synthetic socks (not cotton). Change wet socks.
- Keep toenails short. Remove grit at once.
- Dry your feet and air them at rest stops; tape known trouble spots before walking.

## If a large tense blister stops you walking
Draining it with a clean (flame-sterilised, cooled) needle at the edge may let you walk out, but it raises the risk of infection. Leave the roof of skin in place and cover it.

=== diarrhoea-ors
title: Diarrhoea, vomiting and oral rehydration (recipe)
summary: Prevent dehydration with a homemade oral rehydration solution, and know the warning signs.
tags: diarrhoea, vomiting, oral rehydration, ORS, recipe, salt, sugar, dehydration, gastro
---
!quick
1. **Drink** small, frequent sips. Use oral rehydration solution if you can.
2. **Homemade ORS:** 1 litre of boiled, cooled water + **6 level teaspoons of sugar** + **½ level teaspoon of salt**. Stir until dissolved. Taste: no saltier than tears.
3. Rest; eat plain food when you can.
4. Get help if there is **blood** in the stool, high fever, signs of severe dehydration, or it lasts more than a few days.
!end

## Notes on the recipe
- Measure carefully: too much salt is dangerous, especially for children. Make it fresh each day.
- Sip it steadily: a cupful after each loose stool for adults; smaller, frequent sips for children.
- If vomiting, wait 10 minutes and then try small sips again (a teaspoon every minute or two).

## Prevention outdoors
- Boil **all** water from wild sources ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]), wash hands after the toilet and before food, and keep cooking pots clean.
- Toilet at least 30 m from water. Dig a cat hole 15 cm deep where possible.

## Danger signs: get help
- Very little or no urine, very dark urine, dizziness, confusion, a fast heartbeat; blood or black stool; severe abdominal pain; high fever; symptoms lasting more than 7 days (adults), or a child who is floppy, drowsy or not drinking.

- NHS: Diarrhoea and vomiting; Dehydration (nhs.uk)
- World Health Organization: Oral rehydration salts guidance
- CDC: Rehydration (cdc.gov)

=== heart-attack-stroke
title: Heart attack and stroke
summary: Recognise a heart attack and a stroke (FAST) and act immediately.
tags: heart attack, chest pain, aspirin, stroke, FAST, angina
---
!quick
1. **Heart attack:** chest pain or pressure, perhaps spreading to the arms, jaw, neck or back, with breathlessness, sweating or nausea → **call 999**.
2. Sit them down and lean them back in a comfortable position (often half-sitting, knees bent).
3. Give **one 300 mg aspirin** to chew slowly, unless they are allergic or under 16.
4. **Stroke, FAST:** **F**ace drooping, **A**rm weakness, **S**peech slurred, **T**ime to call 999.
!end

## Heart attack
- Keep them calm and still. Loosen tight clothing. If they have angina medicine (a GTN spray), help them use it.
- Monitor breathing. If they become unresponsive and stop breathing normally, start [[a:cpr-adult]].

## Stroke
- Note the time symptoms started: it matters for treatment.
- Lie them down with the head and shoulders slightly raised; don't give food or drink (swallowing may be affected).

=== seizures
title: Seizures (fits)
summary: Keep the person safe during a seizure, time it, and know when to call 999.
tags: seizure, fit, epilepsy, convulsion
---
!quick
1. **Protect** them from injury: move hazards away, cushion the head. **Don't** restrain them or put anything in the mouth.
2. **Time** the seizure.
3. When the shaking stops, put them in the [[a:recovery-position]] and check breathing.
4. **Call 999** if it is their first seizure, it lasts over **5 minutes**, another follows, they are injured, or they don't recover fully.
!end

## Notes
- Stay with them, and talk calmly as they come round. They may be confused or sleepy.
- Seizures can follow head injury, low blood sugar, heatstroke, or poisoning (for example [[s:hemlock-water-dropwort]]).

=== asthma-attack
title: Asthma attack
summary: Help someone with an asthma attack using their reliever inhaler, and know when it's a 999 emergency.
tags: asthma, inhaler, breathing difficulty, wheeze
---
!quick
1. Sit them **upright**, keep calm; loosen tight clothing.
2. One puff of the **reliever inhaler (usually blue)** every 30–60 seconds, up to **10 puffs**.
3. Not improving, or worse at any point → **call 999**. If waiting longer than 15 minutes, repeat step 2.
!end

## Signs of a severe attack
Too breathless to speak, eat or sleep; blue lips; rapid breathing; the reliever isn't helping; exhaustion or drowsiness.

## Outdoors
Cold air, exertion, smoke and pollen are common triggers. Anyone with asthma should carry a reliever inhaler in an accessible pocket.

=== eye-injury
title: Eye injuries and grit in the eye
summary: Rinse out grit and chemicals, protect penetrating injuries, and know when to get help.
tags: eye, grit, chemical, splinter, smoke
---
!quick
1. **Grit:** blink; rinse with clean water poured from the inner corner outwards; don't rub.
2. **Chemicals or sap:** rinse continuously with clean water for at least **10–20 minutes**. Get help.
3. **Something stuck in the eye:** don't remove it. Cover both eyes lightly to stop eye movement, and get urgent help.

## Notes
- A blow to the eye: apply a cold compress for 10 minutes. Seek help for changes in vision, blood inside the eye, or severe pain.
- Smoky campfires cause sore eyes: sit upwind, and rinse with clean water.

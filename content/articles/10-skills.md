=== bowline
title: Bowline
summary: A fixed loop at the end of a rope that won't slip or jam, and unties easily, for tying round a tree, a person or a post.
tags: bowline, knot, loop, fixed loop, rescue, rope
wiki: Bowline
icon: knot
---
!quick
1. Make a small **overhand loop** (the "rabbit hole") in the standing part, with the working end lying **on top**.
2. Pass the working end **up through the loop** ("the rabbit comes out of the hole").
3. Take it **round behind the standing part** ("round the tree").
4. Bring it **back down through the small loop** ("back down the hole"), and pull tight by holding the end and big loop against the standing part.
!end

## Notes
- The size of the big loop is set before tightening, and doesn't change under load.
- It can shake loose when **not** under load (e.g. a flapping tarp guy). Leave a long tail and tie it off with a stopper knot (an overhand round the loop).
- It is easy to untie after heavy loading: push the "collar" forwards to break it.
- Uses: tying a line round a tree for a ridgeline, a loop for hauling a pack, a rescue loop round the chest (under the arms).

## Sources
- The Ashley Book of Knots (ABOK #1010)
- Animated Knots / Royal Navy and Scouts UK knot guides

=== clove-hitch
title: Clove hitch
summary: A quick, adjustable hitch for tying a rope to a pole or post, and the starting knot for lashings.
tags: clove hitch, hitch, knot, pole, lashing, ridgeline
wiki: Clove hitch
icon: knot
---
!quick
1. Pass the working end **round the pole**.
2. Cross it **over itself** and take it round the pole again.
3. Tuck the end **under the last turn** (between the second turn and the pole).
4. Pull both ends tight. The two turns should sit side by side with an X on top.
!end

## Notes
- Quick to tie and easy to adjust, but it can slip or roll off under a changing load. Add two half hitches for security.
- The standard start and finish for square and diagonal lashings (e.g. building a shelter frame).
- Can be tied "in the bight" and dropped over a post end.

## Sources
- The Ashley Book of Knots (ABOK #1245)
- Scouts UK: Knots and lashings

=== taut-line-hitch
title: Taut-line hitch
summary: An adjustable loop for guy lines and tarp tie-outs: slide it to tension, and it holds under load.
tags: taut-line hitch, tautline, adjustable, guy line, tarp, tent, tension, rolling hitch
wiki: Taut-line hitch
icon: knot
---
!quick
1. Pass the rope round the peg or tree, bringing the working end back alongside the standing part.
2. Make **two turns** round the standing part, working **back towards the anchor** (inside the loop).
3. Make **one more turn** round the standing part, **outside** the loop (further from the anchor).
4. Pull snug. Slide the knot up or down to adjust the tension; it grips under load.
!end

## Notes
- Use it on every tarp and shelter guy line so you can re-tension in wind and rain (cordage stretches when wet).
- If it slips on slippery cord, add an extra turn inside the loop.

## Sources
- The Ashley Book of Knots (ABOK #1857, adjustable hitch)
- Scouts UK and Animated Knots

=== truckers-hitch
title: Trucker's hitch
summary: A pulley-like knot with roughly 3:1 mechanical advantage for drum-tight ridgelines and lashings.
tags: trucker's hitch, truckers hitch, ridgeline, tension, mechanical advantage, tarp
wiki: Trucker's hitch
icon: knot
---
!quick
1. Tie the rope to the first tree. Partway along the line, make a **fixed loop** (a slipped overhand or an alpine butterfly).
2. Pass the working end **round the second tree** (or anchor) and back **through the loop**.
3. **Pull** the working end: the loop acts as a pulley and tightens the line.
4. Pinch to hold the tension, and finish with **two half hitches** round the line (or a slipped half hitch for quick release).
!end

## Notes
- The best way to get a ridgeline tight enough not to sag under a wet tarp.
- A slipped-overhand loop can jam or capsize: an alpine butterfly is more secure.
- Friction at the loop wears thin cord; re-tie in a fresh spot occasionally.

## Sources
- The Ashley Book of Knots
- Animated Knots: Trucker's hitch

=== sheet-bend
title: Sheet bend
summary: Join two ropes, even of different thickness or stiffness, with a secure knot that's easy to untie.
tags: sheet bend, bend, join ropes, different thickness, knot
wiki: Sheet bend
icon: knot
---
!quick
1. Make a **bight** (U-shaped bend) in the **thicker** rope.
2. Pass the **thinner** rope **up through** the bight from underneath.
3. Take it **round behind both legs** of the bight.
4. Tuck it **under itself** (not under the bight). Pull tight. Both short ends should be on the **same side**.
!end

## Notes
- For very different ropes or slippery cord, use a **double sheet bend**: take the thin rope round the bight twice before tucking.
- It can loosen when not under load: leave long tails.
- Use it to join cordage lengths, or tie a cord to the corner of a tarp or cloth.

## Sources
- The Ashley Book of Knots (ABOK #1431)
- Scouts UK: Knots

=== prusik
title: Prusik knot
summary: A friction hitch that grips a rope when loaded and slides when not. Used for tarp tensioning and climbing a rope.
tags: prusik, friction hitch, loop, ridgeline, tarp, climbing, rescue
wiki: Prusik
icon: knot
---
!quick
1. Use a **loop of thinner cord** (tie the ends with a double fisherman's knot) on a thicker rope.
2. Lay the loop behind the main rope and pass it **through itself**, wrapping round the rope **three times**.
3. Dress the wraps neatly, **parallel**, with the loop coming out of the centre.
4. Load it: it grips. Unload and push the knot: it slides.
!end

## Notes
- The thinner the loop cord compared to the rope, the better it grips. Add wraps if it slips (wet or icy rope).
- Put one on each side of a tarp's ridgeline, clip or tie the tarp corners to them, and slide them outwards to tension the tarp.
- In real rope rescue it is used for ascending a rope or as a backup. Get training before trusting it with your life.

## Sources
- The Ashley Book of Knots
- British Mountaineering Council: Prusik and friction hitches

=== cordage-nettle-bramble
title: Cordage from nettle and bramble
summary: Make strong string from nettle stems and bramble bark by stripping the fibres and reverse-wrapping them.
tags: cordage, string, rope, nettle, bramble, fibre, reverse wrap, twist, lime bast, willow
icon: spool
---
!quick
1. **Nettle:** cut tall autumn stems, strip off the leaves (gloves), crush the stem flat, split it open and peel the outer fibre from the woody core. Dry it.
2. **Bramble:** de-thorn long stems (gloves; run a knife back down the stem), split them in four, and scrape away the pith to leave the green-bark fibre. Dry it.
3. **Reverse-wrap:** twist a fibre bundle until it kinks; twist each half **away from you**, then wrap them **towards you**, over each other, repeatedly.
4. Splice in new fibres as you go, staggering the joins.
!end

## Reverse wrapping step by step
1. Take a bundle of fibres and fold it so one side is longer than the other (to stagger the splices).
2. Pinch the fold. Twist the upper strand away from you (clockwise), then bring it over the lower strand towards you.
3. Twist the new upper strand away from you, then bring it over towards you. Repeat. The twists lock against each other.
4. When a strand gets short, lay a new bundle of fibres alongside it and keep twisting them in together.

## Other UK fibres
- [[s:lime-tree]] inner bark (soaked for weeks): excellent strong bast.
- [[s:willow]] inner bark; [[s:honeysuckle]] and [[s:clematis]] stems for rough binding.
- Long grasses and reed leaves: weaker, but quick.

## Uses
Lashings, fishing line, snare-free tasks such as hanging food, sewing repairs, bow drills.

- [[s:nettle]] and [[s:blackberry]] entries in this guide

=== knife-sharpening
title: Sharpening a blade
summary: Keep a knife sharp with stones, a strop or improvised river stones: holding a consistent angle is everything.
tags: sharpening, knife, whetstone, strop, edge, burr, angle, honing
icon: pocket-knife
---
!quick
1. Hold a **consistent angle** (about **20°** each side for a general knife: roughly a coin's thickness under the spine of a small blade).
2. Push or circle the edge along the stone, **coarse first, then fine**, until you can feel a **burr** along the whole edge on the other side.
3. Repeat on the other side, then **alternate light strokes** to remove the burr.
4. Finish by **stropping** (edge-trailing strokes) on leather, a belt, cardboard or [[s:birch-polypore]].
!end

## Improvised sharpening
- **River stones:** fine-grained sandstone or slate. Wet them, and use the flattest face. Coarse granite only for repairing damage.
- **The unglazed ring on the base of a mug**, or the edge of a glass jar.
- **Strop:** a leather belt, the back of a notepad, smooth wood with fine ash or charcoal rubbed in.

## Care
- Dry and lightly oil carbon steel blades to stop rust. Don't use a knife as a pry bar.
- Clean blades after cutting food; acidic fruit stains carbon steel.

=== knife-safety
title: Knife safety and the law
summary: Safe cutting technique for bushcraft, and UK knife law: what you can carry and when.
tags: knife, safety, law, carrying, blood circle, cutting, folding knife
icon: shield-check
---
!quick
1. **Blood circle:** before cutting, check no one is within arm's reach plus a knife's length.
2. **Cut away from yourself**, with the work to the side of your body, not between your legs.
3. **Sheathe it** when not in use. Pass it handle first.
4. **UK law:** carrying a fixed blade, or a locking or folding blade over 3 inches, in public needs a **good reason** (e.g. on your way to bushcraft or work).
!end

## Safe techniques
- Kneel with your knee on the ground and the work to the side, so a slipping blade goes into the ground, not your leg (the femoral artery is on the inside of the thigh).
- Use the "chest lever" and "knee lever" grips for control; take small controlled cuts.
- Keep the blade sharp: blunt knives need force, and slip.

## UK law (England and Wales; similar rules in Scotland and Northern Ireland)
- It is legal to carry a **non-locking folding knife** with a cutting edge of **3 inches (7.62 cm) or less** without a reason.
- Any other knife in public needs a **good reason** (work, a planned bushcraft activity, etc.). Some types are banned outright.
- On private land with permission, using knives for bushcraft is fine.

=== tarp-rigging
title: Tarp rigging
summary: Set up a tarp fast and tight: ridgeline, prusiks, guy lines, and the best pitches for UK weather.
tags: tarp, ridgeline, pitch, A-frame, lean-to, diamond, guy lines, shelter, rigging
icon: tent
---
!quick
1. Tie a **ridgeline** between two trees about 3–4 m apart: [[a:bowline]] on one end, [[a:truckers-hitch]] on the other.
2. Hang the tarp with [[a:prusik]] loops so you can slide and tension it.
3. Guy out the sides with [[a:taut-line-hitch|taut-line hitches]] and pegs (sharpened sticks) at 45°.
4. In wind and rain, **pitch low**, with the back or a corner into the wind.
!end

## Pitches
| Pitch | Use |
|---|---|
| **A-frame** | All-round weather protection: [[a:tarp-a-frame]] |
| **Lean-to** | Open to a fire, back to the wind: [[a:lean-to]] |
| **Diamond / plough point** | One corner to a tree, others pegged: fast, wind-shedding |
| **Low wedge** | Storm pitch: steep, low, one end pegged to the ground |

## Tips
- Re-tension after an hour: cordage stretches, especially when wet.
- Drip lines on the ridgeline, and a gutter dug downhill of the shelter, keep you dry.
- Keep fires well away from the tarp edge: sparks melt nylon.

- Scouts UK: Shelters

=== batteries-electronics
title: Keeping the phone and batteries alive
summary: Make this phone and the arc lighter last: cold, charging, storage at half charge, and saving power.
tags: battery, phone, lithium, cold, charging, 50%, storage, power saving
icon: battery-charging
---
!quick
1. **Keep batteries warm:** in an inside pocket by day, in your sleeping bag at night. Cold makes lithium batteries die early (they recover when warmed).
2. **Don't charge below 0 °C** or in hot sun; charge at room or body temperature.
3. **Long storage: about 50%** charge, cool and dry. Top up every few months.
4. **Save power:** dim screen (Settings → brightness), Low-power mode, dark theme, phone off between uses.
!end

## This phone
- 5,000 mAh battery. Off between uses, it will hold charge for many weeks. Check it monthly.
- The phone is **not waterproof**: keep it in a sealed plastic bag when outdoors.
- The backlight is the biggest drain. Use the guide's brightness slider, and Low-power mode to hide photos.
- Keep airplane mode on: radios searching for a network are a constant drain, and this phone has no SIM to use them.

## Lithium-ion care
- Avoid keeping it at 100% for long periods, or letting it sit at 0%.
- Heat (dashboards, direct sun, near the fire) damages batteries permanently. Swelling means it is damaged: stop using it.
- If the phone gets wet: switch it off, dry it, and don't charge it for 24–48 hours.

=== whistle-and-sound
title: Making noise to be found
summary: Using a whistle, banging and voice to help searchers find you, and saving energy while doing it.
tags: whistle, sound, signal, voice, rescue, searchers
icon: volume-2
---
!quick
1. A **whistle** carries far further than a voice and costs little energy. Six blasts, pause a minute, repeat.
2. Improvise: bang the steel pot with a stone; blow across a bottle neck.
3. When searchers shout, **reply**, then wait and listen.
!end

## Notes
- Sound carries along valleys and over water; wind blows it away from searchers upwind.
- Save your voice: shouting quickly exhausts you and causes dehydration.

=== sewing-repairs
title: Field repairs: clothing, kit and boots
summary: Simple repairs to keep clothing and gear working: stitching, patching, tape, and improvised fixes.
tags: repair, sewing, stitching, patch, boots, tape, gear repair
icon: scissors
---
!quick
1. **Stitch tears early**, with a few big stitches, before they spread.
2. **Tape** (duct tape, wrapped round a bottle or pole) fixes tarps, boots and jackets temporarily. Round the corners so it doesn't peel.
3. **Boots:** tie a broken sole on with cord wrapped under the foot; replace laces with cordage.
4. Dry and air gear whenever possible; damp kit rots and fails.
!end

## Notes
- A thorn or splinter of bone can serve as an awl; nettle fibre or dental floss as thread.
- Waterproofs: patch from inside and outside; keep them clean.

- Scouts UK: Camp skills

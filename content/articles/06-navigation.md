# Navigation. Ordnance Survey maps, compass, finding north, pacing, being found.

=== map-compass-basics
title: Map and compass basics
summary: Set the map, read the key features, and use a baseplate compass to take and follow a bearing.
tags: map, compass, bearing, orienteering, OS map, contours, setting the map, baseplate
wiki: Orienteering compass
---
!quick
1. **Set the map:** turn it so north on the map points to north on the compass needle. Now everything on the map lines up with the ground.
2. **Take a bearing:** put the compass edge from **you** to **where you want to go**, turn the dial so its N lines up with the map's **grid north** lines, then read the number at the index.
3. **Follow it:** hold the compass flat, turn **yourself** until the red needle sits in the red "shed", and walk where the direction-of-travel arrow points.
4. Pick a landmark on that line, walk to it, and repeat.
!end

## Reading OS maps
- **Scales:** 1:25 000 (Explorer, orange): 4 cm = 1 km, so each grid square is 1 km. 1:50 000 (Landranger, pink): 2 cm = 1 km.
- **Contour lines** join points of equal height, usually every 10 m (5 m on some 1:25 000 maps). **Close together = steep.** Numbers are written so the top of the number faces uphill.
- Learn the key: footpaths (green dashes on Explorer), field boundaries, crags, streams, woods, buildings, and bothies.

## Compass parts
- **Baseplate** with a direction-of-travel arrow. **Dial** (housing) with degrees and orienting lines. **Magnetic needle**: red end points to magnetic north.

## Magnetic variation
- Grid north (on the map), true north and magnetic north differ slightly. In Britain in the 2020s the difference is small (a couple of degrees at most). It is printed on the map margin and changes each year.
- For walking short legs it can usually be ignored; for long legs in fog, adjust as the map margin says.
- Keep the compass away from metal, phones and magnets: they deflect the needle.

=== grid-references
title: Reading OS grid references
summary: How to give a precise UK location from an OS map: the 2 letters, then "along the corridor and up the stairs".
tags: grid reference, OS grid, eastings, northings, location, map reference, 6-figure, 8-figure
wiki: Ordnance Survey National Grid
---
!quick
1. Find the **two letters** of the 100 km square (printed in the map corners and margins), e.g. **NY**.
2. **Eastings first** ("along the corridor"): the number of the vertical line to the **left** of your point, then tenths across the square.
3. **Northings second** ("up the stairs"): the number of the horizontal line **below** your point, then tenths up the square.
4. Say it as: **NY 215 072**. 6 figures = within 100 m; 8 figures = within 10 m.
!end

## Worked example
- Your position is in the square whose bottom-left corner is at easting line **21** and northing line **07**.
- You are about **5 tenths** across the square from line 21 → **215**.
- You are about **2 tenths** up from line 07 → **072**.
- With the letters: **NY 215 072**.

## Tips
- Read the letters off the map edge: they matter, because the same numbers occur in every 100 km square.
- Romer scales on compasses help measure the tenths precisely.
- Repeat it slowly to the operator, letter by letter ("November Yankee, two one five, zero seven two").
- If you can't, give a description: the last feature you passed, and your direction and time since.

=== walking-on-bearing
title: Walking on a bearing in poor visibility
summary: Hold a compass bearing in fog or darkness using leapfrogging, landmarks, and pace counting.
tags: bearing, fog, mist, whiteout, compass, leapfrog, poor visibility
---
!quick
1. Take the bearing carefully off the map; set it on the compass.
2. Hold the compass flat and close in front of you, with **the needle in the shed**.
3. Pick something on the line (rock, tussock), walk to it, and repeat. In thick fog, send one person ahead to the limit of sight and line them up.
4. **Count paces** to know how far you've gone ([[a:naismith-pacing]]).
!end

## Tips
- Trust the compass more than your feelings; people drift in fog.
- Short legs between definite features are much easier than long legs.
- **Aiming off:** aim deliberately to one side of a target on a linear feature (e.g. a stream junction), so you know which way to turn when you hit it.

=== finding-north
title: Finding north without a compass
summary: Use the sun, a shadow stick, a watch, the Plough and Polaris. Ignore the moss myth.
tags: north, direction, sun, shadow stick, watch method, Polaris, North Star, Plough, moss myth, stars
---
!quick
1. **Shadow stick:** mark the tip of a stick's shadow, wait 15–20 min, mark again. First mark → second mark points **east**.
2. **Sun:** around **1 pm (BST)** or **noon (GMT)** the sun is due **south** in the UK.
3. **Night:** find the **Plough**; follow its two "pointer" stars to **Polaris**, the North Star.
4. **Moss** grows wherever it is damp and shady. It is **not** a reliable direction finder.
!end

## Shadow stick
!svg shadow-stick
- Push a straight stick upright in level ground. Mark the tip of its shadow with a stone.
- Wait 15–20 minutes and mark the new tip.
- The line from the first to the second mark runs roughly **west → east**. Stand with the first mark on your left and the second on your right: you are facing roughly north.
- Most accurate around the middle of the day.

## The sun
- In the UK the sun rises in the **east** (north-east in summer, south-east in winter), is due **south** at local midday, and sets in the **west** (north-west in summer, south-west in winter).
- Local midday is about **12:00 GMT** in winter and **13:00 BST** in summer (a little later in the west of Britain).

## Watch method (analogue watch, or draw one)
- Hold the watch flat and point the **hour hand** at the sun.
- **South** lies halfway between the hour hand and **12** (in GMT/winter) or **1** (in BST/summer).
- Rough, but useful.

## Polaris (the North Star)
- Find the **Plough** (Big Dipper), visible all year in the UK.
- The two stars at the end of the "pan" (the pointers) point to **Polaris**, about five times the distance between them.
- Polaris is not especially bright, but it sits almost exactly above **north**; its height above the horizon equals your latitude (50–60° in the UK).
- **Cassiopeia** (a W shape) lies on the other side of Polaris from the Plough.

## Unreliable signs
- Moss, lichen, tree growth, ant hills and church orientation are all too unreliable to navigate by.

=== naismith-pacing
title: Pacing, timing and Naismith's rule
summary: Estimate walking time with Naismith's rule, and measure distance by counting your paces.
tags: Naismith, pacing, timing, distance, speed, double pace, ascent
---
!quick
1. **Naismith:** allow **1 hour per 5 km** plus **10 minutes per 100 m** of climb. Add time for rough ground, darkness, the group and the weather.
2. **Pace counting:** count every **double pace** (each time your left foot lands). Most adults take **60–70** per 100 m on the flat.
3. Use tally marks, knots on a cord or pebbles to count each 100 m.
!end

## Naismith's rule
| Distance | Time on flat (5 km/h) |
|---|---|
| 100 m | 1 min 12 s |
| 500 m | 6 min |
| 1 km | 12 min |
| 5 km | 60 min |
Add **1 minute for every 10 m of ascent**. Steep descents also take longer than flat walking.

## Calibrating your pace
- Walk 100 m on known ground (e.g. a measured track) and count double paces. Repeat uphill, downhill and through heather; these all increase the count.
- Note your counts here or on your map case.

=== handrails-catching
title: Handrails, catching features and attack points
summary: Navigate using linear features: follow handrails, stop at catching features, and attack small targets from big ones.
tags: handrail, catching feature, attack point, aiming off, navigation strategy
---
!quick
1. **Handrail:** follow a line you can't lose: a wall, fence, stream, path or forest edge.
2. **Catching feature:** a line beyond your target that tells you you've gone too far. Stop and rethink.
3. **Attack point:** an obvious feature near a hard-to-find target. Navigate precisely (bearing and pacing) from it.
4. **Aim off** to one side of a target on a line, so you know which way to turn.
!end

## Notes
- Plan your route as a chain of easy legs between definite features.
- Tick off features as you pass them ("tick features") and keep your thumb on the map where you are.

- Mountain Training: Navigation techniques
- British Orienteering: Basic techniques

=== contours-terrain
title: Reading contours and terrain
summary: Recognise hills, valleys, ridges, spurs and steepness from contour patterns.
tags: contours, relief, spur, valley, ridge, slope, steep, map reading
---
!quick
1. **Close contours = steep**; widely spaced = gentle.
2. **V-shapes pointing uphill** = valleys (often with streams). **V or U-shapes pointing downhill** = spurs and ridges.
3. **Rings** = hilltops (or hollows, if the numbers go down).
4. Check direction: streams always run downhill, and contour numbers are written facing uphill.
!end

## Notes
- **Cliffs** are shown with a separate rock symbol; contours can merge there.
- Use the shape of the ground to confirm position: "I should have a steep slope on my left and a stream below."

- Ordnance Survey: Understanding map contours
- Mountain Training: Navigation

=== signalling-rescue
title: Signalling for rescue
summary: Whistle and torch distress signals (6 per minute), ground-to-air symbols, body signals, and making yourself visible.
tags: signal, whistle, torch, SOS, distress, ground-to-air, rescue, helicopter, mirror, flash
---
!quick
1. **Six** whistle blasts or torch flashes **in a minute**, then a minute's silence, repeated. That is the **UK mountain distress signal**. The reply is **three**.
2. **SOS** in light: ··· — — — ··· (3 short, 3 long, 3 short).
3. **Ground-to-air:** a big **V** (need help) or **X** (need medical help), at least 3 m long, in contrasting material.
4. **Be seen:** bright clothing spread out, movement, a fire's smoke by day (where safe).
!end

## Sound and light
- A whistle carries much further than a shout and costs no energy. Your torch, or the phone's torch ([Tools](#/tools)), can signal at night.
- Save your battery: signal at intervals (e.g. every 10–15 minutes), and when you hear or see searchers.

## Ground-to-air signals
!svg ground-to-air
Use rocks, logs, dug turf, clothing or a tarp. Make the lines straight and the symbols big, ideally 3 m long or more, with a strong contrast against the ground.

## Body signals to a helicopter
- **Both arms up in a Y**: Yes, we need help.
- **One arm up, one arm down**: No, we don't need help.

## Other tips
- **Mirror** or any shiny surface (the phone screen, the steel pot lid): flash sunlight towards aircraft or distant people. Aim by sighting along your outstretched hand.
- **Smoke:** add green leaves to a fire for white smoke by day. Only where it is safe and legal; don't start a wildfire.
- Stay near open ground, paths and ridgelines where searchers will look.

=== night-navigation
title: Moving at night
summary: Usually stay put at night. If you must move, how to do it safely and save your torch.
tags: night, darkness, headtorch, torch, moving at night
---
!quick
1. **Usually: stop, shelter and wait for daylight**, especially on steep or unknown ground.
2. If you must move: use known paths only; check every few minutes; go slower.
3. Use the torch on low; let your eyes adapt (20–30 minutes) and use the red night mode in Settings.
!end

## Notes
- Darkness doubles your times; Naismith estimates no longer hold.
- Crags and streams are the biggest dangers. Many accidents happen when people try to "get down" in the dark.

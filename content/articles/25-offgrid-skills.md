# Long-term off-grid living, plus the fire, tools, fishing, preserving and phone-navigation pages that support it.

!quick
1. Pick a site **above flood level**, **30 m or more** from water but within a short walk of it, with plenty of **dead wood** nearby.
2. Put your back to the **south-west**: most UK wind and rain comes from that side. Use a bank, a rise or dense trees as a windbreak.
3. Lay out **zones**: shelter; fire just downwind of it; food store apart; latrine **downhill and downwind**, at least 30 m from water.
4. Look **up** for dead branches and **down** for flood debris and bog before you build anything.
!end

## What a long-term site needs
An overnight spot only has to get you through one night ([[a:site-selection]]). A camp you live in for weeks has to keep supplying you. Before committing, spend an hour walking the area and check:

- **Water:** a spring or clear stream within about 5–10 minutes' walk. Camp **at least 30 m away** and **well above** it. Upland streams rise fast after heavy rain upstream, even when it isn't raining where you are. Look for flood debris (grass and twigs caught in low branches, sand and silt lines on the banks) and camp well above the highest you find.
- **Fuel:** dead standing and fallen wood within a few hundred metres. You will burn far more than you expect. A site that runs out of easy wood in a week means long trips later ([[a:permanent-fire]]).
- **Shelter from the wind:** the prevailing wind across the UK is from the **south-west**, and most bad weather arrives from the west. A low ridge, a bank, or a belt of dense trees (conifers, holly) on that side makes a big difference. Avoid hilltops and ridges (wind), and the bottoms of hollows, where cold air, frost and fog collect on clear nights.
- **Drainage:** firm, gently sloping ground that drains. Rushes, sphagnum moss and flat, bright green patches mean bog.
- **Morning sun:** a spot facing east or south-east dries out and warms up first.
- **Materials:** bracken, dry grass or leaf litter for bedding ([[a:insulation-ground]]); straight poles for frames.

## Hazards to rule out
- **Dead trees and hanging branches** overhead. Beech can drop big limbs without warning, and many ash trees are dying of ash dieback and become brittle. Look up before you build, and again after every gale.
- Flood plains, dry stream beds and the inside of river bends.
- Rockfall below crags and scree.
- Fields with livestock, especially cows with calves, and well-used animal paths.
- Dense bracken and long grass: ticks ([[a:tick-removal-lyme]]).
- Still water in summer: midges, especially in the west of Scotland.
- Peat: it burns underground. No fires on it ([[a:fire-safety]]).

## Laying out the camp
Think in zones, a few metres to tens of metres apart.

| Zone | Where | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sleeping shelter | Best-drained, most sheltered spot, opening facing away from the south-west | Stays dry; wind and rain don't blow in |
| Fire and cooking | Just downwind of the shelter (usually north-east of it), on bare soil | Smoke and sparks blow away from you |
| Woodpile | Between shelter and fire, under cover | Dry fuel within reach at night |
| Water | A path to the source; containers at camp kept covered | Clean water, nothing dipped in it |
| Food store | 10 m or more from where you sleep, hung off the ground | Keeps rats, mice, foxes and badgers off your food and away from you |
| Latrine | **Downhill and downwind**, at least 30 m from water and well away from camp | Keeps germs out of your water and food |
| Washing | Away from the water source; tip grey water onto soil, never into the stream | Soap and food scraps foul water |

- Dig a shallow **drainage gutter** on the uphill side of the shelter.
- Wash hands after the latrine and before handling food, every time ([[a:latrines]], [[a:water-hygiene]]).
- Hang food in a bag from a branch, at least 1.5 m up and well out from the trunk. There are no bears in Britain, but rodents and foxes find anything left on the ground.
- Keep tools in one place. A knife dropped in leaf litter is gone.
- Make paths and stick to them, or the camp turns to mud.

## Make it last
- Build the shelter properly, once: a steep roof, thick insulation and a raised bed ([[a:long-term-shelter]]).
- Set a daily routine: water, wood, food, repairs, rest ([[a:daily-routine]]).
- Save the camp as a waypoint in [[t:gps]] so you can find it again, and keep a log in [[t:notebook]].

!law Wild camping and the law
- **Scotland:** wild camping is lawful on most unenclosed land under the Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003, if you follow the **Scottish Outdoor Access Code**: small numbers, only two or three nights in one place, away from buildings, roads and historic sites, and leave no trace. Some areas, such as parts of Loch Lomond and the Trossachs in spring and summer, need a permit.
- **England and Wales:** there is no general right to wild camp. You need the **landowner's permission**, except in parts of **Dartmoor**, where backpack camping is allowed in areas mapped by the National Park Authority.
- **Northern Ireland:** landowner's permission.
- Staying for weeks, cutting live wood or building structures goes beyond any access right, anywhere.
- **In a genuine emergency, your safety comes first.** Contact the landowner or move on as soon as you safely can, and leave the site as you found it.
!end

=== sustainable-water
title: Water for the long term: sourcing and storage
summary: Springs, rain catchment, settling and safe storage: a water supply that keeps a camp going for weeks.

icon: barrel
time: 1–3 hours to set up
level: moderate

!quick
1. **Boil all of it, always**, however clear the spring or rain looks: bring it to a rolling boil ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]).
2. Best sources: a **spring** you have cleaned up, then **rain** caught on a tarp, then a fast-flowing stream.
3. Store water **covered**, in containers that have only ever held food or water. Keep boiled and unboiled water apart.
4. Plan on about **7 litres per person per day** for drinking, cooking and washing hands; more in heat or hard work.
!end

## How much you need
| Use | Litres per person per day |
|---|---|
| Drinking: cool weather, light work | 2.5–3 |
| Drinking: hard work or warm weather | 4–6 |
| Cooking | 1–3 |
| Hand washing and basic hygiene | 2–4 |
| **Total** | **about 7, and up to 15 for comfort** |

Every litre you boil costs fuel and time. Put a full pot on whenever the fire is going. See [[a:water-needs]] and the water calculator in [[t:calc]].

## Springs
A spring is water coming out of the ground: often at the foot of a slope, where a line of rushes or bright green moss begins, or at the head of a stream with a dry valley above it. Water from underground is usually cleaner than a stream, but **surface water often runs in close to the outlet**, and animals drink there. Boil it anyway.

How to improve a spring:
1. Gently clear leaves and silt from the **eye** (where the water comes out). Don't dig into the source itself.
2. Dig a shallow **ditch uphill** of it to turn surface runoff and animal muck away.
3. Make a small **basin** just below the eye, lined with clean stones, and let it run clear for a few hours.
4. Build a **spout** at the basin's outflow, so the water falls clear into your container: a curved strip of bark, a split hollow stem, or a channel of flat stones. You fill straight from the flow and never dip a dirty container in.
5. **Cover** the basin with flat stones, bark or branches to keep out leaves, droppings and light: a simple spring box. Fence it with dead branches to keep animals off.
6. After heavy rain, wait until it runs clear again before collecting.

## Rain catchment
- **1 mm of rain on 1 m² gives 1 litre.** A 2 × 3 m tarp catches about **6 litres for every millimetre**. A day of steady rain (10 mm) can give 60 litres; even drizzle gives a few.
- Pitch the tarp at an angle with one low corner, or tie a dip into the middle with a small stone, so the water runs to one point over your pot or container.
- Let the **first few minutes run to waste**: it washes off bird droppings, dust and debris.
- Water dripping off trees carries droppings and insects: use it only if there's nothing better. More methods: [[a:rain-dew-collection]].

## Settling
For muddy stream or pond water, fill a container, cover it and leave it for several hours or overnight. Pour the clear top water gently through the cotton cloth into the pot, then boil. Settle in one container, drink from another ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]).

## Storage
- Use anything that has held only food or water: your bottle and pot, clean food bags, a sound plastic container. **Never** use containers that held fuel, chemicals, pesticide or antifreeze.
- Keep containers **covered**, in shade, off the ground, and away from the latrine and washing area.
- Mark which containers hold **boiled** water. **Pour, don't dip**: hands and cups carry germs.
- **Rotate:** use the oldest first. Drink boiled water within a day or two. If it was left uncovered, has bits in it or smells odd, boil it again.
- A pit lined with a tarp or survival bag, with a cover, holds a reserve of raw water for washing or boiling later. Treat it as dirty.
- In hard frost, keep bottles in your shelter. Don't let a full, sealed bottle freeze solid: it can split.

## What boiling won't fix
- **Blue-green algae:** scum, or water that looks like green or blue-green paint, usually in still water in late summer. Boiling doesn't destroy the toxins. Find another source.
- **Chemical pollution:** farm runoff, orange-stained water from old mines, a fuel sheen, water below roads and industry. See [[a:boiling-limits]] and [[a:choosing-water-source]].

=== permanent-fire
title: Keeping a permanent fire
summary: A fire that never goes out: hearth and reflector, keeping embers day and night, a dry woodpile and a rain cover.

icon: brick-wall-fire
time: half a day to build; daily upkeep
level: moderate
needs: knife, tarp, cord, firewood
related: banking-fire, carrying-embers, fire-reflector, firewood-uk
---
!quick
1. Build the hearth once: **bare soil**, a shallow pit, a **reflector** wall behind, clear for 2 m round and nothing overhead.
2. Keep a **dry woodpile** off the ground and under cover, with at least **a full day and night** of fuel ready.
3. By day, keep a **bed of hardwood coals** under ash. Every night, **bank** it ([[a:banking-fire]]).
4. Keep a **backup ember** and carry one if you move ([[a:carrying-embers]]). Your ferro rod stays dry for emergencies.
!end

## The hearth
- **Site:** just downwind of your shelter, on mineral soil or gravel. Never on peat, among tree roots or under branches. Clear a circle about 2 m across down to bare earth ([[a:fire-safety]]).
- **Pit:** dig a shallow pit about 60–80 cm across and 10–15 cm deep, keeping the turf to put back later. It shelters the coals from wind and holds the ash, which is what keeps embers alive.
- **Stones:** ring it with stones from dry ground. Never use stones from a stream bed, the shore or wet ground: trapped water turns to steam and can crack them violently. Avoid flint, slate and shale.
- **Reflector:** drive two pairs of stakes into the ground about 50 cm behind the fire and stack green logs between them to knee or waist height. It throws heat towards you, blocks wind, and dries wood stacked in front of it ([[a:fire-reflector]]).
- **Pot support:** two green logs either side of the coals, or a crane: a green pole pushed into the ground at an angle, with a notch at the end to hang the pot.

## Keeping embers through the day
- A fire is easiest to keep when it's mostly **coals**, not flames. Oak, ash and beech give the longest-lasting coals ([[a:firewood-uk]]).
- In the morning, rake the coals together and push one or two thick logs end-on into them, like a star fire ([[a:fire-lays]]). Push them in as the ends burn. A thick log can smoulder for hours.
- When you step away: rake the coals into a heap, lay a thick log on them and **cover with ash** so no flame shows. When you come back, uncover them and blow.
- A coal that looks grey may still be alive: blow on it and watch for a red glow.
- Better still, have someone stay at camp, or carry a smouldering ember in a tin of [[s:tinder-fungus]] or [[s:cramp-balls|cramp balls]] when you go.

## The woodpile
- Gather **every day**, while it's light and dry, in three sizes: kindling, wrist-thick sticks, and leg-thick logs.
- Stack it **off the ground** on two poles, under a roof of bark, branches or a spare tarp, open at the sides so air moves through.
- **Split** thick wood. Split wood dries much faster than round logs, and the inside of dead wood is drier than the outside.
- Keep a day's wood drying **near** (not against) the fire or in front of the reflector, and a reserve of several days in the main pile. When the reserve runs low, gathering wood comes before everything except water.
- Pick up dry tinder whenever you see it and keep it in a bag in your shelter ([[a:natural-tinders]]).

## A rain cover
- Rig a tarp **at least 2 m above** the fire, sloping so rain runs off away from the hearth and the woodpile. Any lower and heat and sparks will melt or burn it.
- Better: a separate roof of overlapping bark slabs, green branches or turf on a pole frame. Leave it open on the downwind side so smoke can escape.
- In heavy rain, keep a small, hot fire rather than a smouldering one, and keep feeding it.

## Safety
- Never burn a fire **inside** an enclosed shelter: carbon monoxide kills ([[a:fire-safety]]).
- Keep bedding, kit and the woodpile out of reach of sparks: nylon and down melt and burn.
- Keep a full pot or container of water by the hearth.
- In dry spells (spring is the worst season) or strong wind, keep the fire **small**, watched and well banked, or put it out.
- When you leave the site for good: drown it, stir, drown again, check with the back of your hand for heat, scatter the cold ash and replace the turf.

=== year-round-food

!quick
1. **Autumn (Sep–Nov) is the only time of plenty:** gather and **dry** nuts and fruit to store.
2. **Winter and the hungry gap (Mar–May)** are lean: ration what you stored, and use the shore and legal fishing.
3. **Energy comes from nuts, fish and shellfish**, not greens. Greens give vitamins and little else.
4. Stay legal: **no traps or snares** ([[a:trapping-law]]); fish only with a licence and permission, or in the sea.
!end

!danger Identification first
Never eat a wild plant or fungus unless you are 100% sure what it is. Read [[a:foraging-rules]] before using anything here, and check the [[a:foraging-calendar|calendar]] and each species' lookalikes.
!end

## The calorie reality
An adult needs roughly **2,000–2,500 kcal a day** (NHS guide figures), and more in cold weather or doing hard work. Most wild food holds very little energy.

| Food, per 100 g | About |
|---|---|
| [[s:hazel]] nuts, shelled | 630 kcal |
| [[s:sweet-chestnut]], roasted | 240 kcal |
| Oily fish such as [[s:mackerel]] | 200 kcal |
| [[s:brown-trout]] | 130 kcal |
| [[s:mussel]] flesh, raw weight | 90 kcal |
| [[s:blackberry]] | 45 kcal |
| [[s:nettle]] tops | 40 kcal |

A day's energy would take about **350 g of shelled hazelnuts**, or about **5 kg of blackberries**. Gathering costs energy too, so put your effort into food that repays it, and accept that you will lose weight over weeks. See [[a:food-priorities]] and [[a:rationing]].

## Through the year
What's ripe right now: [In season now](#/season). Full month-by-month list: [[a:foraging-calendar]].

### Spring and the hungry gap (March–May)
Traditionally the leanest time in rural Britain: stores have run out and new growth is mostly leaves.
- Greens: [[s:nettle]], [[s:wild-garlic]] (pick leaf by leaf: [[a:compare-wild-garlic]]), [[s:cleavers]], [[s:dandelion]], [[s:hawthorn]] leaves, [[s:common-sorrel]].
- Energy has to come from the shore, fish, and whatever nuts you dried in autumn.

### Summer (June–August)
- More greens and the first fruit: [[s:wild-strawberry]], [[s:raspberry]], [[s:bilberry]], early blackberries.
- Fishing is at its best, and mackerel shoals come inshore.
- Long, drier days: the best time to dry food and stockpile firewood.

### Autumn glut (September–November)
- Nuts: [[s:hazel]] (gather from late August, before the squirrels), [[s:sweet-chestnut]], [[s:beech]]nuts (small amounts), [[s:oak]] acorns (only after leaching out the bitter tannins in many changes of water over days).
- Fruit: blackberries, [[s:elder]]berries (cook them: raw ones cause vomiting), [[s:crab-apple]], [[s:dog-rose]] hips (remove the irritant hairs), haws and sloes ([[s:blackthorn]]).
- Fungi are everywhere, but **only eat any an expert has identified in person** ([[a:fungi-id-basics]]).
- **This is when you store.** Dry nuts in their shells, make fruit leather, and smoke and dry fish ([[a:smoking-drying]]).

### Winter (December–February)
- Very little on land: [[s:chickweed]], [[s:hairy-bittercress]], [[s:three-cornered-leek]], [[s:sea-beet]], [[s:jelly-ear]].
- The shore is the richest winter larder ([[a:shellfish-shore]]). Shellfish are traditionally gathered only in months with an "r" in them, partly because toxic algal blooms are more common in warm months.
- Cold raises your energy needs just as food is scarcest. Ration stored food from the start of winter, not the end.

## Fishing, legally
- **England and Wales (freshwater):** an **Environment Agency rod licence** *and* the permission of whoever owns the fishing. Close season for coarse fish on rivers: **15 March–15 June**.
- **Scotland:** no licence, but you still need permission or a permit. No salmon or sea trout fishing on Sundays.
- **Sea fishing** from the shore needs no licence, but minimum sizes and local byelaws apply.
- How to: [[a:fishing-basics]] and [[a:improvised-fishing]].

## Trapping and hunting
Don't. Snares are banned in Scotland and Wales, all wild birds and many mammals are protected, and taking game is poaching. It is never necessary in Britain ([[a:trapping-law]]).

## Small but useful
- **Insects and worms:** protein in tiny amounts; always cooked ([[a:insects]]).
- **Seaweed:** fresh, attached, from clean coasts; a little energy and plenty of minerals.
- Roots such as [[s:burdock]]: uprooting any wild plant needs the landowner's permission.

## Storing what you gather
- Keep dried food in cloth or bark containers, hung off the ground, away from rodents, in dry air.
- Check it every few days. Throw out anything mouldy, damp or smelling off.
- Dry nuts in their shells keep for months.

=== fire-essentials
title: Fire: the essentials
summary: The fastest reliable way to get a fire going with your kit: prepare everything, build off the ground, light it early and keep it small.

icon: flame
time: 10–30 min

needs: ferro-rod, char-cloth, magnifier, arc-lighter, knife, tinder, firewood

!quick
1. **Prepare everything before you strike:** a tinder bundle the size of **two fists**, kindling from **pencil-lead** to **pencil** to **thumb** thickness, and **wrist-thick** fuel. Then gather the same again.
2. Build on a **platform** of dry sticks or bark, off wet ground, out of the wind.
3. **Ferro rod:** tip touching the tinder, striker held still, **pull the rod back** hard ([[a:ferro-rod]]).
4. Feed it **gently**, smallest first. Keep it **small**. Light it **before** you are cold or it is dark.
!end

## Why fires fail
Nearly always for the same reasons: too little kindling, damp or too-thick fuel, smothering the young flame, or trying with numb hands in the dark. Fix those, and any of your ignition methods will work.

## 1. Gather and grade
Collect everything before you strike anything. The snap test: dry dead wood breaks with a crack. If it bends, it's green or wet.

| Size | Thickness | How much |
|---|---|---|
| Tinder | Fine, dry, fluffy fibres | A loose bundle the size of two fists |
| Fine kindling | Pencil lead (matchstick) | Two big handfuls |
| Medium kindling | Pencil | Two big handfuls |
| Thick kindling | Thumb | An armful |
| Fuel | Wrist | An armful, with more stacked nearby |

- Best tinders: [[s:silver-birch]] bark, fatwood, dead bracken, dry grass, shredded honeysuckle or clematis bark, cotton wool ([[a:natural-tinders]]).
- Take wood from **standing dead** trees and dead branches still on trees. Wood lying on the ground in Britain is usually wet.
- In rain, split sticks to the dry core and carve [[a:feather-sticks]]. More in [[a:wet-weather-fire]].

## 2. The base
- Choose a safe, legal spot: bare earth, cleared 1–2 m round, nothing overhanging, never on peat ([[a:fire-safety]]).
- Lay a **platform** of dry wrist-thick sticks side by side, or a slab of bark. Wet ground steals heat from the tinder.
- Shield the windward side with a log, your pack or your body.

## 3. Light it
- **Ferro rod (first choice):** put the rod tip in or touching the tinder, hold the striker still at about 45°, and pull the rod back firmly along its whole length. In damp weather, scrape a little powder onto the tinder first.
- **Arc lighter:** touch the arc to the edge of birch bark or feather-stick curls ([[a:arc-lighter]]). Keep it warm in a pocket: cold batteries fail.
- **Backups that need char cloth:** flint and steel ([[a:flint-and-steel]]) or the magnifier in bright sun ([[a:magnifier-fire]]) give a glowing ember on [[a:char-cloth]], not a flame. Fold the ember into the tinder bundle and blow it into flame.
- When the sun is out, use the magnifier and save the rod and the lighter's battery.

## 4. Grow it
1. When the tinder flames, lean fine kindling over it in a small teepee ([[a:fire-lays]]). Don't drop a heap on top.
2. Add pencil, then thumb-thick sticks, as each size catches. Leave gaps: fire needs air as much as fuel.
3. If it struggles, blow gently at the base with long, slow breaths.
4. Only add wrist-thick fuel once you have a small bed of glowing coals.

## When to light it, and how big
- **Light it early**, while you still have warm hands, daylight and energy. Fine finger movements go quickly in the cold. A fire you can't light at dusk with numb fingers is a fire you won't have.
- **Keep it small.** A small fire is easier to control, uses less wood, and you can sit close. A reflector behind it sends much more of the heat your way ([[a:fire-reflector]]).
- Stack the night's wood before dark: at least double what you think you need.
- Keep your ignition kit dry, together, and in the same pocket every time.

- Scouts UK: Fire lighting (scouts.org.uk)

!quick
1. **The steel pot comes first:** boil and cook in it; make other containers to collect, store and carry.
2. **Birch bark:** only from **fallen or dead** birch. Warm it to soften, **fold** the corners (never cut them) and clip with split sticks.
3. **Burnt bowl:** lay hot coals on a block of dry wood, blow, scrape out the char, repeat.
4. **Hot rocks:** only **dry rocks from dry ground**. Rocks from streams or the shore can **explode** in a fire.

## The steel pot and bottle
The stainless pot is the only container you have that can go on a fire. Keep it clean for boiling water and cooking ([[a:kit-pot-bottle]]).
- **Never heat anything sealed:** take the bottle's cap off before it goes near heat ([[a:boiling-steel-bottle-pot]]).
- Hang it from a green-wood crane or pot hook rather than balancing it on burning logs. Cord burns; use green wood or wire.
- A lid doubles as a plate, a frying pan and a signal mirror.

## Birch bark containers
[[s:silver-birch]] bark is waterproof, resists rot, and turns leathery and flexible when warm.
- **Where:** fallen birch logs and dead standing birch often have sound bark long after the wood inside has rotted. Cut down the length of the log and peel the bark off in one sheet.
- **Soften** stiff bark by passing it over the fire for a few seconds, or dipping it in hot water.
- **Fold, don't cut through:** a cut corner leaks.

A simple tray or pot:
1. Cut a rectangle about 30 × 20 cm.
2. Fold up all four sides by 5 cm. At each corner, pinch the spare bark into a triangular fold and lay it flat against the end, like wrapping a parcel.
3. Clip each folded corner with a split stick of hazel or willow (like a clothes peg), or stitch it with cordage through holes made with an awl ([[a:wood-bone-tools]]).
4. For a deeper pot, use a longer sheet with taller sides, and lash a rim of split hazel round the top to stiffen it.

A bark container holds water for days. Full of water, it can sit at the very edge of hot coals: the water keeps the bark below burning point, but any bark above the waterline will burn. Hot rocks (below) are easier.

!law Never strip living trees
Peeling bark off a living tree can kill it, and it's damage to someone else's property unless the landowner allows it. The Scottish Outdoor Access Code doesn't allow it either. Use bark from fallen or dead trees only.
!end

## Burnt-out wooden bowls
Slow, but needs only fire, a knife and patience.
1. Find a block of dry, sound wood 20–30 cm across: birch, willow, alder, lime or sycamore. Never [[s:yew]], [[s:cherry-laurel]] or [[s:rhododendron]] (toxic), and avoid resinous conifers (the taste).
2. Split it in half or carve a flat face, and level the base so it sits steady.
3. Put two or three **hot coals** in the middle of the flat face. Blow on them gently through a hollow stem (elder with the pith pushed out, or a reed) so they burn down into the wood.
4. When they fade, tip them out, **scrape out** the charcoal with your knife tip, a stone or a shell, and add fresh coals.
5. Keep the rim wet, or smeared with mud or clay, so it doesn't burn where you don't want it to.
6. Repeat until it's deep enough. A cup takes an hour or two; a bowl, most of a day.
7. Scrape it smooth and rinse well before use.

## Bags, pits and cloth
- **Plastic bag or survival bag:** holds water if supported in a hollow or a frame. Double it. Never put it near heat.
- **Lined pit:** dig a hole, line it with the tarp or survival bag, and cover it: a store of raw water or a washing basin.
- **Cotton cloth:** tie it into a bundle for nuts and berries. Keep your water-straining cloth separate.

## Boiling water with hot rocks
Useful when the pot is busy, or you need a lot of hot water.
1. Choose **fist-sized, dense, dry rocks from dry ground**: a bank or a slope well above any stream. **Never take rocks from a stream bed, the shore or wet ground.** Water trapped in cracks turns to steam and can make them **explode**, throwing hot fragments. Avoid flint, slate, shale and any layered or cracked rock.
2. Heat five or six rocks in the fire for 30–45 minutes. Stand back while they heat, and keep others back too.
3. Lift one out with green-wood tongs (a hazel stick bent double, or two sticks), knock off the ash, and dip it briefly in a separate rinse container.
4. Lower it into the water in your wooden, bark or lined container. It hisses and steams. Swap it for the next hot rock.
5. Keep going until the water is at a **full rolling boil**: only then is it safe to drink ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]).

Let ash and grit settle before you drink. Even dry rocks can crack, so never stand over a fire full of rocks.

=== wood-bone-tools
title: Making tools from wood and bone
summary: Carve tent pegs, a digging stick, toggles, spoons and a mallet, fire-harden points, and make bone needles and awls.
tags: tools, carving, whittling, digging stick, tent pegs, toggle, spoon, mallet, bone needle, awl, fire hardening, bushcraft
icon: axe
time: 10 min (a peg) to a few hours (a spoon)
level: moderate
needs: knife, cord
related: knife-safety, knife-sharpening, containers, sewing-repairs
---
!quick
1. **Cut safely:** kneel, work to the side of your body, blade moving away from you, nobody within reach ([[a:knife-safety]]).
2. Use **dead or freshly fallen wood**. Cutting living wood needs the **landowner's permission**.
3. Make what you need most first: **pegs**, a **digging stick** and a **mallet**, then spoons.
4. Make needles and awls from the **bones of fish or meat you have eaten**.
!end

## Choosing wood
| Wood | Good for |
|---|---|
| Hazel | Straight and splits cleanly: pegs, poles, toggles, rods |
| Ash | Tough and springy: digging sticks, handles, mallets |
| Birch, sycamore, alder, lime, willow | Even grain, easy to carve: spoons, bowls |
| Oak, beech | Hard and heavy: mallets, wedges |

- **Never** use [[s:yew]], [[s:laburnum]], [[s:cherry-laurel]] or [[s:rhododendron]] for anything that touches food or your mouth: they are toxic.
- **Green wood** (still full of sap) carves far more easily than seasoned wood. A freshly fallen branch gives you that without cutting a living tree.

## Tent pegs
1. Cut a straight, thumb-thick stick 20–30 cm long.
2. Sharpen one end to a point with long, shallow cuts away from you.
3. Cut a notch 3 cm below the top: a straight stop cut across the stick, then a sloping cut up to meet it. The cord sits in the notch.
4. Bevel the top edge so it doesn't split when you hammer it.

Make a dozen. Drive them in at about 45°, leaning away from the shelter.

## Digging stick
A wrist-thick ash or hazel stick about 1 m long, with one end carved into a flat chisel edge about two fingers wide. Fire-harden the tip (below). Use it for latrine holes, drainage gutters and loosening soil.

## Fire-hardening
- Hold the carved tip **above** (not in) hot coals, turning it, until the wood has dried and just turned light brown. Don't let it blacken: charcoal is weak.
- Drying the wood makes the tip harder and longer-lasting. It works best on green wood.
- **Safety:** a hardened point is a stabbing hazard. Keep points covered or pushed into the ground when not in use, and never carry a sharpened stick where you might fall on it.

## Toggles
A piece of hardwood about finger-thick and 5–6 cm long, with a groove carved round the middle. Tie cord round the groove. Use it as a button, a pot hanger, a handle for a bag, or a quick release: pass a loop of cord through a ring or round a tree and pin it with the toggle. Pull the toggle out and it all comes free.

## Spoons
1. Split a straight-grained piece of green birch, sycamore or hazel, about 25 cm long and 5 cm across, down the middle.
2. Draw the spoon on the flat face with a piece of charcoal.
3. Carve the outline in short, controlled cuts, working with the grain. Leave the neck thick until last.
4. Hollow the bowl by turning the spoon against the knife tip rather than levering the blade. Or burn it out with a small coal and scrape, as for a bowl ([[a:containers]]).
5. Dry it slowly, away from the fire, or it will crack.

A first spoon takes one to three hours.

## Mallet
Take a piece of oak, beech or ash about 40 cm long and as thick as your calf. Carve the bottom half down to a handle; the top half is the head. Use it to drive pegs, and to tap the spine of a **fixed-blade** knife when splitting small logs. Never baton a folding knife: the lock can fail.

## Bone tools
Only use bones from animals you have legally eaten: fish you caught, or meat you carried. Boil them clean first. Boiled bone works better than roasted, which goes brittle.
- **Awl:** crack a leg bone lengthways against a stone, choose a long splinter, and grind it to a point on wet sandstone. Use it to punch holes in bark, leather and cloth.
- **Needle:** grind a thin splinter smooth on fine wet stone. Make the eye by twisting the knife point gently from both sides, or cut a groove round the end and tie the thread to it ([[a:sewing-repairs]]).
- **Fish bones:** stiff fish ribs make pins and gorge hooks ([[a:improvised-fishing]]).
- A [[s:blackthorn]] or [[s:hawthorn]] thorn makes an awl in a minute.

!law Cutting wood
- **England and Wales:** all wood, dead or alive, belongs to the landowner. Ask before you gather. Cutting living trees or branches without permission can be **criminal damage**.
- **Scotland:** access rights don't include cutting or damaging trees. Take only small amounts of dead wood.
- Felling trees usually needs a felling licence, even for the landowner.
- In a genuine emergency, take only what keeps you alive, and make it right with the owner afterwards.
!end

=== improvised-fishing
title: Improvised fishing tackle (and the law)
summary: UK fishing law first, then thorn and gorge hooks, cordage line, bait, where fish lie, and which methods are illegal.
tags: fishing, improvised hook, gorge hook, thorn hook, fishing line, bait, worms, rod licence, close season, sea fishing, angling law, night lines
icon: fishing-hook
time: 30 min to make tackle
level: moderate
needs: knife, cord, strong thread
related: fishing-basics, trapping-law, cordage-nettle-bramble, year-round-food
---
!quick
1. **Law first:** freshwater fishing in England and Wales needs a **rod licence** and the **owner's permission**; Scotland needs permission. **Sea fishing from the shore is free.**
2. **Hook:** a thorn left on its twig, or a **gorge**: a 3 cm sliver of bone or hardwood, pointed at both ends, line tied in the middle.
3. **Bait** with worms or grubs (limpet at sea). Fish at **dawn and dusk** where fish shelter, and **stay with your line**.
4. **Never** use nets, traps, spears, gaffs, snares or lines left unattended.
!end

!law The rules
- **England and Wales:** to fish for salmon, trout, freshwater fish or eels with a rod and line you need an **Environment Agency rod licence**, plus permission from whoever owns the fishing rights. Almost every river, lake and canal is owned or leased by someone.
- **Close seasons:** no coarse fishing on rivers and streams from **15 March to 15 June**. Trout and salmon close seasons vary by region, roughly autumn to spring.
- **Scotland:** no rod licence, but you need the owner's permission or a permit. No salmon or sea trout fishing on **Sundays**. Brown trout close season: **7 October–14 March**.
- **Northern Ireland:** a fishing licence (DAERA, or the Loughs Agency in its areas) and a permit.
- **Sea fishing** from the shore or a pier needs no licence anywhere in the UK, but minimum sizes, catch limits (bass, for example) and local byelaws apply, and some harbours and piers ban it.
- **Illegal for freshwater fish** without special authorisation: nets, traps, spears, gaffs, snares, set lines or **night lines** (lines left unattended), lights, poisons, explosives and electric current.
!end

Rules exist to protect fish stocks, and water bailiffs and police enforce them. Food is your lowest priority in Britain ([[a:food-priorities]]): fish only where it's legal.

## Hooks
- **Thorn hook:** cut a twig of [[s:blackthorn]] or [[s:hawthorn]] with a strong thorn, trimming it to a 2–3 cm shank with the thorn pointing back up it. Cut a small notch at the top of the shank and tie the line there with several turns and a half hitch.
- **Gorge:** a sliver of hardwood, bone or a stiff fish rib, 2–4 cm long, sharpened at both ends, with a groove round the middle for the line. Lay it along the bait. When a fish swallows it, a pull turns it crosswise and it lodges. Give a bite a few seconds before you pull. A gorge is swallowed deep, so you can't return the fish: only use it where you can legally keep what you catch.
- **Metal:** a safety pin or bent pin with the point filed sharp.
- Make several. You will lose them on snags.

## Line, rod, float and weight
- **Line:** strong thread, dental floss, threads unpicked from clothing, or fine plant cordage ([[a:cordage-nettle-bramble]]). Thinner and paler is less visible to fish.
- **Rod:** a straight 2–3 m hazel or willow stick (with permission) lets you reach past bankside weed and handle the fish. Tie the line at the tip and again further down, so a big fish can't snap the tip off with the line.
- **Float:** a piece of dry stick or a feather quill. Set it so the bait hangs just off the bottom.
- **Weight:** a small stone tied into the line.

## Bait
- **Freshwater:** earthworms (under logs and stones, in leaf litter), beetle grubs from rotten wood, smooth caterpillars, scraps of fish, or a bit of your own bread or cheese.
- **Sea:** limpet or mussel flesh, lugworm or ragworm dug at low tide, a strip of fish. For mackerel in summer, a strip of shiny foil or a few bright feathers on the hook.
- Keep bait cool and damp in moss.

## Where and when
- Dawn and dusk, overcast days, and rising water after light rain.
- Rivers: the tail of pools, below riffles, under undercut banks, behind boulders, in the shade of trees. More in [[a:fishing-basics]].
- Sea: rocks and piers on the rising tide, a couple of hours either side of high water. Watch the tide and the waves on rocks ([[a:coast-and-tides]]).
- Move quietly, keep low and keep your shadow off the water.

## Landing and keeping
- Keep the line tight. Lift small fish straight out, or slide bigger ones onto a shallow edge. Don't wade into fast water.
- Kill a fish you are keeping straight away with a sharp blow to the top of the head. Gut it, cook it thoroughly and eat it the same day, or smoke and dry it ([[a:smoking-drying]]).

=== smoking-drying
title: Preserving: smoking and drying
summary: Dry and hot-smoke fish and meat over a fire, and dry berries, fungi and herbs, without making yourself ill in damp British weather.
tags: preserving, smoking, drying, jerky, smoked fish, smoke rack, dehydrate, food storage, botulism, food safety, dried berries, fruit leather, dried mushrooms, herbs
icon: beef
time: 1–3 days
level: moderate
needs: knife, cord, firewood
related: cooking-on-fire, cooking-wild-food, year-round-food, improvised-fishing
---
!quick
1. **Cut thin:** fish and meat in strips **5 mm** thick or less; split fish open flat.
2. **Cook it first** (or hot-smoke until cooked through), **then dry** on a rack over a smoky hardwood fire until it's **hard and dry right through**.
3. Smoke with **hardwood** (oak, beech, alder, ash, hazel). **Never** conifers, holly, yew, laurel or rhododendron.
4. Store it **dry and in moving air**, never sealed while damp. Mould, slime or a bad smell: **throw it out**.
!end

## Why the fire matters in Britain
The air here is damp for most of the year. Food left to dry in the open often goes mouldy or rots before it dries. A fire gives warm, dry, moving air, and the smoke keeps flies off and slows mould. Allow one to three days.

## Prepare
- **Fish:** gut it, take off the head, and split it open along the backbone so it lies flat, like a kipper. Lift out the backbone on bigger fish. Small fish can be gutted and threaded on a stick through the gills.
- **Meat:** trim off all fat (fat goes rancid) and cut it along the grain into strips no thicker than 5 mm.
- **Salt** helps a lot if you have any: rub it in, or soak strips in strong brine for an hour before drying.
- Wash your hands and knife before and after ([[a:cooking-wild-food]]).

## Cook, then dry
Drying at low temperatures doesn't kill bacteria, so **cook meat and fish through first**: food safety guidance is at least 70 °C for 2 minutes. With no thermometer, simmer the strips in boiling water for a few minutes, or roast them close over coals until no pink remains and the juices run clear. Then move them up onto the rack to dry.

## The smoke rack
1. Build a tripod or two A-frames from green hazel, about 1–1.5 m high, joined by green crossbars to make a grid. Green wood resists burning.
2. Hang the strips or lay the split fish over the bars, not touching each other.
3. Light a small fire underneath. Let it burn down to coals, then add damp hardwood chips, green twigs or rotten hardwood to make **steady smoke** with little flame.
4. The food should be warm, never sizzling or dripping. If it cooks or blackens, raise it or reduce the fire.
5. To trap heat and smoke, drape bark, leafy branches or a cotton sheet over the frame. **Never** a nylon tarp near the heat.
6. Turn the food every few hours. Keep the fire going day and night if you can: drying that stops and starts lets bacteria grow.

## Cold or hot smoking
| | Cold smoking | Hot smoking |
|---|---|---|
| Heat at the food | Below about 30 °C | Above about 70 °C |
| Cooks the food? | No | Yes |
| Keeps | Only with proper salt curing and experience | About a day without a fridge, unless then dried hard |
| For you? | **No:** unsafe without proper cures and a thermometer | **Yes:** then dry it hard |

## How to tell it's done
- **Fish:** stiff and hard, with no soft or damp spot in the thickest part.
- **Meat:** dark and leathery; it bends and cracks but doesn't break cleanly.
- If in doubt, dry it longer.

## Woods for smoke
- **Good:** oak, beech, alder (traditional for fish), ash, hazel, apple and other fruit woods.
- **Don't use for food:** pine, spruce, larch and other conifers (resin, bitter soot), holly, and toxic woods: [[s:yew]], [[s:cherry-laurel]], [[s:rhododendron]], [[s:laburnum]]. Never use painted or treated wood. See [[a:firewood-uk]].

## Storage and botulism
- **Botulism** bacteria grow in damp, low-acid food kept away from air: fish or meat sealed while still moist, packed in fat or oil, or wrapped tightly in plastic. The toxin has no smell or taste, and it can kill.
- So **only store food that is fully dry**, in breathable cloth or bark containers, hung up in dry air. Never seal damp food in a bag, tin or bottle.
- Check it every day or two. **Mould, slime, soft spots or an off smell** mean throw it away. Don't cut mould off meat or fish.
- Cook stored meat and fish thoroughly before eating, for example in a stew.
- Botulism symptoms start hours to days later: blurred or double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, and weakness spreading down the body. It is a medical emergency: get to help as fast as you can ([[a:when-you-reach-help]]).

## Drying plant food
- **Berries and fruit:** halve or squash them (blackberries, bilberries, rosehips with the hairy seeds removed, crab apple slices) and spread them thinly on bark, cloth or a flat stone near the fire or in sun and wind. Mashed berries spread 5 mm thick dry into **fruit leather**.
- **Fungi** (only ones an expert has identified in person): slice them 5 mm thick, thread them on cotton or cordage, and hang them near, not over, the fire until crisp.
- **Herbs for tea** (nettle, mint): tie in small bunches and hang them upside down under cover, where air moves.
- **Nuts:** dry them in their shells in a single layer, turning daily, then store them in a hanging cloth bag away from mice.

=== phone-navigation
title: Using this phone's compass, GPS and map
summary: How the phone's compass, GPS, offline map and step counter work, how to get good readings, and what they cost in battery.
tags: phone, GPS, compass, map, offline map, grid reference, waypoint, step counter, pace, calibrate, battery, location, satnav
icon: locate-fixed
time: 5 min to learn
level: easy
needs: this-phone, map, compass
related: grid-references, map-compass-basics, batteries-electronics, kit-phone
---
!quick
1. **GPS:** stand in the open with a clear sky and keep the phone still. The first fix can take **several minutes**. Read the **grid reference** in [[t:gps]], save a **waypoint**, then turn it off.
2. **Compass:** hold the phone **flat**, away from metal. If it seems wrong, **calibrate** with a slow figure-of-eight ([[t:compass]]).
3. **Map:** [[t:map]] works offline. Tap **locate me** once, and keep **follow mode** off unless you need it: it eats battery.
4. The phone **backs up** a paper map and compass. It doesn't replace them.
!end

## The compass
- The phone has a **magnetic sensor**, not a needle. Metal and magnets throw it off: your knife, the steel pot, a car, fences, power lines, iron-rich rocks and magnetic phone cases.
- **Hold it flat**, screen to the sky, at waist height and away from metal. Give it a second or two to settle.
- **Calibrate** if the heading jumps around, disagrees with the sun or a real compass, or the tool asks you to: trace a slow, large figure-of-eight in the air 3–4 times, turning the phone through every angle.
- **Magnetic, true and grid north:** in Britain these are currently within about 3° of each other. Over 1 km, a 3° error puts you about 50 m off line: small for most walking, but check your position more often in mist.
- Whenever you can, check it against a baseplate compass ([[a:map-compass-basics]]).

## GPS
- GPS **doesn't need a SIM, data or mobile signal**: it only listens to satellites. Airplane mode can stay on.
- **First fix (cold start):** after the phone has been off for a while or moved far, finding satellites can take **several minutes**, occasionally more than ten. Help it: get into the open, away from cliffs, dense trees and buildings; keep the phone still, screen up; don't keep restarting. Later fixes are quicker.
- **Accuracy** shows as ± metres. In the open it's typically within about 5–10 m; under trees, in narrow valleys or beside crags it can be much worse. Wait for the figure to settle before you write anything down.
- **Grid reference:** [[t:gps]] shows your OS grid reference. A **6-figure** reference (NY 215 072) is good to 100 m; an **8-figure** one (NY 2154 0726) to 10 m ([[a:grid-references]]). Write it down with the time, on paper or in [[t:notebook]].
- **Waypoints:** save one before you leave anywhere you might need to find again: your camp, a water source, a casualty, a road. The tool then gives the **distance and bearing back** to it. That's a straight line: cliffs, rivers and bog may be in the way, so plan the route on the map.
- **Turn GPS off** once you have your fix. It's one of the biggest battery drains.

## The map
- [[t:map]] is an offline OS-style map of Great Britain: no signal needed.
- **Locate me** puts your GPS position on the map once. **Follow mode** keeps GPS and the screen on to track you, which drains the battery fast: use it only for short, tricky sections, then turn it off.
- Zoom out to see the bigger picture: streams, paths, roads, buildings and which way the ground falls ([[a:contours-terrain]]).
- Small paths, new forestry tracks and recent changes may be missing. Trust what you see on the ground.

## Step counter
- [[t:pace]] counts steps to estimate distance when you can't or won't use GPS: in mist on a bearing, at night, or to save battery.
- Calibrate it by walking a measured 100 m (from the map or two GPS fixes). Your stride shortens uphill, on rough ground and with a load. See [[a:naismith-pacing]].

## Battery cost and limits
| Feature | Battery use |
|---|---|
| Compass | Low |
| One GPS fix, then off | Moderate |
| Map with follow mode on | High: screen and GPS both running |
| Screen brightness | The biggest single drain |

- **Stop, switch on, fix, note, switch off.** Navigate between checks with the map, a compass and the ground.
- Keep the phone **off between checks**, warm in an inside pocket, and in a plastic bag in rain ([[a:batteries-electronics]]).
- Cold can shut the phone down suddenly, even when it shows charge. Warm it in a pocket and it will usually recover.
- Phones fail: drops, water, cold, flat batteries. **Always carry a paper map and a baseplate compass**, and know how to use them.

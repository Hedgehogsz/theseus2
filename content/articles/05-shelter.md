# Shelter. In the UK, wind and wet are the main threats.

=== shelter-priorities
title: Priorities and the rule of threes
summary: Why shelter comes before water and food in Britain, and how to decide what to do first.
tags: rule of threes, priorities, shelter first, exposure, protection, location, water, food
icon: list-ordered
---
!quick
1. **Protection** first: first aid, then out of the **wind and wet**, off the cold ground.
2. **Location:** let people know where you are; signal; stay findable.
3. **Water:** boil it; drink regularly.
4. **Food:** last. Weeks away from mattering.
!end

## The rule of threes
About **3 minutes** without air (or with catastrophic bleeding), **3 hours** without shelter in harsh weather, **3 days** without water, **3 weeks** without food. The numbers are rough, but the order holds.

## In the UK
- The British climate is mild but **wet and windy**. Exposure at 5 °C with rain and a breeze can kill in hours. See [[a:cold-and-wet]].
- Choose the quickest effective shelter first: get **out of the wind** (behind a wall, boulder or bank, or among dense trees), put on all your layers, sit on insulation. Improve the shelter afterwards.
- Natural shelters (debris huts) take **hours** to build well; start long before dark.
- Water is rarely far away in Britain; boil it ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]).

=== site-selection
title: Choosing a shelter site
summary: Spot the hazards: widowmaker branches, flood plains, cold hollows, exposed ridges. Pick a safe, dry, sheltered spot.
tags: site, camp, widowmaker, dead branches, flood, frost hollow, ash dieback, wind
icon: map-pinned
---
!quick
1. **Look up:** no dead branches or dead or leaning trees within falling distance (widowmakers). Beware **ash dieback** and beech limbs.
2. **Look down:** not in a hollow, dry stream bed or flood plain. Choose slightly raised, well-drained, flat ground.
3. **Wind:** in the lee of a slope, wood or wall, but not at the very bottom of a valley (cold air pools there).
4. **Near** fuel and water, but at least 30 m from water. Visible to rescuers.
!end

## Hazards
- **Widowmakers:** dead branches can fall in wind, rain or with no warning. Beech drops large live limbs in summer. Many UK ash trees are dying of **ash dieback**: brittle, with dead crowns. Pitch away from them.
- **Water:** rivers can rise a metre or more overnight. Look for flood debris lines on banks and in branches.
- **Frost hollows:** cold air drains downhill; valley bottoms can be several degrees colder than the slope above.
- **Exposed ridges and summits:** wind and lightning.
- **Rockfall:** below cliffs and scree.
- **Ant nests, wasp nests, animal paths** and tick-heavy bracken.
- **Livestock** fields: cattle trample tents and tarps.

## Good signs
- Gentle slope for drainage, firm dry ground, natural windbreak, plenty of dead fuel and insulation material (leaves, bracken), and a view out for signalling.

=== debris-hut
title: Debris hut
summary: A warm, rain-shedding natural shelter made from a ridge pole, a ribbed frame and a thick pile of leaves. No tarp needed.
tags: debris hut, natural shelter, leaves, ridgepole, insulation, survival shelter
icon: trees
---
!quick
1. **Ridge pole:** a strong pole a little longer than you, one end raised to hip height on a stump, fork or tripod, the other on the ground.
2. **Ribs:** lean sticks along both sides at a steep angle, close together, leaving a small entrance at the high end.
3. **Cover** with a mesh of twigs, then **pile on leaves, bracken and debris at least an arm's length (60 cm+) thick**.
4. **Stuff the inside** with dry leaves; crawl in feet first; plug the door with a pile of leaves.
!end

## Building tips
- Lie down beside the frame to check its size: just big enough for you. Small spaces stay warm.
- Steep sides shed rain. Thick debris is the insulation **and** the waterproofing: the thicker, the better (it settles, so add more).
- Lay a final layer of branches over the top to stop the wind blowing debris away.
- The inside bed should be at least 15–20 cm of compressed dry leaves: see [[a:insulation-ground]].
- It takes **2–3 hours** of hard work. Start well before dark.

## Notes
- Use **dead** material. Get the landowner's permission to build, and take it apart afterwards.
- Check for widowmakers above ([[a:site-selection]]).

- Woodlore / Ray Mears: Natural shelters
- Scouts UK: Bivouac and shelter building

=== lean-to
title: Lean-to shelter
summary: A quick open-fronted shelter with a ridge pole between two trees and a thatched roof, used with a fire in front.
tags: lean-to, shelter, ridge pole, thatch, reflector fire, tarp
icon: house
---
!quick
1. Lash a **ridge pole** between two trees at about chest height.
2. Lean poles against it at **45–60°** on the **windward** side, so the back faces the wind.
3. **Thatch** with bracken, leafy branches or spruce boughs from the bottom up, overlapping like roof tiles.
4. Build a **long fire** about 1 m in front with a reflector wall beyond it.
!end

## Notes
- The steeper the roof, the better it sheds rain (but the less space inside).
- Close the ends with branches and debris to stop draughts.
- A tarp makes a much faster lean-to: tie its top edge to the ridgeline and peg the back edge to the ground ([[a:tarp-rigging]]).
- Thick ground insulation is still essential ([[a:insulation-ground]]).

## Sources
- Scouts UK: Shelter building
- Woodlore / Ray Mears: Lean-to shelters

=== tarp-a-frame
title: A-frame tarp shelter
summary: The most weatherproof simple tarp pitch: a ridgeline between two trees with both sides pegged low into the wind.
tags: tarp, A-frame, ridgeline, pitch, rain, wind
icon: tent
---
!quick
1. Tie a **tight ridgeline** between two trees, about waist to chest height (lower in wind).
2. Drape the tarp over it, centred.
3. **Peg or tie both sides down low**, close to the ground, using guy lines with [[a:taut-line-hitch|taut-line hitches]].
4. Point one closed end into the wind, or pitch it lower so the edges touch the ground.
!end

## Tips
- Use a [[a:truckers-hitch]] to get the ridgeline drum-tight; tension the tarp along the line with [[a:prusik]] loops.
- **Drip lines:** tie a short string on the ridgeline just outside the tarp so rain running along the line drips off rather than inside.
- Steep sides shed rain and wind; a low pitch is warmer.
- Put a groundsheet (or a layer of bracken) inside, with the edges turned up.

=== insulation-ground
title: Insulation from the ground
summary: The ground steals heat faster than air. Put a thick, dry layer underneath you before anything else.
tags: insulation, ground, conduction, bracken, heather, leaves, mat, bed
icon: layers
---
!quick
1. **Never sit or lie directly on the ground** in cold or wet weather.
2. Use a mat, rucksack, rope coil, or a **15–30 cm compressed layer** of dry bracken, heather, leaves, grass or spruce boughs.
3. Keep it **dry**: build it on a raised base of sticks if the ground is wet.
4. Insulate injured people first, before anything else.
!end

## Why
Heat flows into cold, wet ground by **conduction** far faster than into air. A thin layer only stops the cold once it is thick enough not to compress flat. Press down with your fist: if you feel the ground, add more.

## Materials
- Dead [[s:bracken]] (excellent and abundant), [[s:heather]] (springy), dry leaves (beech and oak), dry grass, conifer boughs, moss (if dry), rucksack, spare clothing, rope.
- Avoid ivy, bracken spores in late summer, and nests of ants and ticks. Shake material out.

=== wind-snow-shelter
title: Wind, rain and snow considerations
summary: How weather changes your shelter: lowering profiles in wind, shedding rain, and handling snow in the British hills.
tags: wind, snow, rain, avalanche, snow hole, storm, shelter
icon: wind
---
!quick
1. **Wind:** pitch low, with the narrowest end into the wind; use walls, boulders, dense trees or the lee of a slope.
2. **Rain:** steep roofs, drip lines, and drainage. Don't pitch in hollows.
3. **Snow:** knock it off the tarp regularly; never shelter under a cornice or on a lee slope in the Scottish hills (avalanche risk).
4. Keep air flowing: never use fire or stoves inside enclosed shelters.
!end

## Notes
- **Gusts** in the UK hills often exceed 60 mph on ridges, even when the valley is calm.
- Snow is a good insulator, but snow holes take hours to dig, soak you, and should only be built by trained people. A survival bag in the lee of a boulder may be more realistic.
- Avalanches happen in Scotland, the Lake District and Snowdonia. Avoid steep lee slopes after snow and wind. Check the Scottish Avalanche Information Service when you can.

=== bothies
title: Bothies and other emergency shelter
summary: The UK's network of free mountain bothies, refuges and other buildings that can save a life.
tags: bothy, refuge, mountain bothies association, hut, building, shelter
icon: home
---
!quick
1. **Bothies** are simple unlocked huts in remote parts of Scotland, northern England and Wales, open to all, free.
2. Expect stone walls, a roof, maybe a fireplace, and **no** beds, water, power or fuel.
3. Shelter shown on OS maps may be a **bothy**, a **sheepfold** or **ruins**: all useful against the wind.
!end

## Bothy code (Mountain Bothies Association)
- Respect other users; leave it cleaner than you found it; take rubbish home.
- Don't use live wood; carry in fuel or use what's left. Report damage.
- Toilets: well away from the bothy and water supply.

## Other shelter
- Farm buildings and barns (ask permission if anyone is around), walls, sheepfolds, and dense conifer plantations (very sheltered from wind).
- Summit "shelters" (low stone walls) on many hills give some protection from wind.

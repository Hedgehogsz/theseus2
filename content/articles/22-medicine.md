# Medicine without help, and injured and alone. For when no professional can take over for hours or days.
# Aligned with NHS, NICE, St John Ambulance, British Red Cross, Resuscitation Council UK, UK Sepsis Trust
# and Wilderness Medical Society guidance. No prescription-drug dosing; no procedures beyond lay first aid.

=== medicine-overview
title: Medicine when help isn't coming
summary: The principles for looking after someone for days: prevent problems, keep things clean, record observations, and know when to evacuate.
tags: long-term care, remote first aid, wilderness medicine, observations, monitoring, AVPU, evacuation, no doctor, grid down
icon: briefcase-medical
needs: first-aid-kit, steel-pot, pencil-paper, this-phone
related: when-to-get-help, primary-survey, wound-care, leaving-casualty
---
!quick
1. Deal with life threats first: **bleeding, airway, breathing, cold** ([[a:primary-survey]]).
2. **Clean hands and boiled water** for everything that touches a wound or goes in a mouth.
3. **Record observations every 2–4 hours** in the [[t:casualty]] log: pulse, breathing rate, warmth, alertness (AVPU), urine.
4. **Getting worse despite your care** means you must try to reach help, by messenger or by moving them.
!end

## The four principles
1. **Prevention beats treatment.** Without a doctor, most deaths after the first day come from infection, cold, dehydration and pressure damage, not the original injury. All of these are easier to prevent than to fix.
2. **Clean water and clean hands.** Boil water for wounds and drinking ([[a:settle-strain-boil]], [[t:boil]]). Wash your hands with soap before and after every dressing, every meal and every trip to the toilet. Keep one cloth and one pot for medical use only.
3. **Watch and write it down.** Changes over hours tell you more than any single check. A written record stops you guessing at 3 am.
4. **Know your limits.** You can clean, cover, splint, warm, feed and watch. You cannot operate, set badly displaced bones, or replace lost blood. If someone needs those things, your job is to get them to help or help to them.

## Observations: what to record
Check and write down these every **2–4 hours**, and every **30–60 minutes** if they are unwell or getting worse. Use the [[t:casualty]] log (it timestamps each entry) or a notebook.

| What | How | Normal adult at rest | Worrying |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pulse | Count at the wrist or neck for 30 seconds, double it | 60–100 a minute | Over 130, or rising each check |
| Breathing rate | Count breaths for a full minute while they don't notice | 12–20 a minute | 25 or more, or under 8 |
| Temperature | Back of your hand on their forehead, chest and neck; compare with yours | Warm, dry | Very hot, or cold and clammy |
| Alertness | AVPU (below) | **A**lert, makes sense | New confusion, drowsy |
| Skin | Colour of face, lips, nail beds | Pink | Pale, grey, blue, mottled |
| Urine | How often, roughly how much, colour | Pale straw, at least every 4–6 hours | Dark, or none for 12 hours |
| Pain | Ask them to score it 0–10 | Steady or easing | Increasing, or new pain |

**AVPU:** **A**lert (eyes open, talks sensibly), responds to **V**oice, responds only to **P**ain (a firm squeeze of the ear lobe or nail bed), **U**nresponsive. Any **new confusion** counts as worse than Alert, even if their eyes are open. Children's normal pulse and breathing rates are faster: watch the trend, and how they look and behave.

Also note: what they have eaten and drunk, any vomiting or diarrhoea, medicines taken and when, and dressing changes.

!tip Reading the trend
One fast pulse after a walk to the latrine means little. A pulse that rises 10 beats a minute at every check, with faster breathing and less urine, means something is going wrong: infection, bleeding or dehydration. Act early.
!end

## Daily care routine
- **Morning and evening:** full set of observations, look at every wound and splint, fresh drinking water, a wash.
- **Fluids:** an adult needs about **2–3 litres** a day, more with fever, burns or diarrhoea. Aim for pale urine ([[a:dehydration-signs]]). Use [[a:diarrhoea-ors]] if they are losing fluid.
- **Food:** small, regular, easy to digest. Hunger matters less than water in the first few days.
- **Warmth:** an injured person lying still gets cold fast, even indoors. Insulate underneath first ([[a:insulation-ground]]).
- **Sleep and morale:** explain what you are doing and why. Fear makes pain worse.

## When you must try to get help
Staying put is usually safest, but try to reach help (send a messenger, signal, or move them if it is safe) if:
- They show any **sepsis** sign ([[a:infection-escalation]]).
- They are getting **more drowsy or confused**, especially after a head injury ([[a:head-injury]]).
- They can't keep fluids down for 12 hours, or pass **no urine for 12 hours** or more.
- Breathing is getting faster or harder, or they have chest or belly pain that is getting worse.
- A limb below a splint stays cold, pale or numb after you have loosened everything ([[a:long-splinting]]).
- A burn is large, or on the face, hands or genitals ([[a:burns-over-days]]).
- Any observation is getting worse at every check despite your care.

Weigh the trip: a sick person carried badly over rough ground in bad weather can be harmed by the journey. Sending two fit people with a written [[a:location-report]] is often better than moving the casualty. See [[a:leaving-casualty]] and [[a:when-to-get-help]].

=== wound-closure
title: Wound closure: close it or leave it open?
summary: When a wound can be held closed with strips, when it must be left open and packed, and how to care for it over days.
tags: wound closure, steri-strips, butterfly strips, stitches, sutures, gaping cut, laceration, open wound, packing, bite
icon: scissors
time: 20–40 min
level: moderate
needs: first-aid-kit, steel-pot, cotton-cloth
related: wound-care, severe-bleeding, infection-escalation, tick-removal-lyme
---
!quick
1. **Stop the bleeding**, then **irrigate** with at least 1 litre of boiled-then-cooled water.
2. **Leave it open** (packed and covered) if it is a bite, dirty, a puncture, more than about 6–12 hours old, or looks infected.
3. Only **clean, fresh, straight cuts** can be held together with strips. Leave gaps so it can drain.
4. Don't stitch. Check and redress **every 1–2 days**.
!end

!danger An infected closed wound is worse than an open one
Closing a dirty wound traps bacteria under the skin, where they can spread fast. A wound left open heals more slowly and leaves a wider scar, but it rarely kills. When in doubt, **leave it open**.
!end

## First: clean it properly
Basic cleaning is in [[a:wound-care]]. For a wound you may close, go further:
1. Wash your hands. Boil water and let it cool to warm ([[t:boil]]).
2. **Irrigate under pressure:** at least **1 litre** for a cut of a few centimetres, more if it is dirty. Pierce a clean plastic bag or bottle cap with a pin and squeeze hard from 5–10 cm away. Pressure flushes out dirt and bacteria; a gentle trickle doesn't.
3. Pick out visible grit with boiled tweezers. Rinse again.
4. Look into the wound in good light ([[t:torch]]). Can you see deep yellow fat, white tendon or bone? Can they move and feel everything beyond it? If not, it needs a surgeon: leave it open, splint it, and get help.

## Leave it open if
- It is an **animal or human bite** (including dog, cat and rat bites).
- It is **dirty**: soil, manure, rust, sea or river water, or you couldn't clean it fully.
- It is a **puncture** (nail, thorn, knife tip): deep and narrow wounds can't be cleaned inside.
- It is **more than about 6–12 hours old**. The more time has passed, the more bacteria have multiplied. Use the shorter limit for hands, feet and legs, which heal less well.
- There is a **crush injury**, or ragged edges, or a lot of dead tissue.
- It already shows **redness, swelling, heat or pus**.
- The person has diabetes or poor circulation.

## Caring for an open wound
1. After irrigating, **loosely fill** a deep or gaping wound with clean gauze or boiled-and-dried cotton cloth, slightly dampened with boiled-cooled water. Fill it, don't stuff it: it should drain, not be under pressure.
2. Cover with a dry pad and secure with a bandage or tape.
3. **Change it every 1–2 days**, or when it is soaked or smells. Soak stuck dressings off with boiled-cooled water rather than ripping them.
4. Irrigate again at each change. Healthy wounds look pink or red, moist and slowly shrink from the bottom up.

## Holding a clean wound together with strips
Only for a **clean, fresh** cut (under about 6 hours), with straight edges that come together easily, not on a joint that bends, and not under tension.
- Dry the skin around the cut well. Stick each strip on one side, gently push the edges together (not overlapping), and stick down the other side.
- Space them about **3–5 mm** apart, working from the middle out, so fluid can escape between them.
- No adhesive strips? Cut **butterfly strips** from tape: a strip about 1 cm wide with a narrow, non-sticky bridge over the wound.
- Add strips running parallel to the wound, across the ends, to stop them peeling.
- Keep it dry. Leave strips in place for **5–7 days** (up to 10–14 days over a joint or on the leg); they will start to peel off on their own.
- Don't use household superglue on wounds. Medical tissue glue is a different product.

## Why you shouldn't stitch
Stitching needs sterile kit, local anaesthetic, training and, above all, a wound that is safe to close. In amateur hands it often traps infection, damages nerves and tendons, and causes more harm than a wound left open. Strips do the same job for wounds that are safe to close, and come off easily if infection starts.

## Delayed closure
A wound left open because it was dirty can sometimes be closed with strips after **3–5 days**, if it is clean, pink and shows **no** signs of infection. If you are not sure, keep it open and let it heal from the bottom up. It will take weeks, but it will heal.

## Reopen it if
Redness spreads, the wound swells, throbs, oozes pus or smells, or they get a fever: take the strips off, let it drain, irrigate, and dress it open. Then follow [[a:infection-escalation]]. Dirty wounds also carry a tetanus risk: see [[a:wound-care]].

=== infection-escalation
title: Infection: signs and when it's getting worse
summary: Spot a wound infection early, recognise when it is spreading or becoming sepsis, and what you can do about it.
tags: infection, sepsis, cellulitis, red streaks, lymphangitis, pus, abscess, fever, septic, blood poisoning, wound infection
icon: microscope
needs: first-aid-kit, steel-pot, pencil-paper
related: wound-care, wound-closure, fever, medicine-overview
---
!quick
1. **Draw round the redness** with a pen and write the time. Check it every few hours.
2. Reopen and drain any wound with **pus**; irrigate; dress it open; **raise** the limb; rest; drink.
3. **Red streaks**, fever, or redness spreading past your line → infection is spreading: **plan to get help**.
4. **Any sepsis sign** (confused, extreme shivering, no urine, severe breathlessness, mottled skin) → **send for help now**.
!end

## Normal healing or infection?
For the first 2–3 days, a healing wound is a little pink round the edges, slightly swollen and sore, with some clear or straw-coloured fluid. That is normal. Infection usually shows from about day 2 onwards:
- **Redness** spreading out from the wound, more than about 1–2 cm.
- **Heat:** the skin round it feels warmer than the same spot on the other side.
- **Swelling** and tight, shiny skin.
- **Pain getting worse**, often throbbing, instead of easing.
- **Pus** (thick yellow, green or brown fluid) or a bad smell.

## Signs it is spreading
- **Mark the edge.** Draw round the redness with a pen and write the time next to it. If it has spread beyond the line in a few hours, the infection is winning.
- **Red streaks** running up the limb from the wound towards the armpit or groin (lymphangitis). The infection is travelling along the lymph channels.
- **Swollen, tender glands** in the armpit, groin or neck.
- **Fever**, chills, feeling generally unwell ([[a:fever]]).
- Pain far worse than the wound looks, skin that turns dusky purple or black, blisters, or a crackling feeling under the skin. These are signs of a rare but deadly deep infection: **send for help now**.

## Sepsis
Sepsis is the body's life-threatening reaction to an infection. It can follow any infected wound, a chest or urine infection, or diarrhoea. It can kill within hours.

!danger Sepsis in adults: send for help now if they have any of
- **S**lurred speech or confusion.
- **E**xtreme shivering or muscle pain.
- **P**assing no urine (in a day).
- **S**evere breathlessness.
- **I**t feels like they're going to die.
- **S**kin mottled or discoloured.
!end

!danger Sepsis in children: send for help now if a child
- Is breathing very fast, has pauses in breathing, or makes grunting noises.
- Has blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue.
- Has a rash that doesn't fade when you roll a glass over it.
- Is very drowsy, difficult to wake, floppy, or not responding normally.
- Has a weak, high-pitched cry, is not feeding, or hasn't had a wee or a wet nappy for 12 hours.
- Has a fit (seizure), or feels abnormally cold to touch.
!end

Also worrying in anyone: breathing rate **25 or more** a minute, pulse **over 130**, or a temperature that feels cold and clammy rather than hot. Older people and the very young often don't run a fever; new confusion may be the only sign.

## What you can do
- **Let pus out.** If the wound was closed with strips, take them off. Gently open the wound edges, let it drain and irrigate with plenty of boiled-cooled water ([[a:wound-closure]]).
- **Don't cut into a swelling yourself.** A closed lump of pus (abscess) often comes to a head and bursts on its own: hold a cloth soaked in warm boiled water (comfortably hot, not scalding) on it for 15–20 minutes, 3–4 times a day. When it bursts, let it drain, irrigate, and dress it open.
- **Clean and dress** at least once a day. Wash your hands before and after. Burn or bury old dressings.
- **Raise** the infected part above the heart. Rest it: movement spreads infection. A splint helps for a hand or arm.
- **Fluids:** keep them drinking, aiming for pale urine. Fever and infection increase water needs.
- **Warmth and rest.** Paracetamol for pain and fever, if you carry it ([[a:fever]]).
- **Record** temperature, pulse, breathing rate and alertness every 2–4 hours in the [[t:casualty]] log, plus how far the redness has spread.

## Deciding to evacuate
Antibiotics are the treatment for spreading infection, and they need a doctor. Get help, or move them towards it, if:
- Redness keeps spreading past your pen line, or red streaks appear.
- They develop a fever with a spreading infection.
- The wound is on the hand, face, near a joint, or from a bite.
- They show **any** sepsis sign: that is an emergency, so send for help at once ([[a:when-to-get-help]]).

Infection that is clearly getting better (redness shrinking, less pain, no fever) can be managed where you are.

=== long-splinting
title: Splints for days and weeks
summary: Keep a splinted limb safe for days: padding, circulation checks, swelling, pressure sores, re-padding and the right position.
tags: splint, long-term splinting, fracture care, circulation, CSM, swelling, compartment syndrome, broken bone, cast, immobilise
icon: bone-fracture
time: 30–60 min to re-pad
level: moderate
needs: first-aid-kit, cord, warm-layers, survival-bag
related: fractures-splinting, sprains-strains, pain-without-drugs, moving-with-injury
---
!quick
1. **Check CSM** beyond the splint every 2–4 hours: **C**irculation (colour, warmth), **S**ensation (feeling), **M**ovement.
2. Cold, pale, blue or numb fingers or toes → **loosen the ties at once** and re-check in 15 minutes.
3. **Raise** the limb for the first 2–3 days; it will swell.
4. **Re-pad** daily: look for red, sore spots over bones.
!end

How to make a splint in the first place is in [[a:fractures-splinting]]. This page is about keeping it working for days.

## The CSM check
Do it after splinting, after any re-tightening, and every **2–4 hours** for the first 2–3 days (hourly if swelling is bad). Compare with the uninjured side. Write it in the [[t:casualty]] log.
- **Circulation:** fingers or toes pink and warm. Press a nail for 2 seconds: colour should return within **2 seconds**.
- **Sensation:** touch each finger or toe without them watching. Can they tell which one? Any new numbness or tingling?
- **Movement:** can they wiggle the fingers or toes? Gently, without moving the break.

!danger Pain out of proportion
Severe pain that keeps increasing despite a good splint, pain when you gently stretch the fingers or toes, and a tight, hard swelling of the limb can mean pressure building inside the muscle (compartment syndrome). Loosen everything, keep the limb at heart level (not raised high), and **send for help urgently**. It can destroy the limb within hours.
!end

## Swelling
Swelling peaks around **24–72 hours** after an injury. A splint that fitted on day one can strangle the limb on day two.
- **Raise** the limb above the heart when resting: a leg on a rucksack, an arm in a high sling or propped on a jacket.
- **Cold:** a cloth soaked in cold stream water, wrapped, for 15–20 minutes every 2–3 hours for the first 2 days.
- Tie the splint with broad bands, not thin cord. Tight enough to hold, loose enough to slide a finger under.
- **Loosen and re-tie** if the skin bulges between ties, or CSM gets worse.
- Remove rings, watches and tight boots early, before swelling traps them.

## Pressure sores and padding
A splint pressing on the same spot for hours can cause a pressure sore, especially over bony points: ankle bones, heel, knee, wrist, elbow.
- **Pad everything:** spare clothing, foam mat, moss inside a cloth, dry grass in a sock. Fill the hollows (behind the knee, under the wrist and ankle) so the limb rests evenly.
- **Re-pad every day.** Take it apart one section at a time while someone holds the limb still, so the break never loses support. Check the skin underneath: redness that doesn't fade within 30 minutes of the pressure coming off is an early sore. Add more padding round it.
- Keep the skin **dry and clean**. Wet padding rubs and causes sores; change it.

## Functional position
If a limb has to be held for days or weeks, hold it the way it would be most useful if it stiffened:
- **Hand and wrist:** as if holding a can of drink: wrist slightly bent back, fingers gently curled, thumb out. A rolled sock in the palm helps.
- **Elbow:** bent to about a right angle, in a sling.
- **Knee:** almost straight, with a slight bend (a rolled jacket under it), never locked.
- **Ankle:** foot at a right angle to the leg, not pointing down.

Only straighten a limb if it has no pulse or feeling beyond the injury and you are well trained: otherwise splint it as you find it.

## Improvised splints that last
- **Rigid:** a rolled foam sleeping mat (excellent for ankles and wrists), trekking poles, straight sticks padded in cloth, a rucksack frame stay.
- **Body splints:** leg to leg, or arm to chest. Good for a short time, but harder to manage for days.
- **Slings:** triangular bandage, the bottom of a jacket pinned up, a belt or cord loop round the neck with padding.
- Tie knots on the side away from the injury, and where the person can reach to loosen them.

## Movement
- Keep the joints **not** in the splint moving: wiggle fingers or toes, move shoulders and hips, several times an hour. It reduces stiffness, swelling and clots.
- A splinted lower leg plus crutches can let some people move: see [[a:moving-with-injury]].

## When the limb looks wrong
Get help, or move towards it, if:
- CSM stays poor after loosening everything for 15 minutes.
- The limb below the injury is pale, blue, cold or numb.
- Pain keeps increasing over hours.
- There is an open wound over the break: high infection risk. Keep it covered and clean, and watch for [[a:infection-escalation]].
- Thigh or pelvis injury: risk of serious blood loss. These need rescue, not days in the field.

=== burns-over-days
title: Caring for burns over days
summary: How to dress, clean and watch a burn for days when no hospital is available, and which burns are serious.
tags: burn care, burns dressing, blisters, scald, burn infection, fluids, rule of palms, cling film
icon: calendar-clock
time: 15–30 min per dressing change
level: moderate
needs: first-aid-kit, steel-pot, cotton-cloth
related: burns, infection-escalation, diarrhoea-ors, shock
---
!quick
1. Still in the first 3 hours? **Cool it for 20 minutes** with cool running water, then cover loosely ([[a:burns]]).
2. **Change the dressing every 1–2 days**: wash hands, rinse with boiled-cooled water, cover again with something that won't stick.
3. **Don't burst blisters.** Raise burnt limbs. Keep them drinking.
4. Large burn, face, hands, airway, or infection signs → **get help**.
!end

First aid for a fresh burn is in [[a:burns]]. Cooling is still worth doing up to **3 hours** after the burn. This page is about what comes next.

## How serious is it?
Use the casualty's own **palm with fingers** as a measure: it is about **1%** of their body surface.

| Burn | What it means |
|---|---|
| Red, painful, no blisters (like sunburn) | Superficial. Heals in about a week. |
| Blisters, pink or red, very painful | Partial thickness. Heals in 2–3 weeks if kept clean. |
| White, waxy, leathery, brown or charred, little pain | Deep. Needs hospital; won't heal well on its own. |

**Serious, and you should try to get help** if any of these apply:
- Blistered or deeper burns larger than the casualty's palm, or any deep burn.
- About **10 palms** or more in an adult, or **5** in a child: the body loses a lot of fluid and they may go into [[a:shock]].
- Burns to the **face, neck, hands, feet, genitals** or over joints, or going all the way round a limb or the chest.
- **Airway:** soot round the nose or mouth, singed nostril hair, hoarse voice, cough, noisy breathing. Swelling can block the airway hours later: sit them up and **send for help now**.
- Electrical or chemical burns; babies, older people, and anyone with other injuries or illness.

## Dressing changes
Change the dressing every **1–2 days**, or sooner if it is soaked, dirty or smells.
1. Wash your hands with soap, and boil water and let it cool to lukewarm ([[t:boil]]).
2. Soak off the old dressing with the water. Don't rip it off: that tears new skin.
3. Gently rinse the burn by pouring water over it. Don't scrub. Pat the skin around it dry.
4. Cover it with a **non-stick** dressing if you have one. Cling film or a clean plastic bag is fine for the first day or two but holds moisture, so change it at least daily. A thin smear of petroleum jelly under a clean cloth stops cloth sticking.
5. Add a loose, dry pad on top and bandage lightly. Leave room for swelling.
6. Burnt fingers: dress each one separately so they don't grow together.

## Blisters
- **Don't burst them.** The blister roof is a sterile dressing.
- If a blister bursts on its own, rinse it and cover it. Leave the loose skin in place unless it is dirty.

## Swelling, movement and pain
- **Raise** burnt arms and legs above the heart for the first 2–3 days.
- Move burnt hands and joints gently through their full range several times a day once the pain allows, or they stiffen as they heal.
- Burns are very painful. Cooling, a covered wound (air on a burn hurts), raising it and paracetamol or ibuprofen, if you carry them, all help. See [[a:pain-without-drugs]].

## Fluids
Burns leak fluid through the damaged skin, and bigger burns leak a lot.
- Keep them drinking **little and often**. For burns larger than a few palms, use an oral rehydration drink ([[a:diarrhoea-ors]]) rather than plain water.
- Aim for pale urine every few hours. Record drinks and urine in the [[t:casualty]] log.
- Drowsy, vomiting, not passing urine, cold and clammy: they are going into shock and need rescue.
- Keep them **warm**. People with large burns lose heat fast.

## Infection
Infection is the main danger after the first day. Watch for:
- Redness spreading out from the edge of the burn, increasing pain or swelling.
- Green or yellow discharge, or a bad smell.
- Fever, shivering, or feeling unwell.
- A burn that was healing turning deeper or darker.

See [[a:infection-escalation]]. A burn that is infected or not healing after **2 weeks** needs a doctor.

## Healing
New skin is pink, thin and fragile. Keep it covered from sun and rubbing, and keep it moisturised once healed. It itches: don't scratch.

=== fever
title: Fever
summary: How to tell someone has a fever without a thermometer, keep them comfortable and hydrated, and spot the warning signs.
tags: fever, high temperature, temperature, hot, shivering, chills, paracetamol, ibuprofen, child fever, feverish
icon: thermometer
needs: steel-bottle, cotton-cloth, warm-layers, first-aid-kit
related: infection-escalation, heat-illness, dehydration-signs, when-youre-ill
---
!quick
1. **Fluids:** small drinks often, aiming for pale urine. Rest.
2. **Comfortable, not cold:** light layers; take off blankets if hot, add them if shivering. No cold baths or cold sponging.
3. Paracetamol or ibuprofen **only if you carry them**: follow the packet exactly.
4. **Red flags** (confused, stiff neck, rash that doesn't fade, struggling to breathe, no urine) → **send for help now**.
!end

A fever is a temperature of **38 °C** or more. It is the body fighting an infection, and on its own it is rarely dangerous. What matters is the cause and how unwell the person is.

## Checking without a thermometer
- Place the **back of your hand** on their forehead, chest or back, then on your own. A fever feels clearly hotter. (Hands and feet can be cold even during a fever.)
- Other signs: flushed face, hot dry skin or sweating, glassy eyes, shivering or chills, aches, headache, a fast pulse and fast breathing.
- The **pulse** usually rises by about 10 beats a minute for each degree of fever. Record pulse, breathing rate and how they feel every 2–4 hours in the [[t:casualty]] log.

## Fever or heat illness?
| | Fever | Heat illness |
|---|---|---|
| Comes on | Over hours or days, often with cough, sore throat, diarrhoea, an infected wound or urine pain | After hard work or exposure to heat |
| Shivering | Common as it rises | Not usual |
| What to do | Comfort, fluids, rest | **Cool actively and fast** ([[a:heat-illness]]) |

If someone has been working hard in the heat and becomes confused, hot and unwell, treat it as heatstroke first.

## What to do
- **Fluids:** fever increases water loss. Give small, frequent drinks: water, weak sweet tea, broth, or an oral rehydration drink ([[a:diarrhoea-ors]]). Check urine colour ([[a:dehydration-signs]]).
- **Rest** in a sheltered, airy place. Food when they want it.
- **Layers:** when the fever is rising they feel cold and shiver: cover them until the shivering stops. When they feel hot and sweaty, take layers off and let air reach the skin. Change sweaty clothes to stop them chilling at night.
- **Cooling:** a cloth dampened with lukewarm water on the forehead is comforting. Don't use cold water, cold sponging or cold baths: they cause shivering, which raises the temperature again.
- **Find the cause:** look at every wound, ask about cough, breathlessness, sore throat, ears, urine pain, diarrhoea and tick bites ([[a:tick-removal-lyme]]).

## Medicines, if you carry them
You don't have to treat a fever. Give medicine to make someone more comfortable, not to bring a number down.
- **Paracetamol (adults):** follow the packet. For standard 500 mg tablets that is usually **2 tablets, up to 4 times in 24 hours, at least 4 hours apart, no more than 8 tablets in 24 hours**. Adults under 50 kg may need less: check the leaflet. Don't take any other medicine containing paracetamol at the same time.
- **Ibuprofen (adults):** follow the packet. For 200 mg tablets that is usually **1–2 tablets up to 3 times a day, with food, at least 4 hours apart, no more than 6 tablets in 24 hours**. Avoid it if they are dehydrated, have asthma made worse by ibuprofen, a stomach ulcer, or are pregnant.
- **Children:** only use children's medicines (liquid or soluble), with the dose on the packet for their **age**. Never give adult tablets broken up, and never give **aspirin** to anyone under 16. Don't give paracetamol and ibuprofen at the same time; only switch to the other if the first hasn't helped by the time the next dose is due. Don't give ibuprofen to a child with chickenpox.

## Children
- Don't over-wrap a feverish child or strip them off entirely: dress them normally for the conditions.
- Offer regular drinks; breastfeed as normal. Check for dry nappies.
- Check on them during the night.
- Babies under **3 months** with a temperature of 38 °C or more, and babies 3–6 months with 39 °C or more, need a doctor urgently.

## Red flags: send for help now
- Confusion, slurred speech, very drowsy or hard to wake.
- **Stiff neck**, dislike of bright light, severe headache.
- A **rash that doesn't fade** when you press a glass against it.
- Fast or difficult breathing, grunting, chest pain.
- No urine for 12 hours, or can't keep any fluids down.
- A fit (seizure) ([[a:seizures]]).
- Mottled, blue or grey skin; cold hands and feet with a high temperature.
- Any sepsis sign ([[a:infection-escalation]]).
- A fever lasting more than **5 days**, or one that came back after getting better.

=== rewarming
title: Rewarming someone over hours
summary: How to rewarm a hypothermic person safely over hours: gentle handling, a hypothermia wrap, heat to the core, and the dangers of afterdrop.
tags: rewarming, hypothermia wrap, burrito, afterdrop, warming, cold casualty, heat packs, body heat, survival bag
icon: heater
time: hours
level: moderate
needs: survival-bag, warm-layers, steel-bottle, tarp
related: hypothermia, cold-and-wet, sleeping-warm, insulation-ground
---
!quick
1. **Handle gently and keep them lying flat.** Get them out of wind and rain, onto insulation.
2. **Wet clothes off, dry on**, then wrap: insulation underneath, sleeping bag or layers, a **vapour barrier** (plastic or survival bag), then a windproof outer.
3. **Wrapped** warm bottles or heat packs on the **chest, armpits and back**. Never on bare skin, never on limbs.
4. Warm sweet drinks **only if fully alert**. No alcohol. Don't rub limbs.
!end

Recognising hypothermia, and the first actions, are in [[a:hypothermia]]. This page is about the hours that follow, when nobody is coming soon.

## How cold are they?
| Stage | Signs | Can they rewarm themselves? |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | Shivering hard, alert, clumsy hands, grumpy | Yes, with shelter, dry clothes, food and drink |
| Moderate | Confused, slurred speech, stumbling, shivering may be weaker | No: they need external heat and careful handling |
| Severe | Shivering stopped, very drowsy or unresponsive, slow breathing | No: life-threatening. Send for help. |

Shivering is the body's own furnace. A person who is shivering hard and can eat should be helped to keep shivering: fuel it with food and sugary drinks.

## Handle them like glass
A very cold heart is irritable: rough handling, sitting them up suddenly or making them walk can trigger a cardiac arrest.
- Keep moderate and severe casualties **lying flat**. Don't let them walk, even if they say they can.
- Move them as one piece, gently, onto insulation.
- Check breathing and pulse for up to a full **minute**: they may be very slow. If there is no breathing, start [[a:cpr-adult]] ([[t:cpr]]).

## Build a hypothermia wrap
Work in shelter: a tent, tarp, the lee of a wall, or a building.
1. **Insulate from the ground** first: a foam mat, rucksacks, a thick layer of bracken or heather ([[a:insulation-ground]]). The ground takes heat faster than the air.
2. **Wet clothes off.** Cut them off if needed, one area at a time, to limit exposure. If you have nothing dry to replace them with, leave them on and wrap over them in a vapour barrier.
3. **Dry layers on**, including a hat. Cover the head and neck.
4. **Sleeping bag** or blankets, layers of spare clothing.
5. **Vapour barrier:** a survival bag, bin bag or tarp wrapped round them, inside or outside the insulation. It stops evaporation, which is a big heat loss.
6. **Windproof outer** layer, leaving only the face open.

## Adding heat
- **Where:** the **chest, armpits and back** (and groin if you can). This warms the core. Heat on the arms and legs sends cold blood back to the heart.
- **Warm bottle:** fill your steel bottle with hot (not boiling) water, close it tightly, and wrap it in **two layers** of cloth or a sock. Test it on your own inner wrist for a minute. Cold skin burns easily and they may not feel it.
- **Chemical heat packs:** wrap them; never against bare skin.
- **Body-to-body:** a warm, dry person inside the sleeping bag with them, skin to skin, chest to back. It helps, especially for mild cases, though it is slower than heat packs. Don't let the rescuer get cold.
- **Not:** a fire right against them, a hot bath or shower, or hot rocks against the skin.

## Food and drink
- **Only if fully alert**, able to hold the cup and swallow normally: warm, sweet drinks and high-energy food. Sugar fuels shivering.
- Drowsy or confused: **nothing by mouth**. They could choke.
- **No alcohol.** It opens skin blood vessels and loses heat.

## Afterdrop
After you stop the cold exposure, the core temperature can carry on falling for a while as cold blood from the limbs returns to the heart. This is why:
- You **never rub** limbs or make them exercise.
- You warm the **core**, not the hands and feet.
- You keep checking them, even when they seem to be improving.

## How long?
Rewarming a mild case can take **1–2 hours**; a moderate case many hours. Signs it is working: shivering becomes stronger then eases, they become more alert, speech clears, skin pinks up. Record alertness (AVPU), breathing, pulse and shivering every 15–30 minutes in the [[t:casualty]] log.

## Afterwards
- Keep them in the wrap until they are warm, alert and shivering has stopped. Then let them sit up slowly.
- Anyone who reached moderate or severe hypothermia should **rest for at least a day** and see a doctor when possible. Don't let them walk out the same day.
- Check hands and feet for [[a:cold-injuries]].
- Look after the others: if one person got this cold, others are probably cold too.

- British Red Cross: Hypothermia

- Wilderness Medical Society: Clinical practice guidelines for the out-of-hospital evaluation and treatment of accidental hypothermia (wms.org)

=== pain-without-drugs
title: Pain relief without drugs
summary: Practical ways to reduce pain when you have no painkillers left: immobilising, positioning, cold and heat, breathing and sleep.
tags: pain, pain relief, painkillers, no medicine, comfort, cold pack, heat, breathing, distraction, reassurance
icon: hand-heart
needs: cotton-cloth, warm-layers, steel-bottle
related: long-splinting, sprains-strains, fractures-splinting, cant-be-moved
---
!quick
1. **Keep it still:** splint or support the painful part. Movement is the biggest cause of pain.
2. **Position** them as comfortably as possible, and **raise** an injured limb.
3. **Cold** for fresh injuries (first 2–3 days), **warmth** for stiff muscles later. Always wrapped.
4. **Calm voice, slow breathing**, warmth and sleep. Fear and cold make pain worse.
!end

Pain is a warning, so never ignore new or worsening pain: check the cause first. Pain that keeps increasing despite good care can be a sign of something serious ([[a:infection-escalation]], [[a:long-splinting]]).

## Immobilise
- A well-padded splint is often the most effective painkiller there is for a broken bone or bad sprain ([[a:fractures-splinting]]).
- **Slings** take the weight off a painful arm, shoulder or collarbone.
- Support the joints above and below the injury.
- Fill gaps with padding so the limb isn't hanging or pressing on one point.

## Position
- **Injured limb:** raised above the heart on a rucksack or rolled jacket. It reduces swelling and throbbing.
- **Back pain:** lying on the side with knees bent and padding between the knees, or on the back with knees raised over a rolled sleeping bag.
- **Belly pain:** knees bent, lying on the side or back.
- **Chest pain or broken ribs:** sitting up, leaning against a support. Don't strap the ribs. Ask them to take **a few deep breaths and a gentle cough every hour**, holding a folded jacket against the sore side; this prevents a chest infection.
- **Headache:** quiet, dim, sheltered, with something under the head.

## Cold
Best for fresh injuries in the first **48–72 hours**: sprains, bruises, burns after the first cooling.
- Cold stream water in a bag, a cloth soaked in cold water, snow in a bag.
- **Wrap it** in cloth. Never on bare skin.
- **15–20 minutes** at a time, every 2–3 hours. Stop if the skin goes white or numb.
- Not on someone who is cold or shivering, and not on frostbite.

## Warmth
Best after the first few days, for stiff muscles, muscle spasm and back pain.
- Your steel bottle filled with warm water and **wrapped in cloth**, or a warm (not hot) stone in a sock.
- 15–20 minutes at a time. Check the skin.
- **Not** on a fresh injury (it increases swelling), not on numb skin, and not on an infected area.
- General warmth helps everyone: cold muscles tense up and hurt more.

## Breathing and the mind
Fear, tension and feeling helpless make pain much worse. These things really do reduce pain:
- **Slow breathing:** in through the nose for a count of 4, out through the mouth for a count of 6–8. Keep going for 5 minutes. Do it with them.
- **Relaxing muscles:** starting at the feet, tense each group for 5 seconds, then let it go. Skip the injured part.
- **Distraction:** talk, tell stories, plan meals, sing, play word games. Give them a task they can do lying down.
- **Information and reassurance:** explain what you are doing, what happens next, and that pain usually eases after the first days.
- **Control:** let them choose small things: when you change the dressing, which drink they have. Feeling in control reduces pain.

## Sleep
Pain is worse when tired, and sleep is when the body heals.
- Warm, insulated, dark and quiet. Earplugs or a hat over the ears.
- Support every painful part with padding before they settle.
- Plan dressing changes and checks for the day, not the night, unless they are getting worse.

## Avoid
- **Alcohol:** it worsens bleeding, dehydration and hypothermia, and interferes with sleep.
- **Massage** of a fresh injury, and heat in the first 2–3 days (these increase swelling).
- **Wild plants** as painkillers: willow bark, poppies and others are unreliable and some are dangerous. See [[a:poisoning]].
- Taking more painkiller than the packet says, or two products that both contain paracetamol.

If you do carry paracetamol or ibuprofen, adult doses are in [[a:fever]]. Combined with the steps on this page, they work much better than alone.

- NHS: Sprains and strains; Back pain; Broken ribs (nhs.uk)
- St John Ambulance: Strains and sprains; Broken bones (sja.org.uk)
- British Red Cross: First aid
- NICE CKS: Rib fracture; Sprains and strains (cks.nice.org.uk)
- Wilderness Medical Society: Clinical practice guidelines (wms.org)

=== cant-be-moved
title: Caring for someone who can't be moved
summary: Keep someone who can't be moved alive and comfortable for days: shelter, insulation, turning, fluids, toileting, hygiene and monitoring.
tags: casualty care, can't move, spinal injury, pressure sores, bed sores, turning, nursing, long wait, bedpan, toileting
icon: bed-single
time: days
level: hard
needs: tarp, survival-bag, warm-layers, steel-pot, pencil-paper, this-phone
related: medicine-overview, insulation-ground, signalling-rescue, leaving-casualty
---
!quick
1. **Bring the shelter to them:** tarp over, insulation underneath, windbreak around.
2. **Turn them every 2 hours** (unless you suspect a spinal injury) and pad bony points to prevent pressure sores.
3. **2–3 litres** of fluid a day, a toilet plan, and a wash. Record observations in the [[t:casualty]] log.
4. **Keep signalling** at set times, and send a messenger if you can.
!end

Someone may not be movable because of a suspected spinal, pelvis or thigh injury, a head injury, severe illness, or simply because there is no way to carry them safely over the ground.

## Shelter around them
- Rig a **tarp** over them, low on the windward side ([[a:tarp-rigging]]). Use walls, boulders or a vehicle as a windbreak.
- **Insulation underneath** is the priority: slide a foam mat, rucksacks, coats or a thick layer of dry bracken under them ([[a:insulation-ground]]). With a suspected spinal injury, slide it in from the side while others hold the head in line, or build up insulation round them and wait for more hands.
- Wrap them in a survival bag and layers, with a hat. Someone lying still gets cold even on a mild day ([[a:rewarming]]).
- Light a fire safely to the side if you can, with a reflector ([[a:fire-safety]]).

## Preventing pressure sores
Lying still on one spot cuts off blood to the skin. Sores can start within **a few hours** on the hips, base of the spine, heels, ankles, elbows, shoulder blades and back of the head.
- **Turn them every 2 hours** day and night: on the back, then one side, then the other. On the side, tilt them about 30° (not fully on the hip bone) and prop the back with a rolled jacket or rucksack.
- Pad between the knees and ankles, and lift the **heels** clear by padding under the calves.
- Keep the skin **clean and dry**: sweat, urine and wet clothes make sores much more likely.
- At each turn, look at the bony points. Red skin that doesn't fade within 30 minutes of the pressure coming off is the first sign: keep weight off it.
- Smooth out wrinkles and crumbs from under them.

!warn Suspected spinal injury
If they had a big fall or impact and have neck or back pain, numbness, tingling or weakness: **don't turn them** on your own. A safe log roll needs about **4 people**, one holding the head in line. If you are alone or two, keep them still, pad every pressure point well, and change the padding under the heels and around the hips by sliding, not lifting. Only roll them yourself if their airway is at risk (vomiting) or they are in danger.
!end

## Breathing and circulation
- If they are conscious, ask them to take **10 deep breaths and cough every hour** while awake: lying still causes chest infections.
- Move the **ankles and toes** and any uninjured joints regularly: circles, pointing and flexing. This helps prevent clots.

## Fluids and food
- Aim for **2–3 litres** of fluid a day for an adult, more in fever or heat. Pale urine means enough ([[a:dehydration-signs]]).
- Raise the head and shoulders slightly to drink, if no spinal injury. With a suspected spinal injury, use a tube or a bottle with a sports cap, or a soaked cloth, and give small sips with them turned slightly if possible.
- Only give drinks to someone who is **fully alert** and can swallow. Small, simple meals when they want them.

## Toileting
- **Urine:** a wide-mouthed bottle for men; a shallow container or a plastic bag lined with absorbent cloth for women. Measure or estimate how much and note the colour.
- **Bowels:** roll them onto their side onto a plastic bag or sheet with cloth inside, then roll back. Clean the skin front to back with warm water and dry it well.
- Bury waste well away from water and the shelter ([[c:hygiene]]). Wash your hands every time.
- No urine for **12 hours** in an adult is a warning sign: give more fluid and plan to get help.

## Hygiene
- Wash face, hands, armpits and groin daily with warm water and a cloth. Dry thoroughly.
- Mouth care: rinse or brush teeth twice a day; give sips to keep the mouth moist.
- Keep wounds, dressings and bedding clean and dry. Air bedding during the day.

## Monitoring
Check pulse, breathing rate, warmth, alertness (AVPU), pain and urine every **2–4 hours**, and write it in the [[t:casualty]] log. When to escalate is in [[a:medicine-overview]].

## Morale
- Explain what you are doing, and why, before you do it.
- Keep a routine: meals, washes, turns, checks, signalling. Routine makes days pass and reduces fear.
- Talk, plan, tell stories. Give them jobs they can do lying down: keeping the log, counting signal times.
- Never discuss how serious things are in front of them, even if they seem unconscious. Hearing can remain.

## Signalling while you wait
- Signal at set times: whistle six blasts a minute, torch at night, bright gear laid out in the open ([[a:signalling-rescue]], [[t:signal]]).
- Make an **X** on open ground: the ground-to-air sign for "need medical help".
- If there are enough people, send **two** for help with a written [[a:location-report]] ([[a:leaving-casualty]]). Note the grid reference from [[t:gps]].
- Keep the phone off between uses and charge it from the solar panel when you can ([[t:battery]]).

- NICE: Pressure ulcers: prevention and management (CG179) (nice.org.uk)
- St John Ambulance: Spinal injury (sja.org.uk)
- British Red Cross: First aid
- Wilderness Medical Society: Clinical practice guidelines for spinal cord protection (wms.org)

=== injured-alone
title: Injured and alone
summary: What to do when you are hurt with nobody else around: stop bleeding, get warm, treat yourself, decide whether to move, and plan for a long wait.
tags: alone, injured alone, solo, self-rescue, self first aid, tourniquet on yourself, splint yourself, nobody knows, stranded
icon: user-round-x
needs: first-aid-kit, survival-bag, whistle, head-torch, warm-layers, this-phone
related: stay-or-go, moving-with-injury, signalling-rescue, emergency-night-out
---
!quick
1. **Stop the bleeding** first: press hard, pack, or a tourniquet on a limb ([[a:severe-bleeding]]).
2. **Get warm:** off the ground, into all your layers and the survival bag, out of the wind.
3. **Check yourself head to toe**, splint what's broken, then **decide**: stay put and signal, or move.
4. Assume **nobody knows** yet. Save phone battery, signal at set times, and plan for a long wait.
!end

## 1. Bleeding
- **Press hard** on the wound with a pad or your hand for at least 10 minutes. Kneel or lean your body weight on it if you can.
- **Pack** a deep wound with cloth, then press on top. Bandage firmly.
- **Tourniquet on yourself** (life-threatening limb bleeding pressure can't stop): put it **high and tight** on the limb, 5–7 cm above the wound, not on a joint.
  - **Leg:** both hands free, so this is easier. Wrap a wide band (belt, folded cloth, at least 4 cm) round the thigh, knot it, put a strong stick through, twist until the bleeding stops, then tie the stick down.
  - **Arm:** loop the band round your upper arm before tightening, hold one end in your teeth or trap it under your knee, and twist the windlass with your good hand. A commercial tourniquet is designed to be done one-handed: practise it beforehand.
  - Write the time on your skin. **Don't loosen it.**
- Then lie down, keep warm, and treat yourself for [[a:shock]].

## 2. Warmth
An injured person lying still gets cold fast, even in summer. Cold also worsens bleeding and shock.
- Get off the ground: sit or lie on your rucksack, a mat, rope, bracken ([[a:insulation-ground]]).
- Put on every layer and a hat. Get into the survival bag.
- Out of the wind: behind a boulder, wall or in a hollow. Don't use energy building a shelter if you are badly hurt; wrap up first.
- Eat and drink something if you can.

## 3. Assess and treat yourself
Once bleeding is controlled and you are warmer, check yourself slowly from head to toe. Look and feel for pain, wounds, swelling and deformity. Move each limb gently.
- **Splint a leg:** pad it, then tie it to a trekking pole, stick or rolled mat, or to the other leg, with broad ties. Use a sitting position with the leg straight.
- **Splint an arm:** tuck the hand inside your jacket, zip it up, or pin the sleeve to the chest. A belt or cord round the neck makes a sling.
- Clean and cover wounds ([[a:wound-care]]). Check fingers and toes beyond any splint ([[a:long-splinting]]).
- Hit your head, feeling sick or drowsy? Stay put, and stay where you can be seen ([[a:head-injury]]).

## 4. Stay or move?
**Stay put** if:
- You can't walk or crawl safely, or you have a head, back, pelvis or thigh injury.
- Someone knows your route and when you are due back.
- It is dark, foggy, stormy, or the ground is dangerous.

**Consider moving** if:
- Nobody will raise the alarm for a long time, and you can move without making the injury worse.
- You are somewhere dangerous: rising tide, a river, a cliff edge, a rockfall.
- Shelter, a path, a road or people are close and reachable.

If you move, go short, safe distances: shuffle on your bottom downhill, crawl, or use poles or a stick as a crutch ([[a:moving-with-injury]]). Head for paths, tracks, roads and houses ([[a:stay-or-go]]). Leave a note or marker where you were, with the time and your direction.

## 5. Signal and save battery
- **Whistle:** six blasts in a minute, then a minute's rest. Repeat at set times, and whenever you hear or see anyone ([[a:signalling-rescue]]).
- **Light:** six flashes a minute at night. The [[t:signal]] tool can flash the torch or screen.
- **Be visible:** bright gear on top of you or spread out on open ground ([[a:be-visible]]).
- **This phone:** note your grid reference with [[t:gps]] and write it down. Then **switch it off**. Turn it on at fixed times (for example on the hour, during daylight) to check the map or signal. Check [[t:battery]] before relying on it.

## 6. The mental side
Shock, pain and fear make clear thinking hard. Being alone makes it harder.
- **Stop. Breathe slowly**: in for 4, out for 6. Then make one decision at a time.
- **Talk to yourself** out loud. Say what you'll do next, then do it.
- **Write it down:** time of the injury, what you've done, your plan, observations every few hours ([[t:casualty]], [[t:notebook]]). It keeps you organised and tells rescuers what happened.
- **Small tasks** give control: improve the insulation, sort your food, prepare signals.
- Expect low moments, especially at night. They pass. People survive long waits alone.

## Plan for a long wait
Nobody knows you are hurt until you are missed and someone raises the alarm. Then a search takes time.
- When will you be **missed**? Tonight? In two days? Work out the earliest realistic time.
- **Water first:** ration effort, not water. Drink when thirsty. Collect rain if you can ([[a:rain-dew-collection]]).
- **Food:** small amounts regularly. It keeps you warm.
- **Night:** prepare before dark. Every layer on, survival bag, insulated from the ground, signals ready ([[a:emergency-night-out]]).
- **Daily:** check wounds and splints morning and evening, keep moving uninjured limbs, and keep signalling.

When you reach people or a phone, see [[a:when-you-reach-help]].

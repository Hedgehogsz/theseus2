# Mindset, health and hygiene. Aligned with NHS, Mind, UK Sepsis Trust, Mountain Rescue England and Wales and Mountain Training guidance.

=== stop-method
title: The STOP method
summary: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan. The first five minutes of any bad situation, and why sitting down and drinking something saves lives.
tags: STOP, stop think observe plan, lost, panic, first minutes, calm down, what now, priorities
icon: octagon-pause
time: 5–15 min
level: easy
related: emergency-priorities, lost-person-actions, panic-control, decisions-under-stress
---
!quick
1. **Stop.** Stand still, then **sit down** somewhere safe and out of the wind. Breathe out slowly.
2. **Drink, eat, put a layer on.** A cold, hungry, dehydrated brain makes bad decisions.
3. **Think and observe:** what has happened, where you last knew your position, who is hurt, how much daylight is left ([[t:sun]]).
4. **Plan** one clear next step, say it out loud (or write it down), then do it.
!end

## Why stopping saves lives
When something goes wrong, the instinct is to **keep moving**: walk faster, push on, try to fix it at once. That is how a small problem becomes a big one. Lost walkers who keep going usually walk further from where searchers will look, into worse ground, as darkness and tiredness build. Mountain rescue teams see this again and again: the accident often happens *after* the first mistake, while someone is hurrying to put it right.

Stopping does three things. It stops you making things worse. It lets the first surge of adrenaline fade (it peaks in the first few minutes). And it gives you a chance to look after your body, which is what your brain runs on.

## S: Stop
- **Stand still.** If you are on steep, icy or boggy ground, move only as far as the nearest safe, flat spot.
- **Sit down.** Sitting tells your body the emergency is not a sprint. Sit on your rucksack, a coil of rope or a thick pile of bracken, never directly on wet ground ([[a:insulation-ground]]).
- **Breathe:** a few slow breaths, with the out-breath longer than the in-breath ([[a:panic-control]]).

## Sit down, drink, eat, get warm
Do these **before** you start planning. They take 5–10 minutes and they are not a waste of time.
- **Put on a layer and your hat** before you feel cold. You cool fast once you stop walking, especially if you are damp with sweat.
- **Drink** a few mouthfuls. Mild dehydration causes headaches, tiredness and poor concentration.
- **Eat something sugary or starchy.** Low blood sugar makes people irritable, clumsy and stubborn.
- **Get out of the wind:** behind a wall, boulder, bank or into trees. Get everyone in a group into one huddle.
- **Check yourself and others for injury.** People in shock often don't notice wounds at first ([[a:primary-survey]]).

## T: Think
Ask yourself, calmly, in this order:
1. **Is anyone in danger right now?** Bleeding, not breathing, in water, near an edge, very cold. Deal with that first.
2. **What has actually happened?** Lost, injured, benighted, weather turned, kit failed?
3. **Where was I last sure of my position?** How long ago, and which way have I come since?
4. **Who knows where I am,** and when will they notice I'm missing?
5. **What have I got?** Food, water, clothing, light, shelter, this phone's battery ([[t:battery]]).

## O: Observe
- **Look around properly.** Landmarks, the direction of slope, streams, paths, walls, power lines, buildings, lights.
- **Weather:** is it getting worse? Wind direction, cloud coming down, rain on the way ([[a:mountain-weather]]).
- **Daylight:** check sunset with [[t:sun]]. In a British winter, darkness can come before 16:00.
- **Your own state:** shivering, exhausted, hurt? Be honest. Your body is part of the situation.
- **Position:** get a grid reference with [[t:gps]] and look at [[t:map]] if the battery allows.

## P: Plan
- **List your options.** Usually there are only a few: stay put and signal, retrace your steps, walk out to a road, or make shelter for the night ([[a:stay-or-go]]).
- **Pick the safest one that works,** not the most hopeful. If two options seem equal, pick the one you can undo.
- **Set a trigger point:** "If we haven't reached the wall by 15:30, we stop and shelter."
- **Say it out loud** or write it in [[t:notebook]] with the time. A plan that is written down is harder to drift away from.
- **Then act on it.** STOP is not an excuse to sit and freeze. Once you have a plan, start the first step.

!tip Use it again
Repeat STOP whenever something changes: the weather turns, someone gets hurt, you reach a junction you don't recognise, or you notice you are hurrying. It takes a few minutes each time.
!end

## Common mistakes
- **Skipping the food and the layer** because "it'll only take a minute". It rarely does.
- **Stopping in an exposed spot.** Sit where you are sheltered, or you will hurry the thinking to get moving again.
- **Planning around hope:** "the car park must be just over that ridge". Plan around what you *know*.
- **Changing the plan every ten minutes.** Change it only when something real changes.

=== panic-control
title: Controlling panic
summary: What fear does to your body and thinking, how to slow your breathing in minutes, and how to calm someone else who is panicking.
tags: panic, panic attack, fear, anxiety, breathing, box breathing, tactical breathing, hyperventilation, calm down, adrenaline, scared
icon: wind-arrow-down
time: 2–10 min
level: easy
related: stop-method, decisions-under-stress, loneliness, heart-attack-stroke
---
!quick
1. **Breathe out slowly.** In through the nose for **4**, hold **4**, out through the mouth for **4–6**, hold **4**. Repeat for 2–3 minutes.
2. **Name it:** "This is adrenaline. It is unpleasant but it will pass." Panic usually peaks within **10 minutes**.
3. **Do one small physical task:** put on a hat, drink, tie a lace. Then the next one.
4. **Chest pain, collapse or a known heart condition?** Treat it as a possible heart attack: [[a:heart-attack-stroke]].
!end

## What fear does to you
When you are frightened, your body releases **adrenaline**. It is built for running away or fighting, not for thinking clearly.
- **Heart** pounds and races. **Breathing** becomes fast and shallow.
- **Over-breathing** (hyperventilation) lowers the carbon dioxide in your blood. That causes **tingling** in the fingers and lips, **dizziness**, chest tightness and a feeling that you can't get enough air, which makes you breathe even faster.
- **Tunnel vision:** you notice less and fix on one thing. You may not hear people talking to you.
- **Fine motor skills go:** fumbling with zips, knots, a ferro rod or a phone screen.
- **Time feels distorted,** and the urge to run, or to freeze, can be overwhelming.

None of this is dangerous in itself. Panic feels like dying, but **a panic attack will not kill you**. What kills people is what they *do* while panicking: running in the dark, leaving shelter, abandoning kit, swimming hard in cold water. See [[a:cold-water-immersion]] for the cold shock gasp, which is different and needs you to float for a minute or two.

## Slow your breathing
This is the single most effective thing you can do.
1. Sit down if you can. Drop your shoulders and unclench your jaw and hands.
2. **Box (tactical) breathing:** breathe **in** through your nose for a count of **4**, **hold** for 4, breathe **out** slowly through your mouth for 4, **hold** for 4. Draw a square in your head as you go.
3. If holding feels too hard, simply make the **out-breath longer** than the in-breath: in for 4, out for 6–8. The long out-breath is what slows the heart.
4. Breathe into your belly: put a hand on it and feel it rise.
5. Keep going for **2–3 minutes** even after you start to feel better.

!warn Don't breathe into a paper bag
It is no longer recommended. If the breathlessness is actually from asthma, a heart problem or a chest injury, rebreathing can make it worse. Use slow breathing instead.
!end

## Ground yourself
When thoughts are racing, pull your attention back to the present:
- **5–4–3–2–1:** name 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste.
- Press your feet into the ground and your hands on your knees. Feel the weight.
- Hold something cold (a stone, your water bottle) and describe it to yourself.

## Small tasks
Action beats fear. Pick a job so small you can't fail at it, then do the next one.
- Put on a layer. Drink. Eat a biscuit. Check your pockets. Write the time in [[t:notebook]].
- Then something bigger: clear a spot to sit, put out a [[a:be-visible|bright marker]], get the whistle out.
- Each finished task tells your brain you are coping. This is how STOP starts ([[a:stop-method]]).

## Self-talk
- Talk to yourself as you would to a frightened friend: by name, calmly, in short sentences.
- **Replace** "I'm going to die out here" with "I'm cold and scared. I have a layer, water and a plan. People look for missing walkers."
- **Shrink the time frame:** not "how will I survive the night", but "the next 20 minutes: shelter".
- Counting, reciting something you know by heart or singing quietly all slow the breath too.

## Is it really panic?
Some medical problems feel like panic, and some look like it in other people:
- **Chest pain** spreading to the arm, jaw or back, sweating, nausea: possible heart attack ([[a:heart-attack-stroke]]).
- **Wheeze** or a known asthmatic: [[a:asthma-attack]].
- **Confusion, slurred speech, clumsiness in the cold:** hypothermia ([[a:hypothermia]]).
- **Shaky, sweaty, irritable diabetic:** low blood sugar. Give sugar.
- **Headache, dizziness and sleepiness in an enclosed shelter with a stove:** possible carbon monoxide. Get into fresh air at once.

## Recognising panic in someone else
- Fast breathing, wide eyes, pale or flushed, shaking, clinging, unable to answer simple questions.
- Some people **freeze** and go silent; some get angry; some try to run off. All are panic.

## Helping them
1. **Get close, get low, use their name.** Speak slowly and quietly in short sentences.
2. **Breathe with them:** "Breathe in with me… two, three, four… and out… slowly." Let them copy you.
3. Don't argue or tell them to "calm down". Say: "You're safe right now. I'm staying with you."
4. **Give them a simple job:** hold the torch, count the whistle blasts, look after the map. A job gives back control.
5. Keep them warm, give a drink and food once they can swallow easily.
6. Keep an eye on them afterwards: panic can come back in waves, especially at night.

=== decisions-under-stress
title: Making decisions under stress
summary: Why good people make bad calls in emergencies (tunnel vision, summit fever, "it'll be fine"), and simple rules that keep you safe.
tags: decisions, judgement, summit fever, sunk cost, turn back, turnaround time, deadline, tunnel vision, normalcy bias, risk, choice
icon: split
related: stop-method, stay-or-go, panic-control, emergency-night-out
---
!quick
1. **Stop, eat, drink, warm up** before any big decision. A cold, hungry brain chooses badly.
2. **Write down 2–4 options** in [[t:notebook]]. For each: what's the worst that could happen?
3. **Choose the safest option you can undo.** Turning back is almost always reversible; pushing on often isn't.
4. **Set a deadline:** decide, at the latest, with **2 hours of daylight left** ([[t:sun]]).
!end

## How stress bends your thinking
Most outdoor accidents in Britain don't happen because people lack skills. They happen because, at the critical moment, people make a poor choice. Stress makes these traps much more likely.

### Tunnel vision
Under pressure you fix on one goal (the summit, the car, the bothy) and stop noticing everything else: the weather building, a companion slowing down, the light fading. **Fix:** stop every hour and deliberately ask, "What has changed?"

### Summit fever and sunk cost
"We've come this far." The time, effort and money already spent feel like a reason to continue. They are not. The only question is: **from here, what is safest?** The hill will be there next time. Many mountain rescue call-outs start with a group that knew it should turn back and didn't.

### "Get-home-itis"
Wanting to be home, at work tomorrow, or not to let someone down pushes people into darkness, swollen rivers and storms. Nobody will be angry that you were late and alive.

### "It'll be fine" (normalcy bias)
Your brain assumes things will carry on as normal: the rain will ease, the river will drop, the path will reappear. It underrates sudden change. **Fix:** ask "If this gets worse, not better, what will I do?"

### Following the group
Groups make riskier choices than individuals: nobody wants to be the one who says "let's turn back", and everybody assumes someone else has checked. **Fix:** ask each person, especially the quietest, "Are you happy with this?" One "no" is enough to stop and think.

### The body
Cold, hunger, dehydration, exhaustion, pain and lack of sleep all shrink judgement. **Confusion and stubbornness are early signs of hypothermia** ([[a:hypothermia]]). If someone is suddenly arguing for a silly plan, check whether they are cold.

## Simple decision rules
Rules made in advance protect you from the person you become under stress.
- **Turnaround time:** before you set out, fix a time to turn back whatever happens. Stick to it.
- **Decide before dark:** make the stay-or-go decision with **at least 2 hours of daylight left**. Check sunset on [[t:sun]]. Finding shelter, water and wood by torchlight is far harder.
- **Weather rule:** if visibility drops and you are not on a clear path, or the wind makes walking difficult, go down or shelter.
- **Water rule:** if you're not sure you can cross a river safely, you can't ([[a:river-crossing]]).
- **Injury rule:** if anyone can't walk normally, stop and plan around them, not around the timetable.
- **Group rule:** never split up, except two people together going for help.
- **Reversible first:** prefer the choice you can undo. Waiting an hour usually can be undone; dropping into a gully usually can't.

## Write your options down
Writing slows racing thoughts and stops you going round in circles.
1. Note the **time**, your **position** (grid reference from [[t:gps]]), weather, daylight left, and everyone's condition.
2. List **2–4 options**. Typical ones: stay and signal; retrace to the last known point; walk out to a road; shelter here for the night.
3. For each, write **what it needs** (daylight, fitness, kit), **what could go wrong**, and **can it be undone?**
4. Cross out any option that could kill someone if it goes wrong.
5. Pick one. Write down **why**, and a **trigger** that would make you change it ("if the stream is above knee height, we come back").

| Question | Ask yourself |
|---|---|
| What kills first? | Cold and wet, injury, falls and water usually come before thirst or hunger |
| What do I *know*? | Not what I hope or guess |
| Can I undo it? | Prefer reversible choices |
| What's the deadline? | Daylight, weather, battery, someone's condition |
| Who disagrees? | Listen to the quietest person |

## Once you've decided
- **Commit.** Don't reopen the decision every 10 minutes because you feel anxious. Reopen it only when one of your triggers is hit or something new happens.
- **Tell everyone** the plan and the trigger points.
- **Keep a log** in [[t:notebook]]: time, decision, reason. It helps rescuers and keeps you honest.

!note It's fine to be wrong in the safe direction
A night in a survival bag that turns out to have been unnecessary is a good outcome. Mountain rescue teams would far rather you stayed put and waited than took a risk to avoid "wasting their time".
!end

=== morale-routines
title: Morale and routines
summary: How to keep going over days: a simple daily structure, small wins, humour, a log, goals, and keeping clean.
tags: morale, routine, motivation, hope, daily structure, log, diary, goals, hygiene, keep going, low mood
icon: smile
related: loneliness, groups-children, decisions-under-stress, washing
---
!quick
1. **Make a simple daily plan:** water, fire, shelter repairs, food, signal, rest. Same order each day.
2. **Tick off small wins.** Write them in [[t:notebook]] or on paper each evening.
3. **Keep clean and tidy:** wash your face and hands, brush your teeth, change your socks. It lifts mood more than you'd expect.
4. **Set one goal for tomorrow** before you sleep.
!end

## Why morale matters
People who survive long ordeals are rarely the strongest or best equipped. They are the ones who **keep doing the next useful thing**. Low morale shows up as apathy: not bothering to collect wood, skipping meals, lying in a damp sleeping spot, giving up on signalling. That is dangerous, because the basics only keep you alive if you keep doing them. Morale is not a luxury; it is a survival skill.

## Build a daily structure
A routine takes decisions off your plate and gives each day a shape.
- **Morning:** check yourself (feet, wounds, warmth), drink, light or revive the fire, boil the day's water ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]).
- **Mid-morning:** the main job of the day: shelter improvement, collecting firewood, finding water, walking to a lookout to signal.
- **Midday:** eat, rest, dry clothes and socks.
- **Afternoon:** wood for the night (collect **twice what you think you need**), signal at set times, small repairs.
- **Before dark:** wash, eat, get everything you need for the night within reach, torch and whistle in a known pocket.
- **Evening:** write the log, plan tomorrow, sleep.

Keep the same times each day. Fixed signalling times (for example every hour on the hour while light) give you something to do and something to look forward to.

## Small wins
- Break big jobs into small ones: not "build a shelter" but "ridge pole up", "ribs on", "first layer of leaves".
- **Notice and record** each finished job. A list of done things at the end of the day is proof that you are coping.
- Improve one thing each day: a drier bed, a better windbreak, a cleaner water container. Progress, not perfection.

## Keep a log
Write a short entry at least twice a day, in [[t:notebook]] or with a pencil and paper to save the battery ([[t:battery]]):
- **Date and time.** Days blur quickly; this keeps you anchored.
- **Weather**, what you did, what you ate and drank, how you feel (honestly).
- **Health:** any wounds, how much you're urinating and its colour, temperature, sleep.
- **Plans** and anything you saw or heard (aircraft, vehicles, lights, smoke).

A log helps your own thinking, helps rescuers and doctors later, and gives you a voice when there's no one to talk to ([[a:loneliness]]).

## Goals and things to look forward to
- **Short goals:** today's job list. **Medium:** "by Thursday the shelter is fully waterproof". **Long:** getting home, the people waiting for you.
- **Count up, not down:** "Day 3, still here, still warm" is better than wondering how many days are left.
- Plan small treats: a hot drink at a set time, the last biscuit on day 4, a fire just for the evening.

## Humour
Laughing at the absurdity of it (the soggy socks, the spider in the boot) releases tension and bonds a group. Gallows humour is normal and healthy. Watch only for jokes that turn cruel or leave someone out.

## Looking after yourself
Neglecting your appearance and hygiene is both a sign and a cause of sinking morale.
- **Wash** face, hands, armpits, groin and feet daily, even with a cupful of water ([[a:washing]]).
- **Teeth** twice a day ([[a:dental-care]]). **Dry socks** every night.
- **Tidy your camp:** kit in the same place every time saves time and frustration in the dark.
- **Sleep and rest:** tiredness makes everything feel hopeless. Rest in the warmest part of the day if nights are broken.
- **Eat regularly**, even small amounts. Hunger lowers mood.
- **Daylight:** get out into the light during the day; it helps sleep and mood.

!warn Warning signs of collapsing morale
Not bothering to eat or drink, not getting up, sitting in the cold, letting the fire die, saying "what's the point". In others it can also be a sign of hypothermia, dehydration or illness: check warmth, fluids and wounds first. Then give the person a small, clear task and do it with them.
!end

=== loneliness

summary: Alone for days: talking aloud, keeping a journal, routines, handling dark thoughts, and holding on to hope.

icon: user-round

---
!quick
1. **Talk out loud** to yourself through each task. It keeps your thinking clear.
2. **Keep a routine** and a written log ([[t:notebook]]). Structure fills empty hours.
3. **When dark thoughts come,** notice them, name them, and turn to a small physical job.
4. **Hope is realistic:** people look for the missing, and keep looking. Keep yourself findable.
!end

## What being alone does
After a day or two alone, especially if you are cold, hungry and frightened, it is normal to feel:
- Flat, tearful, irritable or numb, with mood swinging through the day.
- Stuck in loops of worry, regret ("if only I hadn't…") or imagined disasters.
- Unsure of time: days blur, nights feel endless.
- Like giving up, or that nobody is coming.

You may also hear voices, music or your name, or feel that someone is with you. This is common in isolation, exhaustion and cold, and many climbers and sailors have described it. It is usually harmless and often comforting. But **check your body first**: confusion and hallucinations can be signs of hypothermia, dehydration, low blood sugar, infection or carbon monoxide from a stove in an enclosed space ([[a:hypothermia]], [[a:when-youre-ill]]).

## Talk out loud
- **Narrate what you're doing:** "Right. Water first. Pot, bottle, down to the stream." It sounds odd but it keeps you organised and stops your mind drifting.
- **Talk to someone who isn't there:** a partner, a parent, a friend. Tell them your plan for the day. Many survivors say this kept them going.
- **Sing, recite, tell yourself stories.** Anything you know by heart. It also steadies your breathing.
- Use your voice for your whistle routine: three words, three blasts. Rhythm helps.

## Write it down
A journal is company. Use [[t:notebook]] in short sessions to save battery ([[t:battery]]), or pencil and paper.
- Date and time each entry. Note the weather, what you did, what you ate and drank.
- Write **to** someone: a letter you will hand them when you get home.
- Write down **three things that went right today**, however small: the fire lit first time, a dry pair of socks, a robin by the shelter.
- Writing a worry down often shrinks it. Read it back the next morning.

## Keep a routine
Empty time is the enemy. Fill the day with a fixed pattern of jobs, meals, signalling and rest ([[a:morale-routines]]):
- Get up at the same time, even if you slept badly.
- **Signal at fixed times** (for example on the hour, every hour of daylight). It gives each hour a purpose.
- Keep your hands busy in the evening: whittling, mending, making cordage, sorting kit.
- Get out into daylight. Watch the birds and weather. Being interested in the world outside your head helps.

## Managing intrusive thoughts
Unwanted thoughts and images (of dying, of family, of what went wrong) come to almost everyone in these situations. Fighting them often makes them louder.
1. **Notice it:** "There's that thought again."
2. **Name it:** "That's fear talking", "That's a what-if."
3. **Don't argue with it or obey it.** Let it sit there, like a cloud passing.
4. **Turn your attention to your senses or a task:** the feel of the knife in your hand, the next stick to collect, slow breathing ([[a:panic-control]]).
5. **Worry time:** if worries keep coming, tell yourself you'll think about them at a fixed time (say 4 pm, for 15 minutes). When it comes, write them down, then stop.

Nights are hardest. Keep the torch and something to do within reach, and remember that things almost always look better in daylight.

## Hope
- **Someone is likely to notice you are missing.** Families, employers, landlords, bothy users and neighbours raise the alarm. Searches in Britain are organised and persistent, and they continue for days.
- **Make it easy for them:** stay visible, signal at intervals ([[a:signalling-rescue]], [[a:be-visible]]) and leave signs if you move.
- **Hope is something you do,** not just feel. Each job done keeps you alive another day, and each day alive is another day for help to arrive.
- If no one will come, then *you* are the rescue: plan a walk out while you're still strong ([[a:stay-or-go]]).

!note If you're having thoughts of ending your life
It is not unusual to feel this in extreme situations, and it passes. Keep yourself warm, fed and busy, and keep talking to yourself and writing. When you reach a phone or a person, tell them how you've been feeling. In the UK, Samaritans are available day and night on 116 123, free.
!end

=== groups-children
title: Looking after a group and children
summary: Leading a group in an emergency: roles, keeping everyone warm and fed, spotting cold in children and older people, keeping kids calm, handling conflict.
tags: group, leadership, leader, children, kids, family, elderly, older people, roles, conflict, morale, hypothermia, babies
icon: users-round
related: hypothermia, emergency-priorities, morale-routines, decisions-under-stress
---
!quick
1. **One person leads.** Count heads, keep everyone together, give each person a clear job.
2. **Warmth first:** everyone in all their layers, hats on, sitting on insulation, huddled out of the wind. **Children and older people first.**
3. **Feed and water everyone little and often:** a drink and a snack every 1–2 hours.
4. **Check the quiet ones:** a child or older person who goes quiet, clumsy or sleepy may be **hypothermic** ([[a:hypothermia]]).
!end

## Leadership
In an emergency, a group needs **one** decision-maker. It doesn't have to be the fittest or loudest: choose the person with the most experience and the calmest head.
- **Count heads** now and at every stop. Use a buddy system: everyone has a partner they keep track of.
- **Make decisions out loud,** briefly explain why, then stick to them unless something changes ([[a:decisions-under-stress]]).
- **Ask before deciding** on big things, and listen to the quiet ones. They often notice what others miss.
- **Stay calm in your voice and body,** even if you aren't calm inside. Others copy the leader.
- **Look after yourself** too. A leader who doesn't eat, drink or rest makes the worst decisions in the group.
- **Never split up,** except two people going together for help with a written [[a:location-report|location report]].

## Give everyone a job
Jobs keep people warm, busy and feeling useful. Match jobs to ability:
| Role | Jobs |
|---|---|
| Casualty carer | Stays with anyone hurt, keeps them warm, talks to them, logs changes in [[t:casualty]] |
| Shelter | Windbreak, groundsheet, insulation from the ground |
| Fire and water | Lights fire, boils water ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]) |
| Signals | Whistle and torch at set times, bright markers ([[a:signalling-rescue]]) |
| Food | Shares out food fairly, keeps a list of what's left |
| Children's helper | Keeps the youngest occupied and checks they're warm |

Rotate tiring or boring jobs. Give a nervous person a job close to the leader.

## Keep everyone warm and fed
- **Everyone puts on all their layers and a hat before they get cold,** especially when the group stops moving.
- **Off the ground:** sit on rucksacks, rope, bracken or anything dry ([[a:insulation-ground]]).
- **Huddle:** children and anyone cold in the middle. Share a group shelter or survival bag.
- **Drinks:** warm sweet drinks are ideal. Everyone drinks regularly; check urine is pale.
- **Food:** small snacks every 1–2 hours keep the body making heat. Children run out of energy faster than adults.
- **Medicines:** ask early who needs regular medicines (inhalers, insulin, heart tablets, epilepsy drugs) and how much they have. Keep those people close to the leader.

## Children and older people get cold faster
**Children** lose heat quickly: they are small for their surface area and have little fat reserve. They often don't say they're cold, or they stop complaining when they get worse. **Older people** may shiver less, feel the cold less and get confused quickly.

Watch for:
- **Going quiet,** withdrawn or unusually sleepy. A child who suddenly stops moaning is a warning sign.
- **Clumsiness,** stumbling, dropping things, slurred or mumbled speech ("the umbles").
- **Pale or blotchy skin, cold tummy** (feel under the clothes at the tummy or back of the neck, not the hands).
- **Babies:** cold to touch, floppy, very quiet or sleepy, not feeding. Their skin may look pink and healthy even when they are dangerously cold.

Act early: shelter, extra layers, hat, a dry nappy or underwear, food and a warm drink, cuddled against an adult under a shared cover. See [[a:hypothermia]] and [[a:staying-warm-night]].

## Keeping children calm and occupied
- **Tell them the truth at their level:** "We're going to stay here tonight. It'll be uncomfortable, but we're together and we have a plan."
- **Give them real jobs:** collecting twigs, holding the torch, counting whistle blasts, watching for helicopters, being in charge of the snacks.
- **Games:** I spy, 20 questions, counting stones, making up a story one sentence each, singing.
- **Routine and rituals:** meals at set times, a bedtime story, the same sleeping order.
- **Comfort:** physical closeness, a familiar object, praise for every small brave thing.
- **Keep them close:** set a clear boundary ("you must always be able to see me"), give each one a whistle if you have spares, and explain what three blasts mean.
- **Hide your own fear** as far as you can. Children read faces. Step away to steady yourself if you need to.

## Conflict
Cold, hunger, fear and tiredness make everyone short-tempered.
- **Fix the body first.** Arguments often shrink after food, a warm drink and a rest.
- **Be fair and seen to be fair,** especially with food and the warmest sleeping spots. Write down the shares.
- **Separate people** with different jobs if two keep clashing.
- **Deal with it early,** quietly and in private. Listen, acknowledge, then restate the plan.
- **Safety beats feelings:** if someone insists on doing something dangerous (going off alone, crossing a flooded river), the leader says no clearly and explains why.

- British Red Cross: First aid for babies and children (redcross.org.uk)
- Mountain Rescue England and Wales: Hill safety for groups (mountain.rescue.org.uk)
- The Scouts: Safety and leading groups outdoors (scouts.org.uk)

=== preventing-infection
title: Preventing infection and diarrhoea
summary: Clean hands without a tap, safe water and food, clean pots and early wound care: the habits that stop camp illness.
tags: infection, diarrhoea, hygiene, hand washing, handwashing, food hygiene, germs, norovirus, gastro, sickness, clean, utensils
icon: soap-dispenser-droplet
needs: steel-pot, cotton-cloth
related: diarrhoea-ors, settle-strain-boil, wound-care, latrines
---
!quick
1. **Wash your hands** after the toilet and before touching food or clean water: soap and water for **20 seconds**, rinsed with clean water.
2. **Boil all wild water** ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]). Keep dirty and clean containers separate.
3. **Cook food through and eat it hot.** Clean pots and spoons after every meal.
4. **Clean every cut straight away** ([[a:wound-care]]) and keep the toilet **30 m+ from water** and camp.
!end

## Why it matters
In a camp without running water, most illness spreads by the **faecal–oral route**: germs from the toilet get onto hands, pots, food and water, then into someone's mouth. A bout of diarrhoea and vomiting can make you dangerously dehydrated in a day, and spread through a whole group. The fixes are simple, but they only work if you do them **every time**.

## Hand hygiene without running water
**When:** after the toilet, after handling rubbish or animals, before preparing or eating food, before touching clean water or a wound, and after caring for someone who is ill.

**How (soap and water):**
1. Wet hands with clean (ideally boiled) water. Have someone pour, or use a hanging bottle ([[a:latrines]] has a simple handwashing station).
2. Lather with soap for **at least 20 seconds**: palms, backs, between fingers, thumbs, fingertips and under nails.
3. Rinse with **clean** water, poured over the hands, not in a shared bowl.
4. Air-dry or use a clean cloth kept just for hands.

**Short of water:** about **250 ml** washes one pair of hands if someone pours a thin stream. A cup of clean water and friction are better than nothing.

**Alcohol gel** (at least 60% alcohol) is useful between washes, but it **doesn't work on dirty or greasy hands**, and it **doesn't kill norovirus** (the winter vomiting bug). After the toilet and when anyone has diarrhoea, use soap and water.

**No soap:** scrub hard with water and a little clean sand or grit, then rinse well. Keep nails short.

## Water
- **Boil all water** from streams, lakes, springs and rain barrels. A rolling boil is enough ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]).
- Keep **untreated** and **boiled** water in separate, marked containers ([[a:water-hygiene]]).
- Don't dip a cup or hand into a clean water container. Pour from it.
- Keep bottle necks and lids off the ground. Don't share bottles if anyone is ill.

## Food hygiene
- **Cook food thoroughly:** meat, fish and shellfish until steaming hot all the way through, with no pink juices ([[a:cooking-on-fire]]).
- **Eat it hot,** soon after cooking. Bacteria grow fast in food left warm for hours.
- **Leftovers:** cool them quickly, cover, keep cold and shaded, and reheat until **steaming hot** before eating. Reheat only once. If in doubt, throw it out (away from camp).
- Keep **raw meat and fish away from cooked food** and ready-to-eat food. Use a different knife or wash it in between.
- Wash wild fruit and greens in boiled water, or cook them.
- Store food sealed and off the ground, away from flies and animals.

## Pots, spoons and utensils
- **Scrape and clean** pots straight after eating; dried food is harder to remove and feeds bacteria.
- Scrub with hot water and a little sand, grit or [[s:horsetail]], then **rinse with boiled water**.
- Let everything **air-dry** in the sun or wind, upside down off the ground. Don't dry with a dirty cloth.
- **One spoon and one cup per person.** Don't share, especially if anyone is ill.
- Wash up **at least 30 m from water sources** and scatter the waste water.

## Cuts, blisters and bites
Small wounds become big problems when you can't get help. Clean **immediately**, not at the end of the day:
- Irrigate with plenty of boiled, cooled water, cover with a clean dressing, change it daily or when wet ([[a:wound-care]]).
- Pop nothing that doesn't need it; treat blisters early ([[a:blisters]]).
- Check daily for spreading redness, heat, swelling, pus or red streaks ([[a:infection-escalation]]).
- Check for ticks every evening ([[a:tick-removal-lyme]]).

## Toilets
- Toilet at least **30 m from water** and **downhill and downwind of camp**. Bury waste in a cat hole or use a trench latrine ([[a:latrines]]).
- Wash hands **every** time.

## When someone is ill
- **One carer** looks after them if possible. The carer washes hands with soap and water after every contact.
- The ill person **doesn't prepare food** for others until **48 hours** after their last diarrhoea or vomiting.
- They have their own cup, spoon and towel, and their own toilet spot if you can manage it.
- Bury or carry away vomit and diarrhoea promptly, well away from camp and water.
- Keep them drinking: [[a:diarrhoea-ors]].

## Sources

- UK Health Security Agency: Infection prevention and control; Hand hygiene (gov.uk)
- Food Standards Agency: Cooking, chilling and cleaning: the 4Cs of food hygiene (food.gov.uk)
- Mountain Training: Hygiene in the hills (mountain-training.org)

=== dental-care
title: Teeth and dental problems
summary: Cleaning teeth without a toothbrush, easing toothache and lost fillings, spotting a dangerous abscess, and saving a knocked-out tooth.
tags: teeth, dental, toothache, toothbrush, abscess, filling, knocked out tooth, gum, mouth, twig, chew stick
icon: toothbrush
needs: cotton-cloth, knife
related: when-youre-ill, preventing-infection, pain-without-drugs, morale-routines
---
!quick
1. **Clean twice a day:** a chewed [[s:hazel]] or [[s:willow]] twig, or clean cloth over a finger. Spit, don't swallow.
2. **Toothache:** painkillers as the pack says, a warm salt-water rinse, soft lukewarm food.
3. **Danger:** swelling spreading to the **eye or neck**, trouble **swallowing or breathing**, or fever: get out and get help urgently.
4. **Knocked-out adult tooth:** hold by the **crown**, rinse briefly, **push it back in** and bite on cloth. Within the hour if you can.
!end

## Cleaning without a toothbrush
Dental pain can stop you eating and sleeping, and an infection in the mouth can become serious. Keep cleaning every day, morning and night, for about 2 minutes.

### Chew stick
1. Cut a fresh, green twig about **15 cm** long and pencil-thick from a tree you are **certain** of: [[s:hazel]], [[s:willow]] or [[s:silver-birch]] are good choices.
2. Peel back 1–2 cm of bark at one end and **chew** it until the fibres fray into a soft brush.
3. Brush gently along the gum line and every surface of each tooth. Trim off the used end each day.

!danger Never use poisonous wood
Never use [[s:yew]] (every part is deadly), [[s:rhododendron]], [[s:cherry-laurel]], holly, laburnum, elder or anything you can't identify. If in doubt, use a cloth.
!end

### Cloth
Wrap a clean strip of [[a:kit-cotton-cloth|cotton cloth]] round your finger, wet it with boiled water, and rub each tooth and along the gums. Use a clean part of the cloth for each area. Wash and boil the cloth before reuse.

### Salt, ash and toothpaste
- **Toothpaste:** use a pea-sized smear. **Spit, don't rinse** afterwards, so the fluoride keeps working.
- **Salt:** a pinch on a wet cloth or chew stick helps clean, but it is abrasive. Brush gently and not every time.
- Don't brush with wood ash or charcoal. They are abrasive and ash can be caustic ([[a:washing]]).

### Between the teeth
Use dental floss if you have it, or a thin strand of clean cotton thread, or a very thin sliver of clean wood as a toothpick. Food trapped between teeth causes pain and gum infection within days.

### Diet
Sugar is what rots teeth. **How often** matters more than how much: keep sweets and sugary drinks to mealtimes, then rinse your mouth with water.

## Toothache
- **Painkillers:** paracetamol or ibuprofen, exactly as the packet says. **Never put aspirin or any tablet on the gum:** it burns.
- **Salt-water rinse:** half a teaspoon of salt in a cup of warm (boiled and cooled) water. Swish for a minute, spit. Repeat 3–4 times a day. Not for young children.
- **Cold compress** on the cheek for 20 minutes at a time if the face is sore or swollen.
- **Avoid** very hot, very cold, sweet and hard food. Chew on the other side.
- Rest with your head raised: lying flat can make throbbing worse.
- Clove oil is a traditional remedy; if you have it, a tiny amount on cotton wool against the tooth (not the gum) may ease pain.

## Lost filling or broken tooth
- Keep the area very clean and avoid chewing on it.
- **Temporary filling kits** are sold in UK pharmacies; carry one in a long-trip first-aid kit. Dry the hole, press the material in, bite gently to shape it.
- **Sugar-free chewing gum** pressed into the hole can protect it for a day or two.
- A sharp broken edge can be covered with gum or a dab of wax to stop it cutting your tongue.
- Keep any broken piece; a dentist may be able to bond it back.

## Abscess: know the warning signs
A dental abscess is a pocket of pus from an infected tooth or gum. Signs:
- Intense, **throbbing** pain that spreads to the ear, jaw or neck, worse when lying down.
- Red, swollen gum, sometimes with a pimple-like lump; a **bad taste** or pus in the mouth.
- Tooth tender to touch or bite on; swelling of the face; fever and feeling unwell.

Warm salt-water rinses and painkillers help, but **an abscess won't go away on its own**. It needs a dentist to drain it. Plan to get to one within a day or two.

!danger Get out and get urgent help if
- **Swelling spreads** to the eye, down the neck or under the tongue.
- **Difficulty swallowing, opening the mouth or breathing.**
- **High temperature,** shivering, fast heartbeat, confusion or feeling very unwell: possible **sepsis** ([[a:when-youre-ill]]).

These can become life-threatening. See [[a:when-to-get-help]].
!end

## Knocked-out tooth
**Adult (permanent) tooth:** speed matters most. Ideally back in the socket within **30–60 minutes**.
1. Find the tooth. Pick it up by the **crown** (the white chewing part). **Never touch the root.**
2. If it's dirty, rinse it **briefly** (about 10 seconds) in milk, saline or clean water, or let the person suck it clean. **Don't scrub** it or wrap it in tissue.
3. **Push it back into the socket** the right way round, gently but firmly.
4. Bite on a folded clean cloth for 15–20 minutes to hold it. Get to a dentist as soon as you can.
5. If you can't replant it: keep it in **milk**, or in the person's mouth between cheek and gum (adults only; risk of swallowing in children), or in a little of their saliva in a clean container. **Don't let it dry out.**

**Baby (milk) tooth:** **don't** put it back; it can damage the adult tooth underneath. Press a clean cloth on the socket to stop bleeding and get dental advice.

**Bleeding gum:** bite firmly on a rolled clean cloth for 15–20 minutes. Don't keep checking.

- Oral Health Foundation: Dental emergencies; Dental trauma (dentalhealth.org)
- British Dental Association: Dental first aid (bda.org)
- St John Ambulance: Knocked-out tooth (sja.org.uk)

=== when-youre-ill
title: When you're ill with no help
summary: Looking after yourself or someone else through an illness far from help: rest, fluids, warmth, food, the red flags, and when to get out.
tags: ill, illness, sick, fever, temperature, sepsis, meningitis, red flags, chest pain, breathing, confusion, rest, getting out
icon: bed-double
related: fever, infection-escalation, when-to-get-help, stay-or-go
---
!quick
1. **Rest, keep warm and dry,** and drink small amounts often. Urine should be pale.
2. **Eat little and often:** plain, warm food. Sugar and salt if you can't manage more.
3. **Check for red flags** twice a day: confusion, breathlessness, chest pain, stiff neck, a rash that doesn't fade, no urine, mottled skin. See below.
4. **Any red flag, or getting worse after 24–48 hours:** getting to help becomes the priority. Go while you still can.
!end

## The basics
Most illnesses outdoors (colds, flu, stomach bugs, minor infections) get better by themselves with rest, fluids and warmth. Your job is to give your body the best chance, and to notice early if it isn't a minor illness.

### Rest
- Stop all unnecessary work. Do only the essentials: water, warmth, signalling.
- Sleep whenever you can, in the warmest, driest spot, insulated from the ground ([[a:insulation-ground]]).
- If there are others, hand over your jobs and tell them how you feel.

### Fluids
- Drink **small amounts often**, even if you don't feel thirsty: a few mouthfuls every 15–30 minutes.
- Fever, diarrhoea and vomiting all use up water fast. Aim for **pale yellow urine**. Dark urine, or none for 8 hours, means you need more ([[a:dehydration-signs]]).
- With diarrhoea or vomiting, use oral rehydration solution ([[a:diarrhoea-ors]]).
- Warm drinks are comforting and help keep you warm. All water must be boiled ([[a:settle-strain-boil]]).

### Warmth
- Being ill makes you cold faster and less able to notice it. Keep layers on and stay out of the wind and wet.
- **With a fever,** don't overheat: if you feel hot and sweaty, loosen layers. If you're shivering, add them. Aim to feel comfortable, not hot.
- Change out of sweat-soaked clothes into dry ones when you can.

### Food
- **Eat little and often:** small amounts of plain, easy food (porridge, rice, bread, broth). Don't force big meals.
- If you can't eat, keep taking **sugar** (sweets, honey, sweetened drinks) and a little **salt**.
- Appetite returning is a good sign.

### Medicines
- Paracetamol or ibuprofen can ease fever and aches. Follow the dose on the pack; don't exceed it, and don't take ibuprofen if you're dehydrated, have stomach ulcers or asthma made worse by it.
- Keep taking any regular medicines. Count what you have left.
- See [[a:fever]] and [[a:pain-without-drugs]].

## Keep a log
Twice a day, note in [[t:notebook]] or on paper: time, temperature (hot/cold to touch if no thermometer), pulse, breathing rate, how much you've drunk and urinated, what you've eaten, symptoms. It shows you the trend, which is what matters, and helps a doctor later. For someone else, use [[t:casualty]].

## Red flags: this is not a minor illness
If **any** of these appear, assume it is serious.

### Sepsis
Sepsis is the body's extreme reaction to an infection, and it can kill within hours. Look for:
- **S**lurred speech or **confusion**.
- **E**xtreme shivering or muscle pain.
- **P**assing no urine in a day.
- **S**evere breathlessness.
- **I**t feels like you're going to die.
- **S**kin mottled, bluish or very pale.

Any infection can lead to sepsis: a wound, a chest or urine infection, a dental abscess.

### Meningitis
- **Stiff neck** (can't touch chin to chest), **severe headache**, dislike of bright light, fever with cold hands and feet, vomiting, drowsiness or confusion.
- A **rash that doesn't fade** when you press a clear glass against it. On darker skin, check paler areas: palms, soles, inside the eyelids and the roof of the mouth.
- Don't wait for the rash; it can appear late or not at all.

### Breathing and chest
- **Struggling to breathe,** breathing very fast, unable to finish a sentence, lips turning blue.
- **Chest pain,** especially crushing or spreading to the arm, jaw or back ([[a:heart-attack-stroke]]).
- Coughing up blood.

### Other red flags
- **Confusion,** unusual drowsiness or being hard to wake.
- Stroke signs: face drooping, arm weakness, speech problems.
- **Severe abdominal pain,** especially if it settles in the lower right, or a rigid, tender belly.
- **Blood** in vomit or stool, or black, tarry stool.
- A fit (seizure) for the first time ([[a:seizures]]).
- Severe dehydration: no urine, dizziness on standing, very fast pulse ([[a:dehydration-signs]]).
- A child who is floppy, unusually sleepy, won't drink, or has a fever and is under 3 months old.
- Fever that lasts more than **3 days**, or that goes and then comes back worse.

## When to prioritise getting out
Your choice changes from "rest and recover" to "**getting to help is now the priority**" when:
- **Any red flag** appears.
- You are **getting worse** instead of better after 24–48 hours.
- You can't keep fluids down for more than 12–24 hours.
- A wound infection is spreading ([[a:infection-escalation]]).

Then decide how ([[a:stay-or-go]], [[a:decisions-under-stress]]):
- **If someone knows where you are and help is coming:** stay sheltered, signal often ([[a:signalling-rescue]]) and keep the log.
- **If you can still walk and help is within reach:** go **now**, while you still have strength, in daylight, by the easiest route to a road or houses ([[a:finding-help]]). Illness usually gets worse, not better, and in a day you may not be able to walk at all.
- **If you have companions:** two go for help with a written [[a:location-report|location report]]; one stays with the ill person.
- **If you can't move:** maximise warmth, fluids and signalling, and stay findable ([[a:cant-be-moved]]).

When you reach a person or phone, see [[a:when-you-reach-help]].

=== latrines
title: Latrines and sanitation
summary: How to dig a cat hole or a group trench latrine in the right place, set up handwashing, manage periods, and leave no trace.

icon: toilet
time: 10–60 min
level: easy
needs: knife, "trowel or digging stick", cord, steel-bottle
related: preventing-infection, water-hygiene, washing, diarrhoea-ors

!quick
1. **Go at least 30 m from any water,** and away from camp, paths and buildings. Downhill and downwind of camp.
2. **Cat hole:** dig **15–20 cm** deep and about 10–15 cm across. Go, then fill it with the soil and disguise it.
3. **Group staying more than a day or two:** dig a **trench latrine** with a soil pile and cover every use.
4. **Wash hands every time** with soap and clean water.
!end

## Why it matters
Human faeces carry the germs that cause most camp diarrhoea. Rain washes them into streams, flies carry them to food, and they soak into ground water. A badly placed toilet can make your whole group, and everyone downstream, ill. Get it right first time.

## Choosing the spot
- **At least 30 m from any stream, lake, spring, ditch or well:** about 40 adult paces. Further is better.
- **At least 30 m from camp and paths;** in Scotland the access code asks you to keep well away from buildings and water. Bothy users are asked to go **at least 200 m** from the bothy, away from its water supply.
- **Downhill and downwind** of camp, and **downstream** of where you collect water.
- Soft, dark soil with roots and life in it breaks waste down fastest. Avoid peat bog, thin soil on rock, and dry stream beds (they flood).
- Not in hollows that collect water, not in caves, not where livestock gather.
- **Mark the way** so people find it at night, and keep a torch by the exit.

## Cat hole (one person, one use)
1. Dig a hole **15–20 cm deep** and **10–15 cm wide** with a trowel, a flat stick, a sharpened digging stick or the heel of your boot. Keep the top turf in one piece if you can.
2. Squat over it. Use a tree or rucksack to hold on to.
3. Put toilet paper in the hole only if you can't carry it out (see below). Stir in a little soil with a stick to speed up breakdown.
4. **Fill in** with the soil, press it down, replace the turf and cover with leaves. Push a stick upright into it so nobody digs in the same place.
5. Space cat holes widely; don't use the same small area again and again.

## Trench latrine (a group, several days)
For a group staying put, a single latrine is easier to control than scattered cat holes.
1. Dig a trench about **30 cm wide**, **30–45 cm deep** and **1 m long**. Pile the soil at one end with a stick or spare pot as a scoop.
2. Users straddle the trench, starting at one end and working along.
3. **Cover every use** with a layer of soil (a few scoops). This stops flies and smell.
4. Lay sticks along the edge to stand on, and rig a tarp or branch screen for privacy and shelter from the rain.
5. When waste is within **15–20 cm of the surface,** fill the trench with the rest of the soil, mound it over, mark it, and dig a new one.
6. Keep the area **separate** from food, water and sleeping areas, and wash hands on the way back.

## Urine
Urine is mostly harmless, but it smells and attracts animals that dig for salt. Go at least 30 m from water and camp, on bare ground, stone or gravel rather than plants. Dilute with water if you've got plenty.

## Toilet paper
- **Best:** carry it out in a sealed bag (a second bag outside keeps it hygienic).
- If you must bury it, use small amounts, plain unscented paper, and put it deep in the hole.
- **Never burn it:** dry moorland and forest fires in the UK have been started this way ([[a:fire-safety]]).
- Natural alternatives: smooth stones, large soft leaves (only ones you know; **not** nettles, and avoid hairy or unknown leaves), moss, snow.
- Wet wipes don't break down: always carry them out.

## Handwashing station
Set one up beside the latrine and use it **every time** ([[a:preventing-infection]]).
1. **Tippy tap:** a plastic bottle with a small hole near the cap, hung on a cord from a branch so it tips when pulled. Or simply have someone pour from your bottle.
2. **Soap** in a bag or on a string so it stays off the ground.
3. A layer of **stones or gravel** under it so the water soaks away rather than making mud.
4. A clean cloth or air-drying; never a shared wet towel.
Refill it with boiled water and keep it separate from drinking water.

## Periods
- **Pads and tampons:** change at least every 4–8 hours (**never leave a tampon in longer than 8 hours**: risk of toxic shock syndrome). Wash hands before and after.
- **Never bury** used products: they don't break down and animals dig them up. Wrap them, put them in a sealed bag (a little dry tea or charcoal reduces smell) and carry them out. Burning needs a very hot fire and usually leaves remains.
- **Reusable cloth pads** can be made from folded [[a:kit-cotton-cloth|cotton cloth]]. Rinse in cold water first, then wash in hot soapy water, rinse well, and **dry completely**, ideally in sunlight. Carry used ones in a sealed bag until you can wash them.
- **Menstrual cups:** empty into a cat hole, rinse with clean water, and boil for 5–10 minutes between periods.
- Wash the genital area daily with clean water; no soap inside.

## Leave no trace
- Leave the area looking as if nobody was there: holes filled, turf replaced, no paper.
- **High, cold and rocky ground** (mountain tops, popular summits): waste barely breaks down. Carry it out in a bag if you can.
- In **England and Wales** you generally need the landowner's permission to camp; follow any local rules. In **Scotland**, the Scottish Outdoor Access Code applies.
- At home with no water for flushing, the same rules apply: line the toilet bowl with a bag, add a little soil or sawdust after each use, seal it, and store it outside away from water until collection is possible.

## Sources
- NatureScot: Scottish Outdoor Access Code: Human waste (outdooraccess-scotland.scot)
- Mountain Bothies Association: The Bothy Code (mountainbothies.org.uk)
- Mountain Training: Hygiene in the hills; Wild camping (mountain-training.org)
- NHS: Toxic shock syndrome; How to wash your hands (nhs.uk)
- Leave No Trace: Dispose of waste properly (lnt.org)

!quick
1. **Strip wash with 1–2 litres** of warm water and a cloth: face, then armpits, then groin and bottom, feet last.
2. **Wash at least 30 m from streams and lakes.** Carry water away; scatter the waste water on soil.
3. **Don't make lye from wood ash.** Concentrated ash water burns skin and eyes.
4. **Dry thoroughly,** especially between the toes, in skin folds and the groin. Change into dry socks every night.

## Why wash at all
Staying clean does more than make you feel human ([[a:morale-routines]]). Dirt and sweat cause **chafing**, **fungal infections**, **boils**, and infected cuts and blisters, and dirty hands spread diarrhoea. A daily wash takes 10 minutes and a litre of water.

## Washing yourself with little water
**Where:** at least **30 m from any water source,** never in the stream or lake. Even "biodegradable" soap harms water life and makes the water unsafe downstream. Take water to your washing spot in your pot.

**Strip wash (about 1–2 litres):**
1. Warm the water on the fire to a comfortable heat, test it with your elbow. Warm water removes grease far better than cold.
2. Use one corner of a cloth for each area, or two cloths: one for "clean" areas, one for "dirty".
3. Work **clean to dirty, top to bottom:** face and neck, hands and arms, **armpits**, chest and back, then **groin and bottom**, then **feet** last.
4. Use only a little soap, and rinse it off well: soap left on skin causes soreness.
5. Dry thoroughly with a clean cloth or let the air do it, out of the wind.
6. **Scatter** the used water widely on soil or plants, well away from water and camp.

**Very little water (a cupful):** at least wash **hands, face, armpits, groin and feet**. Wet a corner of cloth, wipe, rinse the cloth, repeat. Do this daily even when you can't do more.

**Cold weather:** wash one part at a time and cover it again before moving on. Wash in the warmest part of the day, or by the fire. A wet, cold body after dark is a hypothermia risk ([[a:hypothermia]]).

**Hair:** a quick wash every few days is plenty. Rinse with warm water; use a little soap if you have it. Check for ticks along the hairline and behind the ears ([[a:tick-removal-lyme]]).

## Soap and wood ash
- **Real soap is best.** Carry a small bar; it's lighter than liquid and lasts for weeks.
- **No soap:** warm water and **friction** remove most dirt and germs. Scrub with a cloth, or rub with clean sand and rinse.
- **Wood ash** is strongly **alkaline**. A little fine ash rubbed on greasy pots with water, then rinsed off thoroughly, helps clean them.

!danger Don't make lye from ash
Soaking ash in water and boiling it down, or running water through a deep pile of ash, makes **lye** (a caustic solution). It can cause **chemical burns** to skin and **blindness if it splashes in the eyes**. Don't wash your body in ash water, don't let it near eyes, and never use it on children. If skin feels slippery or starts to sting after contact with ash, rinse with plenty of clean water for 10–20 minutes. Splash in the eye: rinse for at least 20 minutes and get help ([[a:eye-injury]], [[a:burns]]).
!end

Making real soap from ash and fat is a skilled, risky job. In an emergency, warm water and friction are safer and almost as good.

## Laundry
You can't wash everything, so prioritise: **socks** and **underwear** first, then base layers.
1. Fill your pot or a plastic bag with warm water and a little soap. Wash away from water sources.
2. Soak, then knead and squeeze for a few minutes. Pay attention to sock heels and toes.
3. **Rinse** in clean water until no suds come out. Soap left in fabric irritates skin and holds water.
4. **Wring** hard: twist the item round a stick or get a partner to twist the other end.
5. **Dry** in wind and sun, on a line under the tarp edge, or by the fire.
- **Not too close to the fire:** synthetic fabrics melt and boots crack. Feel it: if it's too hot for your hand, it's too close.
- Dry socks inside your clothes or sleeping bag overnight against your body.
- **Rotate:** one pair of socks on, one drying. Never sleep in the wet pair.
- In wet weather, rinsing in plain water and drying is better than not washing at all.

## Chafing
Chafing is red, raw, stinging skin where it rubs: inner thighs, groin, armpits, nipples, under a rucksack hip belt.
- **Prevent it:** keep skin clean and dry, wear well-fitting synthetic or wool layers next to the skin, and change out of wet underwear.
- **Lubricate** rubbing areas with petroleum jelly, lip balm or any clean grease.
- **Treat:** wash gently, dry, apply barrier cream, and keep it uncovered to air when you can. Watch for infection ([[a:wound-care]]).

## Fungal infections
Fungus loves warm, wet, dark skin.
- **Athlete's foot:** itchy, white, cracked or peeling skin between the toes. **Jock itch** and **intertrigo:** red, itchy rash in the groin and skin folds.
- **Prevent:** dry thoroughly between the toes and in folds every day, air your feet whenever you stop, change socks daily, and don't share towels.
- **Treat:** keep the area clean and **dry**; use antifungal cream or powder from the first-aid kit (clotrimazole, miconazole or terbinafine) for the full time on the pack, usually 1–2 weeks after symptoms clear.
- Feet that are wet for days risk **trench foot**: white, numb, swollen, then painful ([[a:cold-injuries]]). Dry them every night.

# Water. Your method: settle, strain through cotton cloth, 1-minute rolling boil in stainless steel, bottle.

=== settle-strain-boil
title: Settle, strain, boil: making water safe
summary: Your water method step by step. Settle, strain through cotton, a 1-minute rolling boil, then store clean. Kills germs; does not remove chemicals.
tags: boil, rolling boil, purify, disinfect, strain, cloth, settle, CDC, safe water
wiki: Boiling
---
!quick
1. **Settle:** let cloudy water stand in the pot or bottle for 30+ minutes; pour off the clearer top water.
2. **Strain** through folded cotton cloth into the steel pot.
3. **Bring to a rolling boil for 1 minute** (big bubbles that don't stop when stirred).
4. **Cool covered**, then pour into a **clean** bottle. Keep the lid on.
!end

## Why this works
Boiling is the most reliable way to kill bacteria, viruses and parasites (including *Cryptosporidium* and *Giardia*, which survive chemical tablets). The CDC advises a **1-minute rolling boil**, or 3 minutes above 2,000 m. The UK's highest point is 1,345 m, so 1 minute is always enough here.

## Step by step
1. **Collect** from the best source you can ([[a:choosing-water-source]]). Scoop from below the surface, avoiding scum and sediment.
2. **Settle:** silt and some germs sink. 30 minutes to a few hours. Pour off slowly, leaving the sludge behind.
3. **Strain:** fold your cotton cloth into 2–4 layers over the pot and pour slowly. This removes debris, insects and much of the cloudiness, which makes boiling more reliable. **Straining does not make water safe on its own.**
4. **Boil:** heat until there are large, continuous, rolling bubbles across the surface, then keep it there for **1 minute**. Count to 60 slowly, or longer if in doubt.
5. **Cool and store:** let it cool with a lid or cloth over the top. Pour it into a clean bottle, ideally one rinsed with boiled water. Don't dip dirty cups into stored water.

## Tips
- Boiling uses fuel: boil a whole potful at once, and keep it covered (it boils faster with a lid).
- Taste flat? Pour it between two clean containers a few times to add air back, or see [[a:charcoal-taste]].
- Keep the "dirty" cloth for straining only, and rinse it in boiled water after use. Don't use it for wounds or food.

!warn Boiling does not remove chemicals
Fuel, pesticides, fertilisers (nitrates), heavy metals, salt and **blue-green algae toxins** are not removed by boiling. Some become more concentrated as water boils off. The only defence is choosing a clean source. See [[a:boiling-limits]].
!end

=== boiling-steel-bottle-pot
title: Boiling in your steel bottle and pot
summary: How to boil safely in single-walled stainless steel, why never to boil a sealed or insulated bottle, and handling hot metal.
tags: stainless steel, bottle, pot, boil, sealed, lid, burn, flask, kit
---
!quick
1. **Remove the lid** or cap before heating. **Never heat a sealed bottle**: steam pressure can make it burst or blow the cap off.
2. Only boil in **single-walled, unpainted** steel. Never heat a vacuum-insulated (double-walled) flask: it can rupture.
3. Fill no more than **three-quarters** to stop it boiling over.
4. Handle with a folded cloth or a stick through the handle. Let it cool before drinking.
!end

## The pot
- The pot is the best boiler: wide base, stable, faster to heat. Use it for most of your water.
- Put it on a stable bed of embers or on two green logs (pot supports), not on top of flames that might tip it.
- Don't let it boil dry: steel discolours, and a dry pot gets hot enough to burn fingers and kit.

## The bottle
- Stand it upright at the edge of the embers, not lying in the flames.
- **Cap off.** A loose leaf or foil over the mouth keeps ash out.
- Watch the neck: water boils over through a narrow neck suddenly. Pull it back a little if it starts spitting.
- Once boiled for 1 minute, move it away with a stick and let it cool. You can screw the cap on once it is just warm. As it cools it will suck the cap tight (a slight vacuum), which keeps it clean.
- Plastic parts (cap seals, loops) melt. Take them off, or keep them away from the heat.

## Hot water bottle at night
A sealed bottle of **hot (not boiling)** water wrapped in a sock or cloth warms a sleeping bag. Check the seal doesn't leak, and never put it directly against the skin of a cold or unconscious person.

=== finding-water-uk
title: Finding water in the UK
summary: Where to look for water in British landscapes, from upland streams to springs, rain and dew.
tags: find water, stream, spring, source, river, lake, hills, upland
---
!quick
1. **Upland streams** fed by springs, above farms and roads, are the best natural sources.
2. Follow **valleys downhill** and listen. Look for lines of rushes, alders, willows or bright green moss.
3. **Rain** is the cleanest water of all: collect it off a tarp ([[a:rain-dew-collection]]).
4. Whatever the source: **settle, strain, boil**.
!end

## Where to look
- **Springs:** where water emerges from the ground, often marked by lush vegetation, reeds or a wet hollow on a hillside. Clear and cold, but still boil it.
- **Upland streams:** fast-flowing over stones, high in the catchment, away from sheep pens, farms, roads and footpaths.
- **Lakes, tarns and lochs:** collect from deep water away from the shore (the edges collect debris and animal waste). Avoid if there is any algal scum.
- **Lowland rivers and ditches:** last resort; often polluted by farm runoff and sewage.
- **Ground clues:** rushes and sedges, alder and willow trees, sphagnum moss, insects in swarms, and animal paths heading downhill.
- **Built sources:** water troughs (dirty), roof gutters and water butts, church and village taps. Ask permission where possible.

## In a real UK emergency
Water is rarely the problem in Britain. Shelter from wind and rain usually matters far more. But don't drink unboiled water in the hope that it is clean: diarrhoea will dehydrate you faster than thirst would.

=== choosing-water-source
title: Choosing a safe water source
summary: How to pick the cleanest water: what to avoid upstream, blue-green algae, and why chemicals matter more than germs.
tags: water source, pollution, farm, sewage, algae, blue-green algae, cyanobacteria, runoff, pesticides
---
!quick
1. **Go upstream** of farms, roads, villages, campsites and sewage outfalls.
2. **Moving, clear, cold** water beats still, warm or cloudy water.
3. **Never use water with algal scum** (green paint-like film, blue-green flecks, or foam), or water that smells of fuel or chemicals. Boiling doesn't help.
4. When in doubt, use **rain** instead.
!end

## Avoid
- **Downstream of farms:** slurry, fertiliser, pesticides and animal faeces, including *E. coli* O157 and *Cryptosporidium* from cattle and sheep.
- **Below roads and car parks:** fuel, oil, de-icing salt and heavy metals.
- **Sewage overflows:** UK rivers receive storm overflows after heavy rain. Look for pipes and outfalls on banks, and for grey fungus or toilet paper on vegetation.
- **Old mines and spoil heaps** (Wales, Cornwall, the Pennines, Scotland): orange-stained water and dead streams mean metals. Boiling won't remove them.
- **Dead animals** upstream; check 50–100 m above where you collect.
- **Stagnant pools**, puddles in tracks, and ditches beside fields.

## Blue-green algae (cyanobacteria)
- Common in lakes, reservoirs and slow rivers in **warm, calm late summer and autumn**.
- Looks like green paint, pea soup, blue-green or brown scum or flecks, sometimes on the downwind shore.
- The toxins cause vomiting, rashes, liver damage, and can kill dogs. **Boiling does not destroy the toxins.** Find another source.

## Best choices, in order
1. Rain collected directly onto a clean tarp or into clean containers.
2. Springs and fast upland streams above all human and farm activity.
3. Large clear lakes away from the shore.
4. Anything else, settled, strained and boiled, with a careful look upstream first.

=== rain-dew-collection
title: Collecting rain and dew
summary: Use a tarp, bin bag or clothing to collect the UK's most abundant clean water source.
tags: rain, rainwater, tarp, dew, collect, catchment
---
!quick
1. Rig a **tarp or bag sloping to one low corner** with a pot or bottle under it.
2. Let the first minute of rain **rinse** the surface, then collect.
3. **Dew:** before sunrise, wipe wet grass with a cloth and wring it into the pot.
4. Boil it if it touched anything other than a clean sheet. It almost always has.
!end

## Rain
- One square metre of tarp catches 1 litre for every millimetre of rain. A wet UK day (5–10 mm) gives a lot of water.
- Tie a short cord or twig at the low point to act as a drip line into your container.
- Trees and roofs collect bird droppings and debris: treat rain from them like stream water.

## Dew
- Heavy dew forms on clear, still nights. Tie a clean cloth round your ankles and walk through long grass at dawn, then wring it out. You can collect a cupful an hour.
- Avoid dew from poisonous plants (bracken, ragwort) and sprayed fields.

=== solar-still
title: Solar stills and transpiration bags (and their limits)
summary: How the classic solar still works, why it rarely helps in the UK, and the transpiration bag alternative.
tags: solar still, transpiration bag, condensation, water, sun
---
!quick
1. In the UK a solar still **rarely gives more than a cupful a day**, and digging it costs more sweat than it makes. Look for a stream or collect rain instead.
2. A **transpiration bag** (clear bag tied over a leafy branch in sun) is easier: a few hundred ml a day.
3. **Never** use poisonous plants (yew, laurel, rhododendron, ivy). Boil the water anyway.
!end

## Solar still
Dig a hole about 1 m across and 60 cm deep in damp ground, put a container in the centre, cover with clear plastic sealed with soil round the edge, and put a small stone in the middle so condensation drips into the container.
- Needs strong sun, damp soil and clear plastic: three things that rarely coincide in Britain.
- Adding green non-poisonous vegetation inside helps a little.

## Transpiration bag
Tie a clear plastic bag over a leafy, sunlit branch of a **non-poisonous** tree (birch, willow, hazel, sycamore), with one corner lower to collect water. Move it to a fresh branch each day.

=== charcoal-taste
title: Improvised charcoal filter (taste, not safety)
summary: Use charcoal from your fire to improve the taste and smell of water. It does not disinfect: always boil.
tags: charcoal, filter, taste, smell, activated carbon, sand
---
!quick
1. Take **hardwood charcoal** (not ash) from a cooled fire. Crush it to rice-sized pieces and rinse it in water.
2. Layer in a cloth-lined container: cloth → fine sand → charcoal → sand → gravel on top.
3. Pour water through slowly; **then boil it** (or filter after boiling to improve the taste).
!end

!warn Charcoal is not a disinfectant
A home-made charcoal filter can reduce bad tastes, smells and cloudiness. It does **not** reliably remove bacteria, viruses or parasites, and it can grow bacteria itself. Always boil.
!end

## Making charcoal
- Burn hardwood (oak, ash, beech) until it is glowing black, then smother it with soil or water to stop it burning to ash. Or collect the black lumps at the edge of an old fire.
- Use charcoal only from untreated wood, never painted or treated timber.

## Notes
- Replace the charcoal every few days. Rinse the cloth in boiled water.
- Pouring boiled water back and forth between containers also improves a flat taste.

=== dehydration-signs
title: Dehydration: signs and how much to drink
summary: Recognise dehydration early and know how much water you need in UK conditions.
tags: dehydration, thirst, urine colour, how much water, fluids
---
!quick
1. Watch **urine colour**: pale straw is fine; dark yellow or brown means drink more.
2. Early signs: thirst, headache, dizziness, tiredness, dry mouth.
3. Drink **little and often**: about 2–3 litres a day, more with hard work or heat.
4. Severe (confusion, very little urine, fast heartbeat) → **get help**; use [[a:diarrhoea-ors]].
!end

## Signs
- **Mild to moderate:** thirst, dark strong-smelling urine, peeing less than 4 times a day, dry lips and mouth, headache, tiredness, dizziness.
- **Severe:** confusion, fast heartbeat, fast breathing, sunken eyes, little or no urine, fainting.

## Notes
- Cold weather hides dehydration (you feel less thirsty), and cold makes you pee more.
- Eating needs water too: if water is scarce, eat less (especially protein).
- Don't ration water you already have while you are thirsty; drink it and look for more.

=== boiling-limits
title: What boiling does NOT remove
summary: Boiling kills germs but leaves chemicals, metals, salt and algal toxins. Some get more concentrated.
tags: boiling, chemicals, pesticides, nitrates, heavy metals, salt, seawater, algae toxins, fuel
---
!quick
1. Boiling makes water **free of germs**, not free of **chemicals**.
2. It does **not** remove: fuel/oil, pesticides, fertilisers (nitrates), heavy metals (lead, arsenic, mine water), salt, or blue-green algae toxins.
3. **Never drink seawater** or urine, boiled or not.
4. The fix is choosing a clean source: [[a:choosing-water-source]].
!end

## Details
- **Concentration:** as water boils off, everything dissolved in it becomes more concentrated. Don't boil longer than needed, and don't reboil the same water.
- **Seawater:** salt dehydrates you further and damages the kidneys. Distillation (collecting steam on a cool lid and catching the drips) can make small amounts of drinkable water, but very slowly.
- **Floodwater** carries sewage, fuel and chemicals: avoid it.
- **Strong taste of chemicals, fuel or a sheen on the surface:** find another source.

=== water-hygiene
title: Water and camp hygiene
summary: Stop sickness spreading in camp: separate dirty and clean water, wash hands, and site the toilet well.
tags: hygiene, hands, toilet, latrine, washing up, gastro
---
!quick
1. Keep **dirty** (untreated) and **clean** (boiled) containers separate and labelled.
2. **Wash hands** after the toilet and before food or handling clean water: boiled water and soap, or a dirty-water rinse followed by a boiled-water rinse.
3. Toilet at least **30 m from water** and camp; dig a cat hole 15 cm deep and cover it.
!end

## Notes
- Clean pots by scouring with sand or [[s:horsetail]], then rinse with boiled water.
- Don't wash in the stream you drink from. Carry wash water away and scatter it.
- Keep drinking bottles off the ground and the necks clean.

=== water-needs
title: How much water you need
summary: Daily water needs in UK conditions, saving water, and planning your boiling.
tags: water needs, litres, ration, sweat, planning
---
!quick
1. Resting in cool weather: about **2 litres** a day. Walking or working: **3–4 litres** or more.
2. Boil a full pot morning and evening; keep at least 1 litre of boiled water ready.
3. Save sweat: work in the cool of the day; rest in shade; keep out of the wind.
!end

## Planning
- Your steel pot sets the pace: estimate how many boils you need, and gather enough firewood before dark.
- Prefill your bottle with boiled water before bed; it keeps for days if sealed and clean.

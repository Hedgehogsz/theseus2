=== nettle
name: Common nettle
latin: Urtica dioica
aka: stinging nettle
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible, medicinal
season: Young tops Mar–Jun (and regrowth after cutting); seeds Aug–Oct; fibre stems Aug–Oct
harvest: young tops 3-6; seeds 8-10; fibre stems 8-10
habitat: Almost everywhere with rich soil: hedgerows, woodland edges, riverbanks, around farms, ruins and old campsites
summary: Stinging herb with toothed, heart-shaped leaves in opposite pairs. A top wild green when cooked and the best UK fibre for cordage.
lookalikes: white-dead-nettle
tags: greens, soup, tea, cordage, fibre, sting
wiki: Urtica dioica
---
## Identification
- Upright, unbranched square-ish stems 0.5–1.5 m, often in large patches joined by yellow roots.
- Leaves in **opposite pairs**, heart-shaped to lance-shaped, coarsely toothed, pointed tip.
- Whole plant covered in stinging hairs. Flowers are tiny greenish catkin-like tassels hanging from the leaf joints (Jun–Sep).

## Uses
- **Food:** pick the top 4–6 leaves of young plants (wear gloves or pinch firmly from underneath). Boiling, steaming or thorough wilting destroys the sting in seconds. Use like spinach, or in soup. Rich in iron and vitamin C.
- Avoid old plants once flowering is well under way: the leaves become gritty (calcium oxalate crystals, the "cystoliths") and can irritate the kidneys.
- **Tea:** steep a handful of fresh tops in boiled water for 5 minutes.
- **Cordage:** autumn stems give strong fibres. See [[a:cordage-nettle-bramble]].

## Cautions
- Stings cause a burning rash lasting hours; rub with dock if you like (a mild cooling effect at best), wash with water, and do not scratch.
- Do not harvest beside sprayed field margins or dog-walking paths.

## Identification
- Square stems; leaves in opposite pairs, heart-shaped and toothed, very like nettle but **soft with no stinging hairs**.
- White, hooded, two-lipped flowers in rings (whorls) around the stem at the leaf joints.

## Uses
- Young leaves raw in small amounts or cooked as a green; flowers can be sucked for nectar.

## Cautions
- Check you are not holding a stinging nettle before eating raw. Once flowering the white flowers make it easy.

tags: greens, salad, coffee substitute, roots, flowers

---
## Identification
- Flat rosette of hairless, deeply toothed leaves ("lion's teeth"), all from the base.
- Each **unbranched, hollow, leafless stalk** carries one yellow flower head, then a white "clock" of seeds.
- White milky sap when stalk or leaf is broken.
- Lookalikes such as cat's-ear and hawkbits have hairy leaves or branched stalks. They are also edible, so confusion is not dangerous.

## Uses
- Young leaves raw (bitter; blanching or boiling reduces it), flowers raw or in fritters, roots roasted dry and ground as a coffee substitute.
- Mild diuretic (the old name "piss-a-bed"); traditionally used for digestion.

## Cautions
- Avoid roadside and sprayed lawns.
- People allergic to daisy-family plants may react.

=== wild-garlic
name: Wild garlic
latin: Allium ursinum
aka: ramsons, bear's garlic
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible
season: Leaves Feb–May; flowers Apr–Jun; seed pods May–Jun
harvest: leaves 2-5; flowers 4-6; seed pods 5-6
habitat: Damp, shady broadleaf woodland, stream banks and hedges, often as dense carpets, especially on chalk and limestone
summary: Carpets of broad, soft leaves smelling strongly of garlic, with white star-shaped flowers. Deadly lookalikes grow in the same woods.
lookalikes: lily-of-the-valley, lords-and-ladies, bluebell, daffodil
tags: garlic, ramsons, greens, spring, pesto
wiki: Allium ursinum
---
!danger Check every leaf
Poisonous [[s:lords-and-ladies]], [[s:lily-of-the-valley]] and bluebell/daffodil leaves grow among wild garlic. Pick **leaf by leaf**, never by the handful, and crush each leaf: it must smell of garlic. Once your fingers smell of garlic the smell test fails, so check the shape of each leaf instead. See [[a:compare-wild-garlic]].
!end

## Identification
- Each leaf grows on its **own single stalk** straight from the ground: bright green, flat, elliptical, 10–25 cm, soft, with a smooth edge.
- **Strong garlic smell when crushed.**
- White, six-petalled star flowers in a loose round cluster (umbel) on a triangular stalk.

## Uses
- Leaves raw or cooked: salads, soups, pesto. Flowers and green seed pods are edible.
- Pick a few leaves per plant and leave the bulbs. Uprooting any wild plant without the landowner's permission is illegal in the UK.

lookalikes: wild-garlic, bluebell
tags: leek, garlic, onion, invasive, spring

---
## Identification
- Flower stem distinctly **three-sided** (triangular in cross-section).
- Narrow, keeled, grass-like leaves; mild onion/garlic smell.
- White bell-shaped flowers, each petal with a fine green central line, hanging to one side.

## Uses
- Leaves, stems, flowers and small bulbs all edible raw or cooked, like spring onion.
- It is an invasive non-native species: it is an offence to plant it or cause it to spread in the wild, so do not move bulbs or soil.

## Cautions
- Always confirm the onion smell. White bluebells (no smell, no green stripe) are poisonous.

## Identification
- Weak, square stems that scramble over other plants, covered in tiny backward-pointing hooks.
- Narrow leaves in **whorls of 6–8** around the stem.
- Tiny white four-petalled flowers, then round, bristly seeds in pairs that cling to fur and socks.

## Uses
- Young tips cooked as a green (the hooks soften). A handful steeped in cold water overnight makes a refreshing drink.
- A mat of stems works as a rough **strainer** for removing debris from water before boiling (this does not make water safe).
- Ripe seeds can be roasted as a caffeine-free coffee substitute (they are related to coffee).

## Cautions
- Can cause skin irritation in sensitive people.

## Identification
- Rosette of narrow, lance-shaped leaves with **3–5 strong parallel ribs**; pulling a leaf apart leaves stringy fibres.
- Leafless, grooved stalks carrying a short, dark brown flower head ringed with white anthers.

## Uses
- Young leaves are edible raw or cooked (bitter, stringy when old).
- Crushed or chewed leaf placed on nettle stings, insect bites and minor grazes is a long-standing folk remedy (soothing, mildly astringent). Clean wounds properly first: see [[a:wound-care]].
- Flower heads taste faintly of mushroom.

=== yarrow
name: Yarrow
latin: Achillea millefolium
aka: milfoil, soldier's woundwort
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible, medicinal, caution
season: Leaves all year; flowers Jun–Nov
harvest: leaves 1-12; flowers 6-11
habitat: Grassland, verges, lawns, dry banks
summary: Feathery, finely divided aromatic leaves and flat-topped clusters of small white or pink flowers. A traditional wound herb.
lookalikes: hemlock, fools-parsley
tags: wound, herb, tea, flowers
wiki: Achillea millefolium
---
## Identification
- Leaves very finely divided into many tiny segments ("thousand leaves"), soft and feathery, with a strong spicy smell.
- Flat-topped clusters of small daisy-like white (sometimes pink) flowers, each with about five short ray florets.
- Tough, hairy, ridged stems; spreads by runners.

## Uses
- A few young leaves as a bitter herb; tea from leaves and flowers.
- Traditionally applied to small cuts (it contains compounds that may help blood clot). Do not rely on it for real bleeding: use [[a:severe-bleeding]].

## Cautions
- Its flat white flower heads can be confused at a distance with deadly carrot-family plants such as [[s:hemlock]]. Yarrow's flowers are tiny daisies, not umbrella-spoked umbels, and its leaves smell spicy, not mousy.
- Avoid in pregnancy and if allergic to daisy-family plants.

=== common-sorrel
name: Common sorrel
latin: Rumex acetosa
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible, caution
season: Leaves Feb–Nov (best in spring)
harvest: leaves 2-11
habitat: Meadows, grassy verges, woodland clearings
summary: Arrow-shaped leaves with a sharp lemony taste and backward-pointing lobes clasping the stem. Edible in moderation.
lookalikes: lords-and-ladies, wood-sorrel
tags: sorrel, sour, lemon, salad

---
## Identification
- Arrow-shaped leaves with two **backward-pointing lobes** at the base; upper leaves clasp the stem.
- Sour, lemony taste (oxalic acid).
- Tall reddish spikes of tiny flowers in early summer.

## Uses
- Raw leaves add sourness to salads, or cook into sauces.

## Cautions
- High in oxalic acid: eat in moderation, and avoid it if you have kidney stones, gout or rheumatism.
- Young [[s:lords-and-ladies]] leaves are also arrow-shaped but are glossy, net-veined, unpleasantly burning and grow in woodland shade. Sorrel tastes sour, not burning.

=== hawthorn
name: Hawthorn
latin: Crataegus monogyna
aka: may, whitethorn
group: plant
kind: tree or shrub
status: edible, medicinal
season: Young leaves Apr–May; flowers May; haws (berries) Sep–Nov
harvest: young leaves 4-5; flowers 5; haws 9-11
habitat: Hedgerows everywhere, scrub, woodland edges, upland valleys
summary: Thorny hedgerow shrub with deeply lobed leaves, white "may" blossom and clusters of dark red haws in autumn.
lookalikes: blackthorn
tags: haws, hedgerow, berries, may blossom, thorns
wiki: Crataegus monogyna
---
## Identification
- Dense, thorny shrub or small tree; thorns are sharp spines on the twigs.
- Leaves deeply cut into **3–7 lobes**.
- White (sometimes pink) five-petalled flowers in May, heavily scented. Leaves appear **before** the flowers, unlike blackthorn.
- Dark red, oval haws in clusters, each with a single large stone.

## Uses
- Young leaves ("bread and cheese") raw. The flesh of ripe haws is edible raw or cooked (jelly, ketchup, fruit leather).
- Thorns can be used as awls or improvised fish hooks.

## Cautions
- **Spit out the seeds:** like apple pips they contain cyanogenic compounds.
- It has been used medicinally for the heart and can interact with heart medicines.

=== blackberry
name: Bramble (blackberry)
latin: Rubus fruticosus agg.
aka: blackberry
group: plant
kind: shrub
status: edible, medicinal
season: Young shoots Apr–Jun; berries late Jul–Sep (traditionally not after Michaelmas, 29 Sep, when they go mouldy)
harvest: young shoots 4-6; berries 7-9
habitat: Hedges, woodland, scrub, waste ground, everywhere
summary: Arching, prickly stems with leaves of 3–5 toothed leaflets and familiar black berries. Very safe and very common.
lookalikes: raspberry
tags: berries, fruit, cordage, tea, hedgerow
wiki: Rubus fruticosus
---
## Identification
- Long arching stems armed with hooked prickles, rooting where tips touch the ground.
- Leaves of 3–5 toothed leaflets, often with prickles on the midrib underneath.
- White to pink five-petalled flowers; berries green → red → black, made of many small "drupelets" that stay attached to the core when picked.

## Uses
- Ripe berries raw or cooked. Pick above dog height and away from roads.
- Young peeled shoots are edible. Dried leaves make tea, a traditional remedy for mild diarrhoea (astringent tannins).
- De-thorned stems give fibre for cordage: see [[a:cordage-nettle-bramble]].

## Cautions
- There are no poisonous UK berries built like a blackberry (made of many small drupelets), which is why it is a classic beginner forage. Still avoid mouldy fruit.

=== elder
name: Elder
latin: Sambucus nigra
group: plant
kind: shrub or small tree
status: edible, medicinal, caution
season: Flowers late May–Jun; berries Aug–Oct
harvest: flowers 5-6; berries 8-10
habitat: Hedgerows, woodland edges, waste ground, around rabbit warrens and old buildings
summary: Fast-growing shrub with pinnate leaves, flat creamy flower heads and drooping clusters of tiny black berries. Flowers and cooked berries are edible.
lookalikes: dogwood, deadly-nightshade, privet
tags: elderflower, elderberry, cordial, berries, hedgerow
wiki: Sambucus nigra
---
!warn Cook the berries
Raw elderberries, unripe berries, stalks, leaves and bark can cause vomiting and diarrhoea. **Cook ripe berries thoroughly** and remove all stalks.
!end

## Identification
- Leaves **pinnate**: 5–7 toothed leaflets in opposite pairs plus one at the tip; unpleasant smell when crushed.
- Corky, grey-brown, deeply furrowed bark; young stems have soft white pith.
- Flat, umbrella-like heads (up to 20 cm) of tiny, creamy, sweetly scented five-petalled flowers.
- Berries: masses of tiny purple-black berries (5 mm) in drooping clusters on purplish-red stalks.

## Uses
- Flowers: cordial, tea, fritters (shake out insects, don't wash away the pollen).
- Ripe berries: cooked into syrup, jam or wine. Rich in vitamin C.
- The soft pith of dry stems pushes out easily, making tubes for blowing on embers.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:dogwood]] has simple oval leaves with curved veins and berries held upright, not drooping. See [[a:compare-elder-dogwood]].
- Single shiny black berries on a herbaceous plant are [[s:deadly-nightshade]].

## Identification
- Arching stems with strong, **curved, hooked prickles**.
- Pinnate leaves of 5–7 toothed leaflets.
- Five-petalled pale pink or white flowers; glossy scarlet, oval hips 1.5–2 cm.

=== blackthorn
name: Blackthorn (sloe)
latin: Prunus spinosa
group: plant
kind: shrub
status: edible, caution
season: Flowers Mar–Apr (before leaves); sloes Sep–Nov (best after frost)
harvest: sloes 9-11
habitat: Hedgerows, scrub, woodland edges, coastal cliffs

## Identification
- Very dense shrub with dark bark and long, sharp thorns.
- Small white flowers appear in early spring **before the leaves** (hawthorn flowers after its leaves, in May).
- Small oval, finely toothed leaves.
- Sloes: round, 1–1.5 cm, blue-black with a dusty white bloom, a single stone, and an extremely sour, mouth-drying taste.

- Compare with [[s:deadly-nightshade]] berries, which are single, glossy, not dusty, held in a star-shaped green cup, on a soft non-woody plant. See [[a:compare-sloe-nightshade]].

=== sweet-chestnut
name: Sweet chestnut
latin: Castanea sativa
group: plant
kind: tree
status: edible
season: Nuts Oct–Nov
harvest: nuts 10-11
habitat: Woods and parks, especially on sandy soils in southern England
summary: Large tree with long, glossy, saw-toothed leaves and very spiny cases holding brown chestnuts. Roast them; do not confuse with poisonous conkers.
lookalikes: horse-chestnut
tags: chestnut, nuts, roast, starch
wiki: Castanea sativa
---
## Identification
- Bark with deep furrows, often spiralling around the trunk on old trees.
- Long (15–25 cm), narrow, glossy leaves with **sharp, regular saw teeth**, each tooth tipped with a bristle.
- Cases covered in **dense, fine, needle-like spines** (like a hedgehog), holding 2–3 flattened nuts with a pointed tip and tuft.

## Uses
- A rich starch food. Slit the shell and roast in embers or boil (unslit nuts explode). Can be dried and ground into flour.

## Cautions
- Do not confuse with [[s:horse-chestnut]] conkers, which are poisonous. Conkers are single, rounded and glossy, inside a thick case with a few short stubby spikes; the tree has big hand-shaped leaves.

- Upper trunk and branches with distinctive **orange-red, flaking bark**; grey-brown and plated lower down.

!warn Carrot family
This plant belongs to the carrot family (Apiaceae), which includes the UK's deadliest plants. Do not eat it unless you know it well: it grows in carpets with leaves in threes and **never** has finely divided, fern-like leaves.
!end

## Identification
- Dense carpets spreading by white underground runners.
- Leaves divided into **three groups of three** toothed leaflets (like elder leaves).
- Hollow grooved stem with **no purple spots**; white flowers in umbels in summer.

## Uses
- Young, shiny, unfolding leaves before flowering, cooked like spinach (it was introduced by the Romans as a pot herb).

!danger Do not forage unless you are an expert
Experienced foragers eat cow parsley, but its lookalikes [[s:hemlock]] and [[s:fools-parsley]] kill. With no expert to confirm it, **leave all fern-leaved white umbellifers alone.** See [[a:compare-cow-parsley]].
!end

## Identification
- Leaves fern-like, finely divided 2–3 times, soft and slightly downy, fresh green.
- Stems **hollow, grooved and slightly hairy, green or flushed purple but never spotted**; a groove runs down the inner side of the leaf stalk.
- Flat-topped white umbels of small flowers, April to early June, the first of the big umbellifers to flower.
- Smells fresh and parsley-like when crushed, not mousy.

## Compared with hemlock
- [[s:hemlock]] is taller (to 2 m+), its stems are **smooth, hairless, and blotched purple**, it flowers later (Jun–Jul), and it smells unpleasant and mousy.

lookalikes: giant-hogweed, cow-parsley, hemlock
tags: carrot family, umbellifer, phototoxic, sap, verge
wiki: Heracleum sphondylium
---
!warn Phototoxic sap
The sap makes skin burn and blister in sunlight (phytophotodermatitis), sometimes leaving brown marks for months. Wear gloves and long sleeves. If sap gets on skin, wash it off and keep the area out of sunlight for 48 hours.
!end

## Identification
- Leaves large, broad, **rough and hairy**, divided into a few coarse, lobed, toothed segments (nothing like the lacy leaves of cow parsley or hemlock).
- Stems hollow, ridged, covered in stiff bristly hairs.
- Flat white (sometimes pinkish) umbels up to 20 cm, the outer flowers with enlarged notched petals. Grows to 2 m.

## Uses
- Experienced foragers cook the young shoots. Because of the sap, and the risk of confusing it with [[s:giant-hogweed]] or [[s:hemlock]], beginners should not eat it.

## Identification
- Leaves **simple** (not divided), oval, smooth-edged, in opposite pairs, with veins that curve forward towards the tip. Tear a leaf gently and stringy latex threads hold the halves together.
- Twigs dark red, especially in winter and autumn; the leaves also turn deep red.
- Flat-topped clusters of small, four-petalled, creamy flowers with an unpleasant smell.
- Clusters of black berries held **upright** on red stalks.

## Uses
- Straight shoots were used for arrows and skewers.

## Cautions
- Berries are bitter and mildly poisonous (cause stomach upset). Don't confuse them with elderberries. See [[a:compare-elder-dogwood]].

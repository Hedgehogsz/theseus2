# More UK animals: stinging and biting hazards, shore food (where legal and safe), and protected species.

=== oak-processionary-moth
name: Oak processionary moth
latin: Thaumetopoea processionea
aka: OPM, processionary caterpillar
group: animal
kind: insect
status: caution, invasive
season: Caterpillars Apr–Jul; old nests stay hazardous all year
habitat: Oak trees, mainly in London and the surrounding counties of south-east England; occasional outbreaks elsewhere
summary: Invasive moth whose grey, hairy caterpillars live in silk nests on oaks. Their tiny hairs cause rashes, sore eyes and throat irritation. Don't touch, camp under or burn them.
lookalikes: brown-tail-moth
tags: caterpillar, hairs, rash, oak, nest, itching, invasive, processionary
wiki: Oak processionary
---
!warn Keep away from the nests
The caterpillars' hairs break off easily, blow in the wind and stay irritant for years, including in old nests on the ground. **Don't touch caterpillars or nests, don't camp or shelter under infested oaks, and don't gather firewood or kindling from them.** Never try to burn or remove a nest yourself.
!end

## Identification
- **Only on oak trees** (occasionally they wander onto nearby fences or plants).
- Caterpillars up to about 3 cm long, **grey bodies with a dark head and long white hairs**.
- They move in **nose-to-tail processions**, often in lines or arrow-head shapes down the trunk in the evening.
- Nests: **white silken webs** from golf-ball to rugby-ball size, on the trunk and the undersides of branches, turning brown and dirty with age.
- The adult moth (Jul–Sep) is a dull brown-grey moth and is harmless.

## Dangers
Each older caterpillar carries many thousands of microscopic hairs containing an irritant protein. Contact causes:
- An itchy, bumpy red rash, often on the neck, arms and face, appearing within hours.
- Sore, red, itchy eyes.
- Sore throat, coughing and, rarely, breathing difficulty in people with asthma or allergies.

Dogs and livestock are affected too.

## First aid
1. Move away from the tree. Take off contaminated outer clothing carefully, turning it inside out, and bag it. Wash it at a high temperature when you can.
2. Wash the skin gently with soap and water. Dab with sticky tape to lift hairs before washing. **Don't scratch or rub**: that drives hairs in and spreads them.
3. Rinse eyes with plenty of clean water for several minutes. Don't rub them. See [[a:eye-injury]].
4. An antihistamine helps itching. Get medical help when you can for eye pain or blurred vision, a severe or spreading rash, or any **breathing difficulty**, which is urgent ([[a:when-to-get-help]]).

## Law and reporting
It is a regulated pest. Report sightings outside the London area to the Forestry Commission (TreeAlert) when you can.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:brown-tail-moth]] caterpillars are dark brown with white tufts and **two orange-red dots near the tail**, and live on hedgerow shrubs, not oaks. Equally irritant.
- Many harmless caterpillars are hairy. If it is hairy and on an oak in summer, treat it as oak processionary.

=== brown-tail-moth
name: Brown-tail moth
latin: Euproctis chrysorrhoea
aka: browntail caterpillar
group: animal
kind: insect
status: caution
season: Caterpillars Mar–Jun; winter tents Sep–Mar
habitat: Hedgerows and scrub, especially hawthorn, blackthorn, bramble and sea buckthorn; mainly coastal south and east England, also some towns
summary: Dark brown, white-tufted caterpillars that live in tough silk tents on hedges. Their hairs cause an intensely itchy rash and can affect eyes and breathing.
lookalikes: oak-processionary-moth
tags: caterpillar, hairs, rash, hedge, itching, tent
wiki: Brown-tail
---
## Identification
- Caterpillars up to about 4 cm, **dark brown to black with broken white tufts along each side** and **two bright orange-red dots** on the back near the tail end.
- Live in groups in **dense, tough white silk tents** at the tips of shrub branches. Small caterpillars overwinter in these tents, then feed in spring, sometimes stripping whole hedges.
- The adult moth is white with a brown tuft at the tip of the abdomen and is seen in July–August.

## Dangers
- The hairs break off and cause a **very itchy rash** (like nettle rash) that can last days to weeks. Hairs also stay on tents and dead vegetation.
- They irritate eyes, and inhaled hairs can cause coughing and breathing difficulty.
- Don't push through infested hedges, use them for shelter, or cut them for cordage or firewood.

## First aid
Treat as for [[s:oak-processionary-moth]]: move away, lift hairs off with sticky tape, wash with soap and water without scrubbing, bag contaminated clothes, rinse eyes, take an antihistamine. Get medical help for breathing difficulty or eye problems.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:oak-processionary-moth]]: grey, long white hairs, on oaks only, no orange tail dots.
- Yellow-tail moth caterpillars look similar but have a red stripe along the back; avoid them too.

=== honey-bee
name: Honey bee
latin: Apis mellifera
aka: western honey bee
group: animal
kind: insect
status: caution
season: Active Mar–Oct; swarms mostly May–Jul
habitat: Everywhere with flowers; hives on farms and in gardens; wild colonies in hollow trees, walls and chimneys
summary: Slim golden-brown bee that stings once and leaves its sting behind. One sting is painful; many stings, or an allergy, are an emergency.
lookalikes: common-wasp, hornet
tags: bee, sting, swarm, hive, anaphylaxis, honey
wiki: Western honey bee
---
!danger Signs of anaphylaxis
Swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, wheezing, difficulty breathing or swallowing, a faint, weak or confused feeling, or collapse within minutes of a sting. Use their adrenaline auto-injector if they have one, and get emergency help. See [[a:stings-anaphylaxis]].
!end

## Identification
- 12–15 mm, **golden-brown to dark brown bands**, slimmer and less furry than a bumblebee, hairier than a wasp.
- Workers stream in and out of a single entrance: a hole in a tree, wall or hive.
- **Swarm**: in late spring and early summer a mass of thousands of bees may hang as a **cluster from a branch** or fly as a loud cloud.

## Dangers
- A honey bee's sting is **barbed** and stays in the skin with its venom sac. **Remove it within seconds**: scrape it out sideways with a fingernail, knife back or card. Speed matters more than method; don't waste time looking for tweezers.
- Bees defend the colony fiercely. Many stings at once (dozens or more) can make anyone seriously ill, even without an allergy.
- If attacked: **cover your face and run** in a straight line to shelter (a building, vehicle or dense scrub). Don't swat or flail. Don't jump into water: bees wait above it.

## First aid for one sting
1. Remove the sting. Wash the area.
2. Cold compress for 10–20 minutes; raise the limb. Painkillers or an antihistamine help.
3. Watch the person for anaphylaxis for the next hour. A big local swelling over the next day is common and not usually dangerous.
4. Stings in the mouth or throat (from a bee in a drink or food) can cause swelling that blocks the airway: get help urgently.

## Notes
- A hanging swarm is usually calm because it has no nest to defend. Leave it alone and keep others back; a beekeeper will collect it.
- Raiding a wild colony for honey means many stings and destroys the colony. It is not worth the risk.
- Bumblebees are rounder and furrier and rarely sting unless handled or their nest is disturbed.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:common-wasp]]: bright yellow and black, shiny, almost hairless, narrow waist. Stings repeatedly and leaves no sting behind.
- [[s:hornet]]: much bigger, brown and yellow.
- Hoverflies mimic bees and wasps but cannot sting: they hover in one spot and have only two wings.

=== cleg
name: Cleg
latin: Haematopota pluvialis
aka: horsefly, notch-horned cleg, gadfly
group: animal
kind: insect
status: caution
season: May–Sep, worst on warm, humid, thundery days
habitat: Damp woods, river banks, moorland and pasture near cattle and horses across the UK
summary: Grey, silent biting fly whose painful bite can swell badly and become infected. Larger horseflies bite the same way.
lookalikes:
tags: horsefly, horse fly, bite, cleg, gadfly, infection, insect
wiki: Haematopota pluvialis
---
## Identification
- About 10 mm, **dull grey-brown**, with **mottled grey wings held roof-like over the body** at rest.
- Large eyes with iridescent green and purple stripes in life.
- Lands **silently** on skin and you often only notice the sharp, stabbing bite. Only females bite.
- Bigger horseflies (*Tabanus* species) are up to 2.5 cm, brown, with clear wings and a loud buzz.

## Dangers
- Horsefly mouthparts slice the skin, so bites are **painful** and bleed a little.
- A raised, red, itchy lump is normal. Some people get a large swelling, blisters, dizziness or weakness.
- Bites easily become **infected**, especially if scratched with dirty fingers in camp.
- Rarely, a severe allergic reaction: treat as anaphylaxis ([[a:stings-anaphylaxis]]).

## First aid
1. Wash the bite with soap and clean water.
2. Cold compress for 10 minutes; raise the limb if it swells. An antihistamine helps itching.
3. **Don't scratch.** Keep it clean and covered.
4. Check it twice a day. Spreading redness, heat, pus, red streaks or fever over the next few days mean infection: see [[a:wound-care]] and get medical help when you can.

## Prevention
Clegs are attracted to movement, warmth, sweat and dark colours. Wear long, light-coloured sleeves and trousers on still, sultry days near livestock and water. Repellents help only partly.

=== harvest-mite
name: Harvest mite
latin: Neotrombicula autumnalis
aka: chigger, berry bug, harvest bug
group: animal
kind: arachnid
status: caution
season: Jul–Oct, worst Aug–Sep
habitat: Long grass, chalk downland, heaths, woodland edges, lawns and gardens, especially on dry, sunny ground
summary: Almost invisible orange larvae that bite where clothing is tight, causing clusters of fiercely itchy red spots that appear hours later. Harmless but miserable.
lookalikes: tick
tags: chiggers, mites, itching, bites, rash, grass
wiki: Neotrombicula autumnalis
---
## Identification
- Larvae are **0.2–0.3 mm**, **bright orange-red**. You may just see tiny orange dots clustered on a grass blade or on skin in sunlight.
- Bites are grouped where clothing presses on skin: **ankles, socks, behind the knees, waistband, groin and armpits**.
- Itching starts **several hours after** you were in the grass, so people often blame the wrong place.

## Dangers
- Intensely itchy red spots, sometimes with blisters, lasting one to two weeks.
- Not known to spread disease in the UK. The real risk is **infection from scratching**.

## What to do
1. Wash skin with soap and warm water as soon as you can after walking or sitting in grass in late summer; this removes mites before they bite.
2. Change and wash clothes.
3. Cold compresses, an antihistamine, and calamine or hydrocortisone cream if you have them. Keep nails short and don't scratch.

## Prevention
Tuck trousers into socks, use insect repellent on socks and boots, and don't sit or lie directly on grass. Use a sit mat or survival bag.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:tick]]: larger, dark, and stays attached; it must be removed. Harvest mites drop off on their own.

=== portuguese-man-o-war
name: Portuguese man o' war
latin: Physalia physalis
aka: bluebottle, man-of-war
group: animal
kind: jellyfish
status: venomous
season: Washed up mainly Aug–Nov after strong westerly winds
habitat: Open Atlantic; stranded on beaches in south-west England, Wales, Ireland and western Scotland
summary: A blue-purple gas float with long, extremely painful stinging tentacles. Stings are severe; dead ones on the beach still sting. Don't touch.
lookalikes: lions-mane-jellyfish
tags: jellyfish, sting, beach, blue, float, venom, siphonophore
wiki: Portuguese man o' war
---
!danger If stung
Get out of the water. Rinse with **seawater**, lift off tentacles with tweezers or a card (not bare fingers), then soak in **very hot water** (as hot as they can bear, without scalding) for at least 30 minutes. Get urgent help for difficulty breathing, chest pain, collapse, a large area stung, or a child stung.
!end

## Identification
- Not a true jellyfish but a colony of animals. A **translucent blue, purple or pink balloon-like float**, 10–30 cm long, with a crest on top.
- **Blue tentacles** hang beneath. They are usually several metres long and can be much longer.
- Often washed up in numbers. Stranded floats look like blue plastic bags.

## Dangers
- Intense burning pain at once, with **raised red welts in lines** where tentacles touched.
- Can cause vomiting, muscle cramps, faintness, breathing difficulty and, rarely, heart problems or collapse. Children and people with heart or lung disease are at most risk.
- Stranded or dead ones **still sting**, and detached tentacles in the water sting too.

## First aid
1. Get the person out of the water and calm. A stung swimmer can panic and drown.
2. Rinse the area with seawater. **Not fresh water**, which can fire more stinging cells.
3. Remove tentacles with tweezers, a stick or the edge of a bank card. Protect your own hands.
4. Immerse in, or pour on, very hot water for at least 30 minutes (test it with your own hand first). Use hot flannels if you can't soak it.
5. Painkillers. Watch for breathing problems for the next few hours.
6. **Don't** use vinegar, urine or ice, and don't rub or bandage the area tightly.

Get medical help when you can for severe or lasting pain, stings to the face or genitals, or signs of infection later. See [[a:coast-and-tides]] for beach safety.

## Lookalikes
- The **by-the-wind sailor** (*Velella*) is a small, flat blue disc with a clear sail and no long tentacles; it is harmless but often stranded alongside. If you are not sure, don't touch.
- [[s:lions-mane-jellyfish]]: a red-brown bell, not a gas float.

=== compass-jellyfish
name: Compass jellyfish
latin: Chrysaora hysoscella
group: animal
kind: jellyfish
status: venomous
season: Jun–Oct
habitat: Coastal waters and beaches, mainly southern and western Britain
summary: Whitish jellyfish with brown V-shaped markings radiating like a compass. Its long tentacles give a painful sting, even when stranded.
lookalikes: lions-mane-jellyfish, moon-jellyfish
tags: jellyfish, sting, beach, sea
wiki: Chrysaora hysoscella
---
## Identification
- Bell up to 30 cm across, **creamy white to yellowish**, with **16 brown V-shaped marks** radiating from a brown centre like a compass rose.
- A brown-scalloped rim with **24 long, thin tentacles** in groups, and four long frilly mouth arms.

## Dangers
Painful, burning stings with red lines, lasting hours. Not usually dangerous, but a large sting area, a child, or a person with a reaction needs watching.

## First aid
Get out of the water, rinse with seawater, lift off tentacles with tweezers or a card, then soak in very hot water for at least 30 minutes and take painkillers. Don't use vinegar or urine, and don't rub. Get urgent help for difficulty breathing or chest pain.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:moon-jellyfish]]: clear, with four purple rings and a weak sting.
- [[s:lions-mane-jellyfish]]: red-brown, with a dense mane of tentacles; worse sting.
- Barrel jellyfish: large, rubbery, white-blue dome with thick frilly arms and no long tentacles; sting is mild.

=== common-toad
name: Common toad
latin: Bufo bufo
group: animal
kind: amphibian
status: poisonous, info
season: Active Feb–Oct; migrates to breeding ponds Feb–Apr, often across roads at night
habitat: Woods, gardens, fields and heaths across Great Britain (not Ireland); breeds in larger, deeper ponds and lakes
summary: Warty brown amphibian that crawls rather than hops. Its skin glands make a poison: never eat a toad or its spawn, and wash your hands after handling.
lookalikes:
tags: toad, frog, amphibian, poison, spawn, pond, dog
wiki: Common toad
---
## Identification
- 8–13 cm, squat, with **dry, warty skin**, brown, olive or grey.
- **Copper-coloured eyes** with a horizontal pupil.
- Bulging **glands behind the eyes** (parotoid glands).
- **Walks or crawls**; makes only short hops.
- Spawn is laid in **long double strings** wound round water plants, not clumps.

## Dangers
- The skin glands release toxins (bufotoxins) when the toad is stressed. **Eating a toad or its spawn can cause vomiting and dangerous heart-rhythm problems.** It is never a survival food.
- Handling is safe for the skin, but the secretion stings eyes, the mouth and cuts. Wash your hands before eating or touching your face.
- Dogs that mouth a toad drool, foam and paw at the mouth. Rinse the dog's mouth with water from the side, without making it swallow.

If someone has eaten part of a toad, get medical help urgently. See [[a:poisoning]].

## Law
The common toad is a priority conservation species, and selling one is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. The rare **natterjack toad** (yellow stripe down the back; coastal dunes and heaths) is fully protected: don't handle it.

## Lookalikes
- **Common frog**: smooth, moist skin, a dark patch behind the eye, long legs, hops; spawn in clumps. Frogs are not a safe or legal food either: amphibians carry parasites and bacteria, and many are protected.

=== great-crested-newt
name: Great crested newt
latin: Triturus cristatus
aka: warty newt, northern crested newt
group: animal
kind: amphibian
status: protected
season: In ponds Feb–Jul; on land under logs and stones the rest of the year
habitat: Ponds without fish in lowland England, Wales and southern Scotland; nearby grassland, scrub, hedges and woodland
summary: The UK's largest newt: dark, warty, with a bright orange, black-blotched belly. Strictly protected: even disturbing it is an offence.
lookalikes:
tags: newt, amphibian, protected, pond, log
wiki: Northern crested newt
---
## Identification
- Up to about 15–17 cm, much bigger than the smooth or palmate newt.
- **Dark brown to black, rough warty skin** with white speckles on the flanks.
- **Bright orange or yellow belly with irregular black blotches**.
- Breeding males have a tall, jagged crest along the back and a separate tail crest with a silvery-white stripe.

## Law
Great crested newts and their habitats are fully protected (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981; Conservation of Habitats and Species Regulations 2017 in England and Wales; Conservation (Natural Habitats, &c.) Regulations 1994 in Scotland). It is an offence to:
- deliberately capture, injure or kill one, or disturb it;
- damage or destroy its breeding or resting places;
- possess one, alive or dead.

## Practical notes
- When you lift logs, stones or old boards for firewood or shelter material, check underneath. **If you find a newt, put the cover back gently** and use something else.
- Don't take water from, or wash in, small ponds where newts breed if there is another source.
- Its skin secretions irritate eyes and mouths. Wash your hands if you touch one by accident.

=== smooth-snake
name: Smooth snake
latin: Coronella austriaca
group: animal
kind: reptile
status: protected, info
season: Active Apr–Oct
habitat: Dry lowland heath with heather and gorse on sandy soil: Dorset, Hampshire (New Forest) and Surrey, with small reintroduced populations elsewhere in southern England
summary: Rare, harmless grey-brown snake with rows of small dark spots and a dark eye-stripe. Often mistaken for an adder. Strictly protected.
lookalikes: adder, grass-snake
tags: snake, heath, harmless, protected, rare, dorset
wiki: Smooth snake
---
## Identification
- Up to about 60 cm, **slender**, grey, brown or reddish, with **smooth, glossy scales**.
- **Two rows of small dark spots or short bars** down the back, sometimes joined like a ladder. No continuous zig-zag.
- **Dark stripe from the nostril through the eye** to the neck; a dark heart- or crown-shaped patch on top of the head.
- **Round pupils.** Very secretive; mostly hidden in heather or under objects.

## Dangers
Not venomous. It may bite and hold on if handled; the bite is harmless. Wash it with soap and water.

## Law
One of the UK's rarest reptiles and a European protected species: it is an offence to deliberately capture, kill, injure or disturb it, or to damage its habitat. Don't pick it up or move it.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:adder]]: stockier, **bold zig-zag** down the back, red eye with a **vertical slit pupil**. If you can't be sure, assume it is an adder and step away.
- [[s:grass-snake]]: larger, olive-green, with a yellow and black collar; near water.

=== hedgehog
name: Hedgehog
latin: Erinaceus europaeus
group: animal
kind: mammal
status: protected, info
season: Active Mar–Nov (nocturnal); hibernates Nov–Mar
habitat: Gardens, hedgerows, woodland edges, parks and farmland across the UK
summary: Spiny, nocturnal insect-eater that hibernates in leaf piles and brushwood. Check heaps before you burn them. Protected from trapping and cruelty; not a food.
lookalikes:
tags: hedgehog, spines, bonfire, brash pile, nocturnal, protected
wiki: European hedgehog
---
## Identification
- 20–30 cm, covered in **brown, cream-tipped spines**, with a pointed face and small dark eyes. Rolls into a ball when alarmed.
- Loud snuffling and snorting at night.
- Droppings: dark, shiny, sausage-shaped, 3–5 cm, full of beetle fragments.
- Hibernation nests are tight balls of leaves under hedges, in brushwood, log and compost heaps.

## Practical notes
- **Before you light a fire on a pile of brushwood, check it**, or better, move the material to a fresh spot just before lighting. Hedgehogs, toads and other animals shelter in heaps, especially in autumn.
- A hedgehog out in daylight is usually ill or in trouble. If you can help, put it in a high-sided box with a towel and give it water and meaty pet food. **Never give milk or bread.**
- Wear gloves to handle one. They carry fleas, ticks, ringworm (a fungal skin infection) and *Salmonella*. Wash your hands afterwards.

## Law
Hedgehogs are declining. It is illegal to kill or take them using traps, snares and other banned methods (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, Schedule 6), and cruelty to any wild mammal is an offence (Wild Mammals (Protection) Act 1996).

=== otter
name: Eurasian otter
latin: Lutra lutra
aka: otter
group: animal
kind: mammal
status: protected, info
season: All year; mostly at night, but by day on Scottish coasts
habitat: Rivers, lakes, lochs, marshes and rocky coasts across the UK, especially Scotland, Wales and western England
summary: Large, sleek, brown aquatic mammal with a thick tapering tail. Its fishy-smelling droppings on rocks show clean water and fish. Fully protected.
lookalikes: american-mink
tags: otter, river, spraint, tracks, holt, protected, coast
wiki: Eurasian otter
---
## Identification
- Body 60–80 cm plus a 35–45 cm **thick, tapering tail**; much larger than a mink.
- **Brown fur with a pale cream throat**, broad flat head, small ears, whiskers.
- Swims low with just the head showing, then dives smoothly.
- **Spraint** (droppings): dark, tarry, full of fish bones and scales, with a **sweet, musky smell** often likened to jasmine tea. Left on prominent rocks, logs and under bridges.
- **Tracks**: round, about 6–7 cm wide, with five toes; webbing sometimes shows in soft mud.

## Practical notes
- Otter signs mean the river holds fish, though they do not show the water is safe to drink untreated. See [[a:fishing-basics]].
- Otters are shy. A cornered or trapped otter bites hard. Keep dogs away.

## Law
A European protected species. It is an offence to kill, injure, capture or disturb an otter, or to damage or block its den (holt or couch).

## Lookalikes
- [[s:american-mink]]: half the size, almost black, white chin, thinner bushy tail, bounds along banks; foul-smelling droppings.

=== american-mink
name: American mink
latin: Neogale vison
aka: mink
group: animal
kind: mammal
status: invasive, caution
season: All year
habitat: Rivers, canals, lakes, marshes and coasts across most of the UK
summary: Small, near-black, ferret-like predator escaped from fur farms. It kills water voles, seabirds and poultry, and bites hard if cornered.
lookalikes: otter
tags: mink, invasive, river, poultry, bite, predator
wiki: American mink
---
## Identification
- Head and body 30–45 cm, tail 13–23 cm and **fairly bushy**; about the size of a ferret or a small cat.
- **Glossy dark chocolate-brown to black** all over, with a **small white patch on the chin**.
- Moves with a bounding, humped gait along banks; swims well with head and back showing.
- Droppings are twisted, full of fur, feathers or fish bones, with a **foul smell**.

## Dangers
- Mink are fierce when cornered or trapped and can bite deeply. **Don't try to handle one.**
- Clean any bite thoroughly and watch for infection. See [[a:wound-care]].
- They raid food stores, poultry and fish catches: keep food sealed and hung up.

## Law
An invasive non-native species. It is illegal to release a mink or allow one to escape into the wild (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981). Control is done by landowners or trained people with approved traps.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:otter]]: twice as long, brown with a pale throat, thick tapering tail, sweet-smelling droppings. Protected: never harm it.

=== grey-seal
name: Grey seal
latin: Halichoerus grypus
aka: Atlantic grey seal
group: animal
kind: mammal
status: caution, protected
season: All year; pups born Aug–Jan (earlier in the south-west, later on the east coast)
habitat: Rocky coasts, islands, sandbanks and beaches all round the UK, especially Scotland, Wales, Cornwall, Norfolk and Lincolnshire
summary: Large seal with a long "Roman" nose. Can bite hard if approached, and bites infect easily. Leave pups alone: they are rarely abandoned.
lookalikes:
tags: seal, beach, pup, bite, seal finger, coast
wiki: Grey seal
---
## Identification
- Bulls up to about 2.5 m, cows smaller. Grey to brown, with darker or paler blotches.
- **Long, straight or arched "Roman" nose**; **nostrils almost parallel**.
- Pups have **white fluffy coats** for their first two to three weeks.
- The smaller **common (harbour) seal** has a rounded, dog-like face and V-shaped nostrils.

## Dangers
- Seals are wild animals with strong jaws. They bite people who get close, try to touch or feed them, or come between them and the sea. Bites have also happened to swimmers and to dogs.
- **Seal bites and handling seals can cause "seal finger"**: a hugely painful, swollen finger or joint days later, which needs specific antibiotics. Clean any bite or graze well ([[a:wound-care]]) and **tell the doctor about seal contact** when you get medical help.
- Don't touch dead seals.

## Behaviour around seals
- Stay well back (at least 50 m on land), quiet and downwind. If a seal looks at you, raises its head or moves towards the sea, you are too close.
- Keep dogs on a lead.
- **A pup alone on a beach is normal**: the mother feeds at sea and returns. Don't approach, touch or move it.
- In the water, if a seal comes close, swim calmly away. Don't reach out to it.

## Law
Seals are protected throughout the UK. It is an offence to kill, injure or take one (Conservation of Seals Act 1970 in England and Wales; Marine (Scotland) Act 2010, which also makes it an offence to harass seals at designated haul-out sites in Scotland).

=== herring-gull

## Identification
- 55–65 cm long, **pale grey back**, white head and body, **black wingtips with white spots**.
- **Pink legs**; yellow bill with a **red spot** near the tip; pale yellow eye.
- Young birds are mottled brown for their first few years.
- The lesser black-backed gull is similar but has a dark grey back and yellow legs.

## Dangers
- From April to August adults defend nests and chicks (on roofs, cliffs and dunes). They swoop, call, defecate and can **strike your head with their feet**, sometimes drawing blood.
- Grounded fluffy or brown chicks in July–August are normal and still guarded by the parents. Don't approach them.
- Gulls snatch food from hands and open packs. Eat with your back to a wall, keep food covered, and never feed them.

## If attacked
Hold an arm, stick or bag above your head, keep your face down and walk steadily away from the nest area. Clean any scratch from a beak or claw well.

## Law and health
- All wild birds, their nests and eggs are protected under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 (in Scotland as amended by the Wildlife and Natural Environment (Scotland) Act 2011). Herring gulls are red-listed and can be controlled only under a specific licence. **Taking eggs is illegal.**
- **Don't touch sick or dead wild birds**: avian influenza ("bird flu") has killed many UK seabirds.

## Identification
- About the size of a large gull but **heavier and bulkier**, **dark brown with paler streaks**.
- Hooked black bill. **Bold white flashes at the base of the wings**, obvious in flight.
- Deep, barking calls as it comes in low and fast at head height.

## Dangers
- Nesting skuas attack people and dogs that enter their territory on open moorland. They come in low from behind and **strike the head with their feet**. Injuries are usually minor cuts and bruises, but a fall on rough ground is the bigger risk.
- **Arctic skuas** (smaller, slimmer, with pointed central tail feathers) and **Arctic terns** (small, white, with a black cap and red bill) do the same; terns peck and often draw blood from the scalp.

## What to do
1. **Hold a walking pole, stick or arm straight up above your head**: they aim at the highest point. A hat helps.
2. Keep walking steadily out of the area. Don't run on rough ground, don't wave things at them, and never strike them.
3. Stick to paths; a bird that keeps attacking means you are near its nest or chick.

## Law and health
Protected, like all wild birds and their nests and eggs (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 as amended in Scotland). Great skuas have been hit badly by avian influenza: **don't touch sick or dead seabirds**.

## Identification
- Huge, **all white**, about 1.5 m long, holds its neck in a graceful **S-curve**.
- **Orange bill with a black knob** at the base, larger in males (cobs).
- Cygnets are grey-brown.
- Winter visitors (whooper and Bewick's swans) have **yellow and black bills** and straight necks.

## Dangers
- A swan defending its nest or young **hisses, raises its wings over its back and charges**, then may strike with its wings and bite. Injuries are usually bruises, but it can knock over children, attack dogs, and tip paddleboarders or canoeists into cold water.
- Give nests and families a wide berth (at least 20–30 m). Back away calmly while facing the bird. Keep dogs on a lead near water in spring.

## Law
Protected under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, with its nests and eggs. Unmarked mute swans on open water are also traditionally claimed by the Crown. Killing, taking or eating one is illegal.

## Identification
- Oval shell up to about 25 cm across, **reddish-brown**, with a **frilled "pie-crust" edge**.
- Large, heavy claws with **black tips**.
- Small ones hide under boulders and in crevices at low tide; bigger ones live below the tide line.

## Law
- Recreational crabbing from the shore needs no licence, but **minimum sizes apply** (they vary by area, typically about 13–16 cm across the widest part of the shell). Return smaller ones.
- Return **egg-carrying ("berried") females** (a mass of eggs under the tail flap) and soft, recently moulted crabs. Taking them is illegal in many areas.
- Using pots or nets may need a local permit and marked gear. Local byelaws are set by Inshore Fisheries and Conservation Authorities in England and by the Welsh and Scottish governments.

## Dangers
- A big crab's claw can crush or cut a finger. Pick it up from behind, holding the shell at the back, away from the claws.
- Turning boulders on a rising tide is how people get cut off: watch the sea. See [[a:coast-and-tides]].

## As food
- **Only cook crabs that were alive.** A crab found dead may already be spoiling and can cause food poisoning.
- Kill it quickly before cooking. Chilling it in cold air first makes it sluggish.
- Boil in salted water (seawater is fine) for about 15 minutes for a small crab and up to 20 minutes for a large one, until the shell is bright orange-red and the meat is white and opaque throughout.
- Remove and throw away the feathery grey gills ("dead man's fingers") and the stomach sac behind the mouth.
- The **brown meat** concentrates heavy metals such as cadmium and, at times, algal toxins: eat it in moderation and mostly eat the white meat. Eat cooked crab the same day.

## Lookalikes
No dangerous UK lookalikes. The green **shore crab** is small with little meat, and the **velvet swimming crab** has red eyes and paddle-shaped back legs. Both are edible but bite readily. The safety risk with crab is freshness, not species.

## Identification
- **Cone-shaped shell** 3–6 cm across, grey-white to greenish, with ridges radiating from the peak.
- Clamped tight to rock; leaves an oval scar where it lives.
- Underneath: an orange-yellow muscular foot.

## Gathering
- Approach quietly and knock it **sideways** in one firm blow with a flat stone, or slide a blade under the edge before it notices. Once it clamps down it is almost impossible to move, and the shell breaks.
- Take a few from each rock rather than stripping one area.
- Limpets are grazers rather than filter feeders, so they are a lower biotoxin risk than [[s:mussel|mussels]] and [[s:cockle|cockles]], but they still pick up pollution. Avoid harbours, outfalls and shores below towns, and check for any local shellfish warning notices. See [[a:shellfish-shore]].

## As food
- Roast upside down in the shell on embers, or boil for about 5 minutes, until the meat lifts free.
- Cut off and discard the dark gut sac; eat the pale foot. It is chewy: slice it thinly or stew it.
- Discard any that smell bad or were not clamped tightly to the rock when you found them.

## Lookalikes
No dangerous lookalikes. The slipper limpet (a non-native species stacked on other shells, with a shelf inside) and keyhole limpet (a hole at the peak) are different shapes. [[s:periwinkle]] is a coiled snail, also edible.

!danger Tides and quicksand
Cockle beds are on wide, flat sands where the tide comes in **faster than you can walk** and fills channels behind you. Soft mud and quicksand can trap your legs. Go only on a falling tide, start back well before low water, know the tide times, and never go out in fog or alone. See [[a:coast-and-tides]].
!end

## Identification
- Rounded, **heart-shaped from the side**, 2–5 cm, cream, yellowish or brown, with **22–28 strong ribs** radiating from the hinge.
- Live 2–5 cm down in sand. Look for small paired holes or slight bumps on the surface; rake or dig by hand.

## Law
- Many cockle beds are regulated fisheries. There may be permits, open seasons, minimum sizes and **daily personal limits**, and beds are closed when stocks are low or the shellfish are contaminated. Read any notices at the access point.
- Never gather from areas marked as closed.

## Shellfish safety
- Cockles filter seawater, so they concentrate **sewage bacteria and viruses (norovirus)** and **algal toxins**. Toxins are **not destroyed by cooking**.
- Official monitoring by the Food Standards Agency (Food Standards Scotland in Scotland) and local councils closes areas when toxins or pollution are found. Closures can happen in any month. Respect any warning signs.
- Avoid estuaries below towns, outfalls, harbours and marinas, and don't gather for a few days after heavy rain, when sewage overflows wash out.
- The old rule of only eating shellfish "when there is an R in the month" (Sep–Apr) helps a little, because algal blooms and spoilage are worse in warm months. **It does not make shellfish safe.**
- **Paralytic shellfish poisoning** starts within minutes to a few hours: tingling or numbness of the lips, mouth and fingers, then weakness and difficulty breathing. It is an emergency with no antidote: get urgent help and be ready to start rescue breaths. See [[a:shellfish-shore]] and [[a:poisoning]].

## As food
1. Gather only live cockles: shells closed, or closing when tapped. Discard broken or gaping ones.
2. Purge them in a bucket of clean seawater for several hours, changing the water, so they spit out sand.
3. Boil or steam until the shells open, then **keep cooking for a further 2 minutes**. Commercial cockles are heated to 90 °C for 90 seconds to kill norovirus.
4. Throw away any that stay shut.

## Lookalikes
Other buried clams look different (smooth, oval or elongated), and dead empty shells are common. If it is not ribbed and heart-shaped, or is not alive and closed, don't eat it. [[s:mussel]] grows attached to rocks, not buried in sand.

## Identification
- Usually 30–50 cm inshore, bigger offshore.
- **Bronze, olive or greenish-brown back**, golden-brown sides, pale belly.
- **Dark lateral line that curves sharply up over the pectoral fin.**
- **Lower jaw sticks out** beyond the upper; **no chin barbel**. Three dorsal fins.

## Law
- No licence is needed for recreational sea fishing from the shore in the UK.
- Minimum sizes and local byelaws apply. **Pollack stocks are low, and in recent years there have been daily bag limits, and at times catch-and-release-only rules, for recreational anglers in UK waters.** Check current rules where you can; if in doubt, release it.
- Selling fish you caught recreationally is illegal.

## As food
- Kill it quickly, gut it at once and keep it cool. See [[a:fishing-basics]].
- Cod-family fish often contain **parasitic worms** (small coiled nematodes) in the gut and flesh. Remove any you see and **cook thoroughly**, until the flesh is opaque and flakes easily. Don't eat it raw.
- Fry, bake in foil or leaves on embers, or add to stews.

- **Cod**: has a chin barbel and a pale lateral line. Also edible.
- [[s:mackerel]]: blue-green with black wavy stripes; easy to tell apart.

## Identification
- Long, torpedo-shaped body, up to 1 m or more.
- **Olive-green with rows of pale cream spots or bars**; pale belly.
- **Dorsal fin set far back near the tail**, above the anal fin.
- **Long, flat, duck-bill snout**; huge mouth with **hundreds of sharp, backward-pointing teeth**.

## Dangers
- Teeth and gill rakers **slash fingers badly**. Never put your hand in its mouth. Use long-nosed pliers or forceps to unhook it, and control it by laying it on wet grass rather than gripping hard.
- Pike bite through ordinary line, so lures are often lost; a wire trace is used.
- Clean any cut well. Freshwater wounds can become infected ([[a:wound-care]]).

## Law
- **England and Wales**: you need a rod licence and the permission of whoever owns the fishing. The coarse fishing close season applies on rivers and streams (15 Mar–15 Jun). National rules allow you to take **only one pike, up to 65 cm, per day**, unless local rules are stricter. Many waters are catch-and-release only. See [[a:fishing-basics]].
- **Scotland**: no rod licence or national close season for pike, but you still need permission.
- Returning or moving live fish between waters is regulated: don't do it.

## As food
- Lean white flesh with many fine **Y-shaped bones**. Fillet carefully or cook whole and pick the flesh off the bones.
- Freshwater fish can carry **tapeworm larvae and other parasites**: **always cook thoroughly**, never raw or lightly smoked.
- Don't eat fish from water with a blue-green algal bloom, or from polluted water.

## Lookalikes
No dangerous lookalikes. Zander (a different predator found in some English rivers and canals) has a spiny front dorsal fin and glassy eyes; it is also edible.

## Identification
- Long, snake-like fish up to about 1 m, **slimy**, olive-brown with a yellowish belly (or silvery when about to migrate).
- **Small pectoral fins just behind the head**; a single long fin runs along the back, round the tail and under the belly.
- Lower jaw slightly longer than the upper.
- **Lampreys** look similar but have a round sucker mouth instead of jaws and a row of seven gill holes.

## Law
- Eel numbers have collapsed and the species is critically endangered.
- **England and Wales**: anglers must **return all eels** they catch, alive and unharmed. Taking them needs an authorisation you will not have.
- **Scotland**: fishing for or taking eels is illegal without authorisation.
- Moving or selling eels is tightly controlled.

## Handling
- If you catch one by accident, unhook it with wet hands or a wet cloth (or cut the line close to the mouth if it has swallowed the hook) and release it at once into the same water.
- Eel blood is toxic when raw and stings eyes and cuts. Wash your hands after handling.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:grass-snake]] swims well in ponds and rivers, but has scales, a yellow-and-black collar and no fins, and holds its head out of the water.

# Poisonous and deadly UK plants (and close lookalikes of edible plants).

=== hemlock
name: Hemlock
latin: Conium maculatum
aka: poison hemlock
group: plant
kind: herb
status: deadly
season: Leaves from late winter; flowers Jun–Jul; dead stems stand into winter
habitat: Damp ground: ditches, riverbanks, rough verges, field edges, waste ground and rubbish tips
summary: Tall, smooth carrot-family plant with purple-blotched stems, lacy leaves and a mousy smell. Every part can kill.
lookalikes: cow-parsley, fools-parsley, hemlock-water-dropwort, common-hogweed
tags: carrot family, umbellifer, coniine, socrates, purple spots
wiki: Conium maculatum
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
All parts contain coniine, which paralyses the muscles, including those used for breathing. Even small amounts of leaf or root can kill. Wash your hands after touching it. Never use the hollow stems as drinking straws or whistles. If anyone may have eaten it, call **999** at once: see [[a:poisoning]].
!end

## Identification
- Tall (1.5–2.5 m), branched, with **smooth, hairless, waxy stems blotched with purple-red spots or streaks**. This is the key feature.
- Leaves large, finely divided (fern-like, 2–4 times pinnate), dark glossy green, hairless.
- Small white flowers in many umbrella-like umbels (Jun–Jul), later than cow parsley.
- **Unpleasant "mousy" or musty smell** when crushed.
- White, carrot-like taproot.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:cow-parsley]]: shorter, downy (slightly hairy) stems without blotches, fresh smell, flowers in Apr–May.
- See the full comparison: [[a:compare-cow-parsley]].

=== hemlock-water-dropwort
name: Hemlock water-dropwort
latin: Oenanthe crocata
aka: dead man's fingers
group: plant
kind: herb
status: deadly
season: Leaves from Nov (green through winter); flowers Jun–Jul
habitat: Wet ground beside streams, rivers, ditches, canals and marshes, especially in the south and west of Britain
summary: Probably the UK's most poisonous plant: a lush, celery-scented waterside umbellifer whose finger-like roots kill quickly.
lookalikes: watercress, fools-watercress, wild-celery, ground-elder, cow-parsley, water-mint
tags: carrot family, umbellifer, dead man's fingers, wetland, oenanthotoxin, roots
wiki: Oenanthe crocata
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
The roots ("dead man's fingers") contain oenanthotoxin, which causes violent seizures and kills within hours. A piece of root can be fatal. Leaves and stems are also very toxic. Do not eat **any** carrot-family plant that grows by water. Call **999** for any suspected ingestion.
!end

## Identification
- Robust, hairless, to 1.5 m, often in big clumps right at the water's edge.
- Leaves bright, shiny green, divided 3–4 times into **broad, flat, wedge-shaped, toothed leaflets** (like flat-leaf parsley or celery).
- Hollow, grooved stems; the cut stem and roots ooze a sap that turns yellow-orange in air.
- Smells of celery or parsley (it does **not** smell bad, which makes it dangerous).
- Roots: a cluster of fat, pale, **finger-like tubers**, often washed out and exposed on riverbanks.
- Domed white umbels (Jun–Jul).

## Lookalikes
- Wild celery, wild parsnip, [[s:watercress]] (four-petalled flowers, no umbels), [[s:ground-elder]], [[s:cow-parsley]].

=== fools-parsley
name: Fool's parsley
latin: Aethusa cynapium
group: plant
kind: herb
status: poisonous
season: Flowers Jun–Oct
habitat: Gardens, cultivated fields, waste ground
summary: Small garden umbellifer like flat-leaf parsley but with long, hanging "beards" of bracts under each small umbel. Poisonous.
lookalikes: cow-parsley, hemlock
tags: carrot family, umbellifer, garden weed, parsley
wiki: Aethusa cynapium
---
## Identification
- 10–80 cm; dark green, glossy, finely divided leaves like flat-leaf parsley, with an unpleasant smell when crushed.
- **Each small umbel has 3–4 long, thin bracts hanging down on the outer side only**, like a beard. This is the giveaway.
- Small white umbels, summer to autumn.

## Cautions
- Contains alkaloids similar to hemlock's. It causes burning in the mouth, vomiting and, in larger amounts, paralysis. Its presence in gardens is the reason never to eat "wild parsley".

=== giant-hogweed
name: Giant hogweed
latin: Heracleum mantegazzianum
group: plant
kind: herb
status: poisonous, invasive
season: Leaves from Mar; flowers Jun–Jul
habitat: Riverbanks, waste ground, railway lines and roadsides, spreading along watercourses
summary: Enormous umbellifer (3–5 m) with purple-blotched bristly stems and umbels up to 80 cm across. Its sap causes severe burns in sunlight.
lookalikes: common-hogweed, hemlock
tags: carrot family, umbellifer, phototoxic, burns, invasive, blisters
wiki: Heracleum mantegazzianum
---
!danger Do not touch
The sap, even from a brush against a broken leaf, makes skin react to sunlight. It causes severe, blistering burns that can scar, and skin may stay light-sensitive for years. Sap in the eyes can cause blindness.
!end

## Identification
- Huge: **3–5 m tall**, with stems up to 10 cm thick, hollow, covered in **purple blotches and stiff bristles**.
- Leaves deeply cut and jagged, up to 1.5 m or more across.
- White umbels up to **80 cm across**.

## If sap touches skin
1. Cover the area from sunlight at once.
2. Wash thoroughly with soap and water as soon as possible.
3. Keep it out of sunlight for at least 48 hours.
4. Blisters: treat like burns ([[a:burns]]) and seek medical help. Sap in the eyes: rinse with plenty of clean water and get urgent help.

## The law
- It is an invasive species. Causing it to grow in the wild is an offence.

=== cowbane
name: Cowbane
latin: Cicuta virosa
aka: water hemlock
group: plant
kind: herb
status: deadly
season: Flowers Jul–Aug
habitat: Shallow water and swamps by lakes, fens and ditches; local (Norfolk Broads, Cheshire/Shropshire meres, parts of Scotland and Ireland)
summary: Rare but extremely poisonous waterside umbellifer whose hollow rootstock is divided into chambers. Related to the North American water hemlocks.
lookalikes: hemlock-water-dropwort, fools-watercress
tags: carrot family, umbellifer, cicutoxin, water hemlock, wetland
wiki: Cicuta virosa
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
Contains cicutoxin, which causes violent seizures. A small piece of rootstock can kill an adult.
!end

## Identification
- 50–150 cm, hairless, hollow stems.
- Leaves 2–3 times divided into long, narrow, sharply toothed leaflets.
- The swollen rootstock, cut lengthways, is **hollow with cross-walls (chambered)**.
- White umbels in summer.

## Cautions
- It is one more reason never to eat **any** umbellifer growing in or beside water.

=== foxglove
name: Foxglove
latin: Digitalis purpurea
group: plant
kind: herb
status: deadly
season: Leaf rosettes all year; flowers Jun–Sep (second year)
habitat: Woodland clearings, heaths, hedge banks, acid soils, gardens
summary: Tall spikes of purple tubular bell flowers spotted inside. The soft leaves are deadly, and the rosettes can be confused with comfrey or borage.
lookalikes: comfrey, burdock
tags: digitalis, heart, glycosides, bells
wiki: Digitalis purpurea
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
All parts contain cardiac glycosides (digitoxin), which cause vomiting, a slow and irregular heartbeat, visual disturbance (yellow vision), and cardiac arrest. Call **999** for any ingestion.
!end

## Identification
- First year: a rosette of large, soft, grey-green, **downy** leaves, finely toothed, with a network of veins and winged stalks.
- Second year: a single tall spike (1–2 m) of hanging, tubular, purple-pink (sometimes white) flowers, all on one side, spotted dark inside.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:comfrey]] leaves are rough and bristly, with no teeth, and their bases run down the stem.
- Young [[s:burdock]] leaves are woolly only underneath and have hollow stalks.

=== deadly-nightshade
name: Deadly nightshade
latin: Atropa belladonna
aka: belladonna
group: plant
kind: herb
status: deadly
season: Flowers Jun–Aug; berries Aug–Oct
habitat: Woodland edges and clearings, scrub, old quarries and ruins on chalk and limestone (uncommon, mainly south and east England)
summary: Bushy plant with dull, bell-shaped purple-brown flowers and glossy black, cherry-like berries sitting in a green star. A few berries can kill a child.
lookalikes: blackthorn, bilberry, elder, black-nightshade, bittersweet
tags: belladonna, atropine, berries, black berries
wiki: Atropa belladonna
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
Contains atropine and related alkaloids: dilated pupils, blurred vision, fast heart, dry hot flushed skin, confusion, hallucinations, seizures. The berries taste sweet, and a few can kill a child. Call **999**: see [[a:poisoning]].
!end

## Identification
- Bushy herbaceous plant, 0.5–1.5 m (dies back each winter; not woody like a sloe bush).
- Large, oval, smooth-edged leaves, often in unequal pairs (one big, one small).
- Single, dull **purple-brown, bell-shaped flowers** (2.5 cm) hanging from the leaf joints.
- **Single** shiny black berries the size of a cherry (1–1.5 cm), each **sitting in a green, five-pointed star** (the calyx).

## Lookalikes
- Sloes from [[s:blackthorn]] have a dusty bloom and grow on a thorny woody shrub. [[s:bilberry]] berries are small, grow on a dwarf shrub on moorland, and have a crown scar. See [[a:compare-sloe-nightshade]].

=== black-nightshade
name: Black nightshade
latin: Solanum nigrum
group: plant
kind: herb
status: poisonous
season: Flowers Jul–Oct; berries Aug–Nov
habitat: Gardens, arable fields, waste ground
summary: Low weedy plant with small white star flowers with yellow centres and clusters of small, dull black berries. Treat it as poisonous.
lookalikes: deadly-nightshade, bittersweet
tags: solanum, berries, black berries, weed
wiki: Solanum nigrum
---
## Identification
- 10–70 cm, branching, often blackish stems; oval, wavy or bluntly toothed leaves.
- Small white star-shaped flowers with a **yellow cone of anthers** (like potato flowers).
- Clusters of small (6–8 mm) green berries ripening to dull black.

## Cautions
- Unripe berries and the leaves contain solanine (causing vomiting, diarrhoea and drowsiness). Varieties and growing conditions differ, so treat all of it as poisonous.

=== bittersweet
name: Bittersweet
latin: Solanum dulcamara
aka: woody nightshade
group: plant
kind: climber
status: poisonous
season: Flowers May–Sep; berries Jul–Oct
habitat: Hedgerows, scrub, woodland edges, riverbanks, reedbeds
summary: Scrambling climber with purple star flowers with a yellow beak, and oval berries turning green to orange to bright red. Poisonous.
lookalikes: rowan, black-nightshade, deadly-nightshade
tags: woody nightshade, red berries, solanine
wiki: Solanum dulcamara
---
## Identification
- Scrambling, slightly woody stems over hedges and reeds.
- Leaves oval, often with one or two small lobes or "ears" at the base.
- Flowers: five swept-back **purple petals around a pointed yellow cone** (like a tiny potato flower).
- Drooping clusters of oval, glossy berries turning green → yellow/orange → **bright red**, often all colours at once.

## Cautions
- Contains solanine-type alkaloids: vomiting, diarrhoea, drowsiness; dangerous to children, though rarely fatal.

=== lords-and-ladies
name: Lords-and-ladies
latin: Arum maculatum
aka: cuckoo pint, wild arum, jack-in-the-pulpit (UK)
group: plant
kind: herb
status: poisonous
season: Leaves Jan–May; flower hood Apr–May; red berries Jul–Sep
habitat: Shady woodland, hedgerow bases and banks, often among wild garlic and bluebells
summary: Arrow-shaped, glossy, often dark-spotted leaves in woodland, then a hooded flower and a spike of bright orange-red berries. Very painful poison.
lookalikes: wild-garlic, common-sorrel
tags: arum, cuckoo pint, red berries, calcium oxalate, wild garlic lookalike
wiki: Arum maculatum
---
!danger Poisonous: grows among wild garlic
Every part contains needle-like calcium oxalate crystals and other irritants. They cause immediate intense burning and swelling of the mouth and throat, which can obstruct breathing. Children are attracted by the berries. Young leaves are easily picked up with handfuls of [[s:wild-garlic]].
!end

## Identification
- Leaves **arrow-shaped with backward-pointing lobes**, glossy, sometimes with **dark purple-black spots**, and a **net of branching veins** (wild garlic leaves have parallel veins and no lobes).
- Leaf stalks wrap around each other at the base; no garlic smell.
- Pale green hood (spathe) around a purple-brown club-shaped spadix in spring.
- Late summer: a spike of shiny berries turning green → **orange-red**, standing alone after the leaves have died back.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:wild-garlic]]: flat, elliptical leaf on its own stalk, parallel veins, garlic smell. See [[a:compare-wild-garlic]].
- [[s:common-sorrel]] leaves are also arrow-shaped but are thin, matt, sour-tasting and grow in grassland.

=== lily-of-the-valley
name: Lily of the valley
latin: Convallaria majalis
group: plant
kind: herb
status: deadly
season: Leaves Apr–Jun; flowers May–Jun; red berries Aug–Sep
habitat: Dry woods on limestone (local and scattered in the wild); garden escapes near habitation
summary: Paired, broad, elliptical leaves with tiny scented white bell flowers on one side of a stalk. A garlic lookalike that can stop the heart.
lookalikes: wild-garlic
tags: cardiac glycosides, bells, wild garlic lookalike
wiki: Convallaria majalis
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
Contains cardiac glycosides (like [[s:foxglove]]): vomiting, slow or irregular heartbeat, collapse. Call **999** for any ingestion.
!end

## Identification
- Usually **two (sometimes three) broad elliptical leaves wrapped around each other from one sheathing stem**. Wild garlic leaves each come up separately on their own stalk.
- Leaves are firmer, slightly paler underneath, with **no garlic smell**.
- Small, white, nodding **bell-shaped** flowers, strongly sweet-scented, all on **one side** of a separate leafless stalk.
- Red berries in late summer.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:wild-garlic]]: see [[a:compare-wild-garlic]].

=== yew
name: Yew
latin: Taxus baccata
group: plant
kind: tree
status: deadly
season: Evergreen; red "berries" (arils) Sep–Nov
habitat: Churchyards, chalk downland woods, gardens, old woodland
summary: Dark evergreen with flat needles in two rows and red cup-shaped berries. Everything except the red flesh is deadly, including the seed inside it.
lookalikes: scots-pine, norway-spruce
tags: conifer, taxine, red berries, churchyard, bow wood
wiki: Taxus baccata
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
Leaves, bark, wood, sawdust and the seed contain taxine alkaloids. These can stop the heart, sometimes with no warning symptoms. A handful of needles can kill an adult. Never use yew needles for "pine needle tea", or yew twigs as skewers or spoons. Call **999** for any ingestion.
!end

## Identification
- Dark green, flat, soft needles 1–3 cm, pale green below, **arranged in two flat rows** along the twig. Pine needles are in pairs; spruce needles grow all around the twig.
- Reddish-brown, flaky, purplish bark; often a huge, gnarled trunk.
- Female trees carry single red, fleshy, **cup-shaped** "berries" (arils) open at the end, showing a dark seed.

- Climbs with tiny hairy rootlets. Leaves glossy, dark, with 3–5 lobes on climbing stems, but oval and unlobed on flowering stems.

=== ragwort

group: plant
kind: herb
status: poisonous
season: Flowers Jun–Nov
habitat: Pasture, verges, sand dunes, waste ground
summary: Yellow daisy-flowered plant in flat-topped clusters with ragged, deeply cut leaves. Toxic to the liver; do not eat or handle with bare hands for long.

## Identification
- 30–100 cm, with ragged, deeply lobed, dark green leaves (unpleasant smell).

status: deadly

=== cherry-laurel

- Big (10–20 cm), very glossy, leathery, bright green leaves, with a few small glands near the base underneath.

- Leaves with **three toothed lobes**, like a maple or currant leaf, in opposite pairs.
- Flat "lacecap" flower heads: big white sterile flowers around small fertile ones.

- Creeping and rooting stems like watercress.

## Identification
- Woody stems twisting **clockwise** around trees (sometimes deeply grooving them).
- Oval leaves in opposite pairs.
- Clusters of long, tubular, cream-yellow flowers flushed pink, strongly scented at dusk.
- Clusters of glossy red berries in autumn.

## Uses
- The old stems shed **papery, stringy bark** in strips: shred it between your palms into a fine nest for catching an ember. See [[a:natural-tinders]].
- Flowers can be sucked for nectar.

## Cautions
- The berries cause vomiting and diarrhoea if eaten in quantity.

## Identification
- Long, rope-like woody stems hanging from hedges and trees, with **shaggy, stringy bark** peeling in strips.
- Leaves of 3–5 toothed leaflets that twine around supports.
- Greenish-white flowers, then masses of feathery, silvery seed heads ("old man's beard") through the winter.

## Uses
- **Tinder:** strip the dead outer bark from old stems and rub it between your palms into a fluffy nest; it catches an ember readily. The fluffy seed heads flare quickly too. See [[a:natural-tinders]].
- Long thin stems can be used for rough binding.

## Cautions
- All parts contain irritant protoanemonin: don't eat it, and the sap can blister skin.

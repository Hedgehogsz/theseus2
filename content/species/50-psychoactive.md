# Psychoactive (mind-altering) UK plants and fungi. Included for identification, safety, the law and first aid
# only: never add dosing, preparation, "how to use" or effects-seeking information.

=== liberty-cap
name: Liberty cap
latin: Psilocybe semilanceata
aka: magic mushroom, psilocybin mushroom
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: psychoactive, poisonous
season: Aug–Dec, mainly Sep–Nov
habitat: Grazed or fertilised grassland, especially wet sheep and cattle pasture, also lawns and playing fields; in grass, never on wood or directly on dung
summary: Small, slender grassland mushroom with a pointed, nipple-topped brown cap. A Class A drug under UK law, and it grows among deadly little brown mushrooms.
lookalikes: funeral-bell, pholiotina-rugosa, deadly-fibrecap, banded-mottlegill
tags: psilocybin, psilocin, magic mushroom, shrooms, little brown mushroom, lbm, class a, hallucinogen, grassland
wiki: Psilocybe semilanceata
---
!danger Deadly lookalikes grow in the same grass
Several **little brown mushrooms** in UK grassland, lawns and woodchip contain **amatoxins** (the death cap poison): [[s:pholiotina-rugosa]] and small Galerina species (relatives of the [[s:funeral-bell]]). Brown **fibrecaps** ([[s:deadly-fibrecap]] and others) contain muscarine. Experienced mycologists use microscopes to separate these. A field guide and a photo are not enough. See [[a:compare-liberty-cap]].
!end

## Identification
- Cap 0.5–2.5 cm across, **conical to bell-shaped**, usually with a **sharp nipple** (papilla) on top; the cap does not open flat.
- Colour changes with moisture: **chestnut to olive-brown when wet**, drying to **pale cream or ochre**. Fine lines show at the edge when wet.
- Cap skin slightly sticky when wet; peels as a thin rubbery film.
- Gills crowded, attached to the stem: cream at first, then grey-brown, then **purple-black**, with a paler edge.
- Stem 4–12 cm long but only 1–3 mm thick, pale, wavy, tough and bendy. **No ring.**
- The stem base may bruise **bluish**. This is **not** a safe test: other fungi stain blue, and the deadly ones may be growing alongside.
- Spore print **dark purple-brown** to purple-black.
- Grows singly or scattered in grass, often in the same field year after year.

## Lookalikes
- **DEADLY:** [[s:pholiotina-rugosa]] (deadly conecap): small, brown, conical, in lawns and woodchip. Has a **ring** on the stem and a **rusty-brown** spore print. Contains amatoxins.
- **DEADLY:** small **Galerina** species grow in moss in grassland and lawns; the [[s:funeral-bell]] grows on wood. Rusty-brown spores, often a ring or ring zone. Amatoxins.
- **DEADLY:** **fibrecaps** (Inocybe), such as the [[s:deadly-fibrecap]]: conical, radially fibrous, often splitting caps, brown spores, in grass near trees. Muscarine.
- [[s:banded-mottlegill]] and other mottlegills (Panaeolus): black spores and mottled gills, often on manured ground.
- Dozens of other little brown mushrooms (LBMs) in grass look much the same. Many have never been tested for toxins.

## Law
!law Class A drug
- Mushrooms containing psilocin or psilocybin, including liberty caps, are **Class A** drugs under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, **fresh or dried**. The Drugs Act 2005 made this explicit for fresh mushrooms. This applies across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.
- Picking them to keep, possessing, giving away or selling them are offences. Maximum penalties: **up to 7 years** in prison for possession; **up to life** for supply or production.
- There are only narrow exemptions, for example where they simply grow wild on land, or are picked only to destroy them or hand them to the police.
!end

## Dangers
- **Misidentification**: the main killer. The amatoxin species give no symptoms for 6–24 hours and then destroy the liver.
- **Impairment outdoors kills.** Confusion, panic and poor judgement lead to falls from heights, getting lost, walking into water, drowning, and not noticing cold until hypothermia sets in. You cannot look after yourself or anyone else.
- Panic, paranoia and frightening confusion can last several hours. Mixed with alcohol or other drugs the risks rise.
- Can trigger lasting mental health problems in some people.
- Children who eat them can become seriously ill (high temperature, fits).

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** usually start **20–60 minutes** after eating and last about **4–8 hours**: large pupils, nausea and vomiting, fast heartbeat, confusion, disorientation, seeing or hearing things, anxiety or panic, poor coordination.

!danger Think "deadly lookalike" if
- Symptoms start **6 hours or more** after eating, or there is **severe vomiting and watery diarrhoea**: possible amatoxins. They need hospital **urgently, even if they then seem to recover**.
- Within 15 minutes–2 hours they have **sweating, drooling, tears, a slow pulse** or breathing trouble: possible muscarine (fibrecaps).
!end

**First aid:**
- **Don't** make them vomit. Give sips of water only.
- **Keep a sample** (whole mushrooms, leftovers, or vomit) in paper or cloth, and photograph it. Note **what, how much and when**.
- Keep them **safe**: away from water, edges, roads and fire. Stay with them; don't let them wander off.
- Keep them **warm**: a confused person won't notice the cold. Use a survival bag and insulation from the ground ([[a:hypothermia]]).
- Speak calmly and simply; reduce noise and bright light. Don't restrain them unless they are in danger.
- If drowsy or unresponsive but breathing: [[a:recovery-position]]; check breathing continually. Not breathing: CPR ([[t:cpr]]). Fits: [[a:seizures]].
- **Get them to medical help** as soon as it is safe to move, or send someone. When you reach a phone or a person, ask for an ambulance and say it may be a mushroom poisoning. See [[a:poisoning]] and [[f:injured]].

=== wavy-cap
name: Wavy cap
latin: Psilocybe cyanescens
aka: magic mushroom, psilocybin mushroom
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: psychoactive, poisonous
season: Sep–Dec, mainly Oct–Nov
habitat: Woodchip and bark mulch in parks, flower beds, landscaped car parks and gardens; also rich woody debris. Introduced; mainly in towns
summary: Brown mushroom with a strongly wavy cap edge, growing in clusters on woodchip mulch, bruising blue. A Class A drug, and deadly Galerina grows on wood too.
lookalikes: funeral-bell, pholiotina-rugosa, sulphur-tuft
tags: psilocybin, psilocin, magic mushroom, woodchip, mulch, bark, little brown mushroom, class a, hallucinogen
wiki: Psilocybe cyanescens
---
!danger The funeral bell also grows on wood
The deadly [[s:funeral-bell]] (Galerina) grows in groups on rotting wood and woodchip and is the same size and colour. It contains **amatoxins** (the death cap poison). The deadly [[s:pholiotina-rugosa]] also grows in woodchip. See [[a:compare-liberty-cap]].
!end

## Identification
- Cap 1.5–5 cm, convex then flattening, with a **wavy, undulating edge** when mature.
- Colour **caramel to chestnut-brown when wet**, drying pale buff or cream.
- Gills attached, cream-brown then dark purple-brown.
- Stem 2–8 cm, white, fibrous; a faint cobweb-like veil when young, leaving at most a faint ring zone.
- Bruises **blue** where handled. Not a safe test: deadly species may grow in the same mulch.
- Spore print **dark purple-brown**.
- Grows in groups or clusters on woody mulch, often in large numbers in late autumn.

## Lookalikes
- **DEADLY:** [[s:funeral-bell]]: honey-brown, often with a thin ring or brown band on the stem, **rusty-brown** spore print. On wood.
- **DEADLY:** [[s:pholiotina-rugosa]]: small, conical, **membranous ring**, rusty-brown spores; woodchip and gardens.
- [[s:sulphur-tuft]]: clusters on wood, **sulphur-yellow** cap and greenish-yellow gills, very bitter. Poisonous.
- Many other brown mushrooms grow on woodchip. Treat every one as dangerous.

## Law
!law Class A drug
- As for the [[s:liberty-cap]]: any fungus containing psilocin is **Class A** under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, fresh or dried (Drugs Act 2005). Up to 7 years in prison for possession, up to life for supply.
!end

## Dangers
- **Misidentification** with the funeral bell: symptoms of amatoxin poisoning start 6–24 hours later and are often fatal without hospital care.
- **Impairment** outdoors: falls, wandering, drowning, hypothermia and bad decisions. Often stronger and less predictable than people expect.
- Panic, confusion and lasting mental health problems in some people. Dangerous for children and dogs that find it in parks and gardens.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** usually within **20–60 minutes**, lasting **4–8 hours**: large pupils, nausea, vomiting, fast heartbeat, confusion, hallucinations, panic, poor coordination.

!danger Suspect the funeral bell if
Symptoms start **6 hours or more** after eating, or there is **severe vomiting and watery diarrhoea**. They need hospital **urgently, even if they seem to recover**.
!end

**First aid:**
- **Don't** make them vomit. Sips of water only.
- **Keep a sample** and a photo; note what, how much and when.
- Keep them **safe** (away from water, roads, heights and fire), **warm** and **watched**.
- Drowsy but breathing: [[a:recovery-position]]. Not breathing: CPR ([[t:cpr]]).
- **Get them to medical help.** When you reach a phone or a person, ask for an ambulance and mention mushrooms. See [[a:poisoning]].

=== banded-mottlegill
name: Banded mottlegill
latin: Panaeolus cinctulus
aka: Panaeolus subbalteatus, weed Panaeolus
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: psychoactive, poisonous
season: May–Nov
habitat: Manured or composted ground: dung heaps, stable bedding, compost, well-manured fields and gardens
summary: Brown mushroom on manured ground whose cap dries paler from the centre, leaving a dark band at the edge. Contains psilocybin (Class A).
lookalikes: pholiotina-rugosa, funeral-bell, liberty-cap
tags: panaeolus, mottlegill, psilocybin, magic mushroom, dung, manure, compost, little brown mushroom, class a
wiki: Panaeolus cinctulus
---
!danger Little brown mushroom: deadly lookalikes
Gardens, compost and manured ground also grow the deadly [[s:pholiotina-rugosa]] (amatoxins) and many other small brown mushrooms. Don't try to tell them apart for eating. See [[a:compare-liberty-cap]].
!end

## Identification
- Cap 2–5 cm, bell-shaped then broadly convex, often with a low central bump.
- **Reddish-brown to cinnamon when wet**, drying tan from the centre outwards, leaving a **dark band near the edge** (the "band").
- Gills attached, crowded, **mottled** grey and black as the spores ripen unevenly.
- Stem 4–10 cm, slender, reddish-brown, paler at the top, with no ring.
- Spore print **black**.
- Grows in groups on rich, manured or composted ground.

## Lookalikes
- **DEADLY:** [[s:pholiotina-rugosa]]: smaller, rusty-brown spore print and a **ring** on the stem.
- **DEADLY:** Galerina species such as the [[s:funeral-bell]]: rusty-brown spores; on wood or in moss.
- The very common brown mottlegill (mower's mushroom) of lawns, and many other little brown mushrooms.

## Law
!law Class A drug
- Contains psilocybin, so it is **Class A** under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, fresh or dried (Drugs Act 2005). Up to 7 years in prison for possession, up to life for supply. See [[s:liberty-cap]].
!end

## Dangers
- Misidentification with deadly little brown mushrooms.
- Impairment outdoors (falls, drowning, hypothermia, getting lost), panic and confusion.
- Dung and compost carry bacteria: stomach upsets are common even without toxins.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** usually within **20–60 minutes**, lasting several hours: large pupils, nausea, vomiting, confusion, hallucinations, panic, fast heartbeat.

Symptoms starting **6 hours or more** later, with severe vomiting and watery diarrhoea, suggest a deadly amatoxin mushroom: get them to hospital **urgently** even if they seem to recover.

**First aid:**
- **Don't** make them vomit. Sips of water only.
- **Keep a sample** and a photo; note what, how much and when.
- Keep them **safe, warm and watched**; [[a:recovery-position]] if drowsy.
- **Get them to medical help.** See [[a:poisoning]].

=== spectacular-rustgill
name: Spectacular rustgill
latin: Gymnopilus junonius
aka: laughing gym, Gymnopilus spectabilis
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: psychoactive, poisonous
season: Aug–Dec, mainly Sep–Nov
habitat: Large clumps at the base of broadleaf trees (often oak) and on stumps and buried roots; sometimes conifers
summary: Big golden-orange mushroom in clumps at tree bases, with a ring and rusty-orange spores. Intensely bitter and poisonous.
lookalikes: jack-o-lantern, honey-fungus, sulphur-tuft
tags: gymnopilus, rustgill, laughing gym, orange mushroom, clusters, stump, bitter
wiki: Gymnopilus junonius
---
## Identification
- Cap 5–15 cm (sometimes more), **golden-yellow to orange-tawny**, convex then flattening, dry, finely fibrous or scaly.
- Gills crowded, yellow at first, then **rusty-brown**.
- Stem thick (1–3 cm), fibrous, often swollen in the middle, with a **ring** that turns rusty from fallen spores.
- Spore print **rusty orange-brown**.
- Flesh pale yellow; taste **intensely bitter**.
- Grows in **dense clumps** from wood or buried roots, often at the foot of living or dead oaks.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:jack-o-lantern]]: bright orange clusters on wood, gills running down the stem, **orange flesh**, no ring, pale spores. Causes severe vomiting.
- [[s:honey-fungus]]: smaller, with dark hair-like scales on the cap and **white** spores. Raw honey fungus is poisonous.
- [[s:sulphur-tuft]]: smaller, sulphur-yellow, greenish gills, purple-brown spores. Poisonous.

## Law
!law Uncertain content
- Any fungus that contains psilocin is Class A under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971. Whether this species contains psilocybin is **debated**: some collections abroad have been reported to, but British and European collections are generally reported to contain little or none.
- Supplying any substance for its mind-altering effect can be an offence under the Psychoactive Substances Act 2016.
!end

## Dangers
- Mainly a **poisonous, bitter** mushroom: stomach upset is the likely result.
- Its effects, if any, are unpredictable and depend on the collection.
- Easily confused with other orange clustering species, some of which cause severe vomiting.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** nausea, vomiting, stomach pain and diarrhoea, usually within a few hours. Dizziness, confusion or seeing things are possible.

**First aid:**
- **Don't** make them vomit. Give small sips of water to replace fluid ([[a:diarrhoea-ors]]).
- **Keep a sample** and a photo; note what, how much and when.
- Keep them warm and watched; [[a:recovery-position]] if drowsy.
- If symptoms start **6 hours or more** after eating, suspect a different, deadly mushroom: get them to hospital **urgently**. Otherwise get them to medical help when you can. See [[a:poisoning]].

=== pholiotina-rugosa
name: Deadly conecap
latin: Pholiotina rugosa
aka: Conocybe rugosa, Conocybe filaris, ringed conecap, fool's conecap
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: deadly
season: Jun–Nov
habitat: Woodchip and bark mulch, flower beds, lawns, gardens, compost and disturbed rich soil; also grassland and woodland edges
summary: Tiny, fragile orange-brown mushroom with a membranous ring, common in garden mulch and lawns. Contains amatoxins, the death cap poison.
lookalikes: liberty-cap, wavy-cap, banded-mottlegill, funeral-bell
tags: pholiotina, conocybe, conecap, amatoxin, little brown mushroom, lbm, woodchip, lawn, deadly
wiki: Pholiotina rugosa
---
!danger Deadly
Contains **amatoxins**, the same poison as the [[s:death-cap]]. Symptoms are **delayed 6–24 hours**, then liver failure. It grows where people look for magic mushrooms and is easily mistaken for them. See [[a:compare-liberty-cap]].
!end

## Identification
- Cap 0.5–2.5 cm, **conical to bell-shaped**, then convex; **orange-brown to rusty tawny**, drying paler; fine lines at the edge when wet.
- Gills attached, pale ochre then **rusty brown**.
- Stem 2–6 cm, thin, fragile, pale brownish, with a **distinct membranous ring** about half-way up (it can slide or fall off).
- Spore print **rusty brown**.
- Grows singly or in small groups.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:liberty-cap]]: no ring, sharp nipple on the cap, purple-black spores; in pasture.
- [[s:wavy-cap]]: larger, wavy edge, purple-brown spores, bruises blue; woodchip.
- [[s:banded-mottlegill]]: no ring, black spores, mottled gills.
- [[s:funeral-bell]]: also deadly; on wood, larger.

## Dangers
- A few small caps can cause fatal liver failure. Cooking, drying or freezing don't destroy amatoxins.
- **Children and dogs** eat small mushrooms from lawns and flower beds.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** none for **6–24 hours**, then violent vomiting, stomach pain and watery diarrhoea. They may seem to recover after a day while the liver fails over the next 2–5 days.

**First aid:**
- They need hospital **immediately, even if they feel well**. Treatment works best early.
- **Don't** make them vomit. Sips of water only.
- **Keep a sample** and a photo; note what, how much and when.
- Keep them warm; replace fluid with small frequent sips if they are vomiting ([[a:diarrhoea-ors]]).
- **Get them to medical help** or send someone now. See [[a:poisoning]].

=== thorn-apple
name: Thorn-apple
latin: Datura stramonium
aka: jimsonweed, devil's trumpet, datura
group: plant
kind: herb
status: deadly, psychoactive
season: Flowers Jul–Oct; spiny seed pods Aug–Nov
habitat: Casual on disturbed ground: arable fields, waste ground, gardens, tips and under bird feeders (from birdseed); commonest in hot summers in southern and eastern England
summary: Rank-smelling weed with large white trumpet flowers pointing upwards and spiny, walnut-sized seed pods. Every part is deadly poisonous.
lookalikes: angels-trumpet, henbane
tags: datura, jimsonweed, stramonium, trumpet flower, spiny pod, tropane, atropine, scopolamine, hyoscine, anticholinergic
wiki: Datura stramonium
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
Contains **atropine, hyoscyamine and scopolamine** (tropane alkaloids). Every part is toxic, especially the **seeds**. The amount in a plant varies hugely, so there is no "safe" amount. Seeds have contaminated food crops. Poisoning causes days of delirium and can kill.
!end

## Identification
- Annual, 30 cm–1.5 m, stout, branching, hairless stems, often purplish.
- Leaves large (up to 20 cm), oval, with **coarse, irregular, pointed teeth**; an **unpleasant smell** when crushed.
- Flowers **white** (sometimes purple-tinged), **trumpet-shaped**, 5–10 cm long, held **upright**, opening in the evening.
- Seed pod an egg-shaped capsule 3–5 cm, **covered in sharp spines**, splitting into four to release many **black, kidney-shaped seeds**.
- Appears suddenly after soil disturbance and may not return the next year.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:angels-trumpet]]: a woody garden shrub whose much larger trumpets **hang down**; pods smooth. Equally deadly.
- [[s:henbane]]: sticky-hairy, with dull yellow, purple-veined flowers.
- Young plants can be mistaken for leafy vegetables or weeds among crops.

## Law
- Not a controlled drug under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971; it is legal to grow. Supplying it for its mind-altering effect is an offence under the **Psychoactive Substances Act 2016**.
- Uprooting any wild plant without the landowner's permission is illegal (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981).

## Dangers
- **Anticholinergic poisoning**: remember "**hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter**".
- Delirium lasts **a day to several days**. People become completely confused, don't recognise others, talk to people who aren't there, wander off and injure themselves. Outdoors this leads to falls, drowning and exposure.
- Fits, very high temperature, dangerous heart rhythms and coma in serious cases.
- Children are attracted to the pods and seeds.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** start **30 minutes–2 hours** after eating (later with seeds) and can last **24–48 hours or longer**:
- **Hot**: high temperature, no sweating. **Dry**: very dry mouth and skin, thirst, can't pass urine.
- **Red**: flushed face and skin. **Blind**: **very large pupils**, blurred vision, dazzled by light.
- **Mad**: agitation, confusion, hallucinations, picking at the air or clothes, incoherent speech.
- Fast heartbeat, poor coordination; fits and coma in severe cases.

**First aid:**
- **Don't** make them vomit. Don't give alcohol or medicines.
- **Keep a sample** (leaves, flowers, pods or seeds) and a photo; note what, how much and when.
- **Keep them safe**: stay with them constantly, away from water, edges, roads and fire. They cannot judge danger.
- If they are **overheating**, loosen clothing, move to shade and cool gently with damp cloths; once their temperature falls, guard against chilling ([[a:heat-illness]], [[a:hypothermia]]).
- Drowsy or unresponsive: [[a:recovery-position]], check breathing. Fits: [[a:seizures]]. Not breathing: CPR ([[t:cpr]]).
- This needs **hospital treatment**. Get them to medical help, or send someone now. See [[a:poisoning]].

=== henbane
name: Henbane
latin: Hyoscyamus niger
aka: stinking nightshade, black henbane
group: plant
kind: herb
status: deadly, psychoactive
season: Flowers Jun–Aug; seed capsules Jul–Oct
habitat: Disturbed sandy or chalky ground: coastal sand and shingle, farmyards, field edges, old rabbit warrens, waste ground; scarce and scattered, mainly in England
summary: Sticky-hairy, foul-smelling plant with dull creamy-yellow flowers netted with purple veins. Contains the same deadly alkaloids as deadly nightshade.
lookalikes: thorn-apple, deadly-nightshade
tags: hyoscyamus, stinking nightshade, tropane, hyoscine, scopolamine, hyoscyamine, anticholinergic
wiki: Hyoscyamus niger
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
All parts contain **hyoscyamine and scopolamine**, especially the seeds and roots. Roots have been mistaken for parsnips and leaves for edible greens. Get emergency help: [[a:poisoning]].
!end

## Identification
- Annual or biennial, 30–80 cm, covered in **sticky glandular hairs**; the whole plant **smells foul**.
- Leaves greyish-green, soft, oval with **large, irregular teeth or lobes**; upper leaves clasp the stem.
- Flowers 2–3 cm, funnel-shaped, **dull creamy-yellow with a dense network of purple veins** and a **dark purple centre**, in rows along one side of the stem.
- Seed capsules enclosed in a **tough, urn-shaped calyx with five stiff, spiny teeth**, in two rows along the stem; they open by a lid and hold many small grey-brown seeds.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:thorn-apple]]: white trumpets and spiny pods; same poisons.
- [[s:deadly-nightshade]]: purple-brown bells and shiny black berries; same poisons.
- First-year rosettes can be confused with other leafy weeds; the smell and sticky hairs are clues.

## Law
- Not a controlled drug under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971. Supplying it for its mind-altering effect is an offence under the **Psychoactive Substances Act 2016**.
- Uprooting any wild plant without the landowner's permission is illegal (Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981).

## Dangers
- Anticholinergic poisoning ("hot, dry, red, blind and mad"), as for [[s:thorn-apple]]: delirium, dangerous behaviour, high temperature, fits and coma.
- Outdoors, a delirious person cannot protect themselves from water, heights, cold or heat.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** start **15 minutes–2 hours** after eating, lasting a day or more: very dry mouth, flushed hot dry skin, **large pupils**, blurred vision, fast heartbeat, confusion, agitation, hallucinations; fits and coma in severe cases.

**First aid:**
- **Don't** make them vomit.
- **Keep a sample** and a photo; note what, how much and when.
- **Stay with them**, keep them safe from water, edges and fire; cool them gently if overheating.
- Drowsy: [[a:recovery-position]]. Fits: [[a:seizures]]. Not breathing: CPR ([[t:cpr]]).
- They need **hospital**. Get them to medical help, or send someone now. See [[a:poisoning]].

=== angels-trumpet
name: Angel's trumpet
latin: Brugmansia
aka: brugmansia, datura (wrongly)
group: plant
kind: shrub
status: deadly, psychoactive
season: Flowers Jun–Oct outdoors; much of the year under glass
habitat: Gardens, patios, pots and conservatories; not frost-hardy, so never wild in the UK. Often stood outside in summer
summary: Garden shrub with huge, hanging, scented trumpet flowers in white, yellow, pink or peach. Every part is deadly poisonous.
lookalikes: thorn-apple
tags: brugmansia, datura, trumpet flower, garden plant, tropane, scopolamine, hyoscine, anticholinergic, pupil
wiki: Brugmansia
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
All parts, including the flowers and nectar, contain **scopolamine and atropine**. The amount varies greatly from plant to plant. Poisoning causes long-lasting delirium and can kill.
!end

## Identification
- Shrub or small tree, usually 1–3 m in a pot or bed, with soft, woody stems.
- Large, soft, oval leaves, 10–30 cm, often slightly hairy.
- Flowers **15–50 cm long trumpets that hang downwards**, white, yellow, pink, orange or peach, often strongly scented in the evening.
- Fruits smooth, green, elongated (not spiny).

## Lookalikes
- [[s:thorn-apple]] (Datura): a smaller annual weed with **upright** trumpets and **spiny** pods. Same poisons.

## Law
- Not a controlled drug under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971; it is sold legally as an ornamental. Supplying it for its mind-altering effect is an offence under the **Psychoactive Substances Act 2016**.

## Dangers
- Anticholinergic poisoning ("hot, dry, red, blind and mad"), as for [[s:thorn-apple]]: delirium that can last days, high temperature, fast heart, fits, coma.
- **Sap or pollen in the eye** can make **one pupil very large** for a day or more. This can be confused with a head injury or stroke: ask whether they have handled the plant. Wash hands after gardening.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** start **30 minutes–3 hours** after eating and can last **1–3 days**: very dry mouth, hot flushed dry skin, **large pupils**, blurred vision, fast heartbeat, confusion, agitation, hallucinations, inability to pass urine; fits and coma in severe cases.

**First aid:**
- **Don't** make them vomit.
- **Keep a sample** (flower or leaf) and a photo; note what, how much and when.
- **Stay with them** and keep them safe from water, heights, traffic and fire. Cool gently if overheating.
- Drowsy: [[a:recovery-position]]. Fits: [[a:seizures]]. Not breathing: CPR ([[t:cpr]]).
- They need **hospital**. Get them to medical help, or send someone now. See [[a:poisoning]].
- **Eye contact only:** rinse the eye with clean water for 10 minutes. The large pupil settles on its own, but if they have a headache, drowsiness, weakness or confusion, treat it as a possible head injury or stroke ([[a:head-injury]], [[a:heart-attack-stroke]]).

## Identification
- Bushy perennial, 40 cm–1 m, woody at the base.
- Leaves **silvery-grey and silky-hairy on both sides**, deeply divided two or three times into blunt segments.
- **Strong aromatic smell** and an **intensely bitter** taste.
- Flowers tiny, globe-shaped, **dull yellow, drooping heads** (3–5 mm) in loose leafy spikes.

## Lookalikes
- **Mugwort** (Artemisia vulgaris), a very common roadside weed: leaves **dark green above and white-woolly beneath**, reddish stems, smaller reddish-brown flower heads. It also contains thujone, in smaller amounts.
- Garden "silver" Artemisias and other grey-leaved herbs.

## Law
- Not a controlled drug. Wormwood is used in small amounts as a bitter flavouring, and food law limits the thujone allowed in food and drinks. Supplying any substance for its mind-altering effect (other than as food) can be an offence under the **Psychoactive Substances Act 2016**.

## Dangers
- **Thujone** is a convulsant: large amounts of the plant, and especially **wormwood oil** (essential oil), can cause **fits**, muscle breakdown and kidney damage.
- Its old reputation for causing hallucinations (as "absinthe madness") was mostly due to very strong alcohol, but the fits are real.
- Avoid in pregnancy and in anyone with epilepsy.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** within **30 minutes–a few hours**: nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, restlessness, dizziness, confusion, tremor and **fits**. Dark urine later can mean muscle or kidney damage.

**First aid:**
- **Don't** make them vomit (especially after swallowing oil: it can get into the lungs).
- **Keep a sample** or the bottle; note what, how much and when.
- Fits: protect the head, don't restrain, [[a:seizures]]. Afterwards: [[a:recovery-position]].
- Keep them warm and watched. Get them to medical help, especially after essential oil or any fit. See [[a:poisoning]].

## Identification
- Whole nutmeg: a hard, oval, brown seed, 2–3 cm long, with a **wrinkled network** on the surface and a warm, sweet smell.
- Ground nutmeg: pale brown powder in a spice jar.
- **Mace** is the dried red-orange (fading to orange-brown) lacy coat around the seed, with similar chemicals.

## Lookalikes
- Not a wild plant. Ground nutmeg can be confused with other brown spices (cinnamon, mixed spice); the danger is the quantity eaten, not confusion.

## Law
- Not a controlled drug. Food is exempt from the **Psychoactive Substances Act 2016**. It is a normal food ingredient.

## Dangers
- Normal cooking amounts are safe. Poisoning happens when someone eats **far more than any recipe uses**: a child eating from the jar, or someone taking it deliberately.
- Contains **myristicin and elemicin**. Poisoning is unpleasant and can be frightening; serious harm is rare but possible, especially for children or people with heart problems.
- Symptoms are **delayed by several hours**, so someone may think they are fine.
- In a survival situation, a day or two of confusion, vomiting and palpitations is dangerous in itself: you can't navigate, keep warm or look after anyone.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** usually start **3–8 hours** after eating and last **24–48 hours** (sometimes longer): nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, dizziness, **fast heartbeat** and palpitations, flushing, dry mouth, anxiety, a sense of doom, agitation, confusion, drowsiness, sometimes hallucinations.

**First aid:**
- **Don't** make them vomit. Sips of water only.
- Keep the **jar or packet**; note how much and when.
- Keep them calm, warm and watched. Drowsy: [[a:recovery-position]].
- Get them to medical help, especially a **child**, anyone with chest pain, a very fast or irregular pulse, or confusion. See [[a:poisoning]].

=== opium-poppy
name: Opium poppy
latin: Papaver somniferum
aka: breadseed poppy, garden poppy
group: plant
kind: herb
status: poisonous, psychoactive
season: Flowers Jun–Aug; seed heads Jul–Oct
habitat: Gardens, allotments, waste ground, tips, roadsides and field edges (a common garden escape); also licensed farm crops in southern England
summary: Tall blue-grey poppy with large white, pink, mauve or red flowers and a big round seed head. Its sap and seed heads contain opioids such as morphine.
lookalikes: 
tags: papaver, poppy, opium, morphine, codeine, opioid, seed head, garden poppy
wiki: Papaver somniferum
---
!danger Poisonous: opioids
The unripe seed heads, sap and dried plant contain **morphine, codeine and related opioids** in unpredictable amounts. Opioid poisoning stops breathing. Products made from the plant have caused deaths in the UK.
!end

## Identification
- Annual, 30 cm–1.2 m, the **whole plant waxy blue-grey** (glaucous) and mostly hairless.
- Leaves large, oblong, with wavy, toothed edges, **clasping the stem** at their base.
- Flowers 5–10 cm across, four large crumpled petals, **white, pink, lilac or red**, often with a **dark purple blotch** at the base; frilly double forms in gardens.
- Seed head a large, smooth, **round to egg-shaped capsule** (2–5 cm or more) topped by a flat, star-ribbed disc; it holds many tiny grey-blue or white seeds.

## Lookalikes
- **Common (field) poppy** (Papaver rhoeas): smaller, **scarlet** flowers, **bristly-hairy** green stems and leaves, small seed head. Not an opium poppy.
- Other garden poppies have hairy stems or different seed heads.

## Law
- **Opium** and **poppy straw** (the harvested plant, except the seeds) are **Class A** drugs under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971; morphine and codeine are controlled too. Up to 7 years in prison for possession and up to life for supply or production.
- Growing the plant as an ornamental is not itself an offence, and seeds for baking are legal. Commercial crops are grown under Home Office licence: **keep out** of these fields.

## Dangers
- **Opioid poisoning**: drowsiness, slow and shallow breathing, then breathing stops. Children and anyone who has drunk alcohol or taken sedatives are at greatest risk.
- Outdoors, a drowsy person cools very quickly: hypothermia follows.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** within **30 minutes–2 hours** (eaten), lasting many hours:
- **Pinpoint pupils**, very drowsy or impossible to wake.
- **Slow, shallow or noisy breathing**, or long pauses; **blue or grey lips**.
- Nausea, vomiting, itching, confusion, cold clammy skin.

**First aid:**
- Check breathing. **Not breathing normally: start CPR** ([[t:cpr]]).
- Breathing but drowsy: [[a:recovery-position]]; **keep checking breathing** and keep them awake if you can. Symptoms can come back for hours.
- If anyone has **naloxone** (an opioid reversal kit carried by some drug services, hostels and first aiders), use it as its instructions say. It can wear off before the poison does, so they still need hospital.
- **Don't** make them vomit. Keep them warm. Keep a sample or photo.
- Get them to medical help, or send someone now. See [[a:poisoning]].

=== ergot
name: Ergot
latin: Claviceps purpurea
group: fungus
kind: fungus on grasses
status: deadly, psychoactive
season: Jul–Sep, in the ears of ripening grasses and cereals
habitat: Rye, wheat, barley and many wild grasses, including field edges, verges and coastal grasses; commonest after a wet flowering season
summary: Hard, dark purple-black, horn-like growths replacing grains in grass and cereal ears. Poisonous: ergotism caused gangrene, fits and hallucinations in the past.
lookalikes: 
tags: claviceps, ergotism, St Anthony's fire, rye, grain, cereal, grass seed, sclerotia, alkaloids
wiki: Claviceps purpurea
---
!danger Poisonous grain
Contains **ergot alkaloids** that narrow blood vessels and affect the brain. Grain or wild grass seed contaminated with ergot has caused mass poisonings ("St Anthony's fire") with **gangrene, fits, hallucinations and death**. Check any grain or grass seed you gather.
!end

## Identification
- **Hard, dark purple to black, curved "horns"** (sclerotia) **1–5 cm long**, sticking out of a grass or cereal ear **in place of a grain**. Larger than the normal grains around them.
- Inside, the flesh is white to pinkish-purple.
- Early in summer, infected flowers drip a sticky, sweet **"honeydew"** that attracts insects.
- In spring, fallen sclerotia sprout tiny pinkish-purple drumstick-like stalks (about 1 cm).

## Lookalikes
- Normal grains are pale, plump and much smaller. Smut and rust diseases make grains or leaves black or orange **powdery**, not hard horns.

## Law
- Ergot is not a controlled drug, and it is **not** a natural form of LSD: it is a poison. Food law sets strict limits on ergot in cereals sold for food or animal feed. Supplying any substance for its mind-altering effect can be an offence under the **Psychoactive Substances Act 2016**.

## Dangers
- **Gangrenous ergotism**: burning pain, then numb, cold, pale fingers and toes; in time gangrene of the limbs.
- **Convulsive ergotism**: twitching, painful spasms, fits, confusion and hallucinations.
- Dangerous in **pregnancy**: it can cause miscarriage.
- Poisonous to grazing animals too.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** within hours of a large amount: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, stomach pain, headache, dizziness, tingling or numbness, cold pale fingers and toes, chest pain, confusion, fits. Smaller amounts eaten over days or weeks cause gradual burning pain, cold limbs and numbness.

**First aid:**
- **Stop eating the grain or seed.** Keep a sample.
- **Don't** make them vomit.
- Keep them **warm**, but don't rub or put direct heat on cold, numb hands or feet.
- Fits: [[a:seizures]]. Chest pain: [[a:heart-attack-stroke]].
- Get them to medical help: cold, painful, pale or blue limbs are an emergency. See [[a:poisoning]].

## Identification
- Annual, usually 0.5–2 m (taller in crops), with a **strong, resinous, skunk-like smell**.
- Leaves **hand-shaped (palmate)**: usually **5–9 narrow, lance-shaped leaflets with saw-toothed edges**, all spreading from one point on a long stalk.
- Male and female flowers on separate plants: males in loose hanging clusters, females in dense, leafy, hairy clusters at the tips.
- Seeds small, smooth, grey-brown, mottled (common in wild-bird food).

## Lookalikes
- Several garden plants (Japanese maple, lupins, spider flower, some Potentilla) have similar hand-shaped leaves, but not the narrow, saw-edged leaflets and resinous smell together.
- Field crops of industrial hemp look the same: they are grown under Home Office licence.

## Law
!law Class B drug
- Cannabis is **Class B** under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971. Possession: up to 5 years in prison; supply or production: up to 14 years. **Growing it is an offence** (cultivation) without a Home Office licence.
- Specialist doctors can prescribe some cannabis-based medicines; this does not make other cannabis legal.
!end

## Dangers
- **Impairment outdoors**: poor coordination, slow reactions and poor judgement increase the risk of falls, getting lost and drowning, and you notice the cold less while still losing heat.
- Anxiety, panic and paranoia, especially with strong products.
- **Edibles** (sweets, cakes) are a common cause of serious poisoning in **children and dogs**.

## If someone has eaten it
**Symptoms:** when eaten, usually **30 minutes–2 hours** later, lasting **6–12 hours** or more: drowsiness, unsteadiness, slurred speech, red eyes, fast heartbeat, dizziness or fainting on standing, anxiety, panic, vomiting. **Children:** very drowsy, floppy, slow breathing, fits or coma.

**First aid:**
- **Don't** make them vomit.
- Keep them sitting or lying down (fainting risk), **warm** and watched; speak calmly.
- Drowsy: [[a:recovery-position]]; check breathing.
- **Children, or any adult with chest pain, fits or who can't be woken: get them to medical help urgently.** Keep the packet. See [[a:poisoning]].

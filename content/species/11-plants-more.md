# More UK plants: coastal and upland edibles, herbs, invasives and poisonous plants.

=== alexanders
name: Alexanders
latin: Smyrnium olusatrum
aka: horse parsley, black lovage
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible, caution
season: Leaves Oct–Apr (green right through winter); flowers Mar–Jun; black seeds Jun–Sep
harvest: young stems and leaves 2-4; flower buds 3-5; seeds 7-9
habitat: Coastal hedge banks, cliff tops, verges and waste ground near the sea, often around old castles and abbeys; mainly southern and eastern coasts, spreading inland along roads
summary: Glossy, celery-scented umbellifer with leaves in threes and yellow-green flower heads, lush when little else is green. Edible, but it belongs to the family that includes deadly hemlock water-dropwort.
lookalikes: hemlock-water-dropwort, hemlock, wild-parsnip, wild-celery, ground-elder
tags: carrot family, umbellifer, coastal, winter greens, spring, pepper, seeds
wiki: Smyrnium olusatrum
---
!danger Carrot family: be certain
[[s:hemlock-water-dropwort]] also has glossy, celery-scented leaves through winter and spring. Only pick from a patch you have **seen in flower with yellow-green umbels** (they come back in the same place every year), away from water, and check every feature below. Never dig and eat the roots. If in doubt, leave it.
!end

## Identification
- Stout, hairless, clump-forming plant up to 1.5 m; one of the first big green plants of the year.
- Leaves **dark, very glossy green, divided into threes**: the stalk splits into three, and each part into three again. Leaflets broad, rounded-diamond shaped, bluntly toothed.
- Leaf stalks have **broad, papery sheaths** where they clasp the stem; upper leaves smaller and yellowish.
- Stems grooved, solid when young and hollow later, **never purple-spotted**.
- Flowers **yellow-green** (never white), in dense rounded umbels, Mar–Jun.
- Seeds black, ridged, about 7–8 mm, peppery.
- Strong celery- or myrrh-like smell when crushed.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:hemlock-water-dropwort]] (deadly): **white** flowers in Jun–Jul, leaves divided three or four times into wedge-shaped toothed leaflets, grows with its feet in wet ground or water, and has clusters of fat finger-like roots. Before flowering the two can look alike to a beginner.
- [[s:hemlock]] (deadly): finely divided fern-like leaves, purple-blotched smooth stems, mousy smell, white flowers.
- [[s:wild-parsnip]]: also yellow umbels, but in summer (Jun–Sep); leaves once-divided and slightly hairy; its sap burns skin in sunlight.
- [[s:wild-celery]] and [[s:ground-elder]]: white flowers.

## Uses
- Young stems: peel, then steam or boil for a few minutes like asparagus.
- Leaves: cooked as a strong-flavoured green, or a little added to soups and stews.
- Flower buds: raw or lightly cooked.
- Ripe black seeds: crush as a pepper substitute.

## Law
- Picking a few stems is fine. Uprooting any wild plant without the landowner's permission is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981.

=== rock-samphire
name: Rock samphire
latin: Crithmum maritimum
aka: sea fennel
group: plant
kind: herb (succulent)
status: edible, caution
season: Leaves Apr–Oct (best May–Jul, before flowering); flowers Jun–Oct
harvest: leaves 5-7
habitat: Sea cliffs, rocks, sea walls, harbour walls and shingle just above the tide, mainly on the south and west coasts of Britain
summary: Bushy, grey-green coastal plant with fleshy, cylindrical leaflets and a strong resinous smell. Edible cooked or pickled; the cliffs it grows on are the real danger.
lookalikes: samphire, hemlock-water-dropwort
tags: coast, cliff, succulent, pickle, sea fennel, umbellifer
wiki: Crithmum
---
## Identification
- Bushy, hairless perennial 15–45 cm, woody at the base, growing from cracks in rock.
- Leaves **fleshy, grey-green, divided into narrow, cylindrical, pointed segments** 2–5 cm long, like fat pine needles, with sheathing bases.
- **Strong smell when crushed**: resinous, like citrus peel, carrot and turpentine together.
- Small yellow-green flowers in umbels, Jun–Oct, followed by ridged, corky, egg-shaped fruits.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:hemlock-water-dropwort]] (deadly) sometimes grows where streams run down cliffs. Its leaves are **flat, broad and toothed**, never fleshy cylinders, and its flowers are white. If it doesn't have fleshy round leaflets and the strong smell, don't pick it.
- [[s:samphire]] (marsh samphire): grows on saltmarsh mud, not rock, and is a jointed, leafless green stem. Edible.
- Golden samphire: a scarce yellow daisy with flat, fleshy leaves. Leave it alone.

## Uses
- Snip young leaves and shoot tips before flowering. Raw, the taste is very strong: steam or boil for 2–3 minutes, or pickle in vinegar.
- Contains some vitamin C.

## Dangers
- **The cliffs.** Never climb or lean over a cliff edge to reach it: edges crumble and overhang. Pick only what you can reach from safe ground.
- Tides cut off rocky platforms and the bases of cliffs quickly. Check the tide before going out: see [[a:coast-and-tides]].

## Law
- Snip, don't uproot. Many sea cliffs are Sites of Special Scientific Interest, where damaging plants is an offence.

=== sea-purslane
name: Sea purslane
latin: Atriplex portulacoides
aka: Halimione portulacoides
group: plant
kind: shrub (low, saltmarsh)
status: edible
season: Leaves all year (best Apr–Oct)
harvest: leaves 1-12
habitat: Salt marshes, especially fringing creeks and pools covered by high tides; south, east and west coasts of Britain
summary: Low, silvery-grey shrub lining saltmarsh creeks, with small, fleshy, mealy leaves that taste salty and crunchy. Edible raw or cooked, but mind the tide and the mud.
lookalikes: samphire, sea-beet
tags: coast, saltmarsh, salty, greens, estuary
wiki: Atriplex portulacoides
---
## Identification
- Sprawling, much-branched shrub 20–80 cm, woody at the base, often forming dense grey banks along the edges of creeks.
- Leaves **mostly in opposite pairs**, oval to spoon-shaped, 2–5 cm, untoothed, **thick and fleshy** with a **silvery-grey, mealy coating**.
- Tiny yellowish flowers in short spikes, Jul–Oct.
- Leaves crunch when bitten and taste salty.

## Lookalikes
- No dangerous plant looks like it in a saltmarsh.
- [[s:samphire]] (marsh samphire) grows on the open mud nearby: jointed green stems with no leaves. Edible.
- Oraches on the strandline have thinner, often arrow-shaped mealy leaves and are also edible. [[s:sea-beet]] has large, glossy, dark green leaves.

## Uses
- Pick leaves or snip shoot tips. Eat a few raw, or blanch or steam for 1–2 minutes. Don't add salt.
- Salty food makes you need more water: allow for it ([[a:water-needs]]).

## Cautions
- **Saltmarsh mud can trap you**, and creeks fill fast on a rising tide, cutting off the ground behind you. Check tide times, stay near firm ground and don't go out alone: see [[a:coast-and-tides]].
- Wash it well. Don't pick near sewage outfalls, busy harbours or polluted estuaries.

=== scurvy-grass
name: Common scurvygrass
latin: Cochlearia officinalis
aka: spoonwort
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible
season: Leaves all year (best Feb–Jun); flowers Apr–Aug
harvest: leaves 1-12; flowers 4-8
habitat: Salt marshes, sea cliffs, sea walls and coastal rocks; also mountain ledges. The related Danish scurvygrass lines salted main roads and motorways inland.
summary: Low coastal plant with fleshy, glossy, heart- to kidney-shaped leaves and small white four-petalled flowers. Hot and bitter, but rich in vitamin C, which gave it its name.
lookalikes: lesser-celandine, watercress
tags: coast, vitamin c, scurvy, cress, greens, cabbage family
wiki: Cochlearia officinalis
---
## Identification
- Low rosette 5–30 cm.
- Basal leaves on **long stalks**, fleshy, hairless and shiny, **rounded heart- or kidney-shaped**, 1–5 cm. Stem leaves smaller, toothed and clasping the stem.
- Flowers white, 8–10 mm, **four petals in a cross**, in rounded clusters, Apr–Aug.
- Small, round seed pods.
- Crushed leaves smell of cress; taste hot and bitter, like horseradish.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:lesser-celandine]] (poisonous raw): similar glossy heart-shaped leaves in spring, but **yellow** star flowers with 8–12 petals, no cress taste, and it grows in damp woods and hedge banks, not by the sea.
- Danish scurvygrass: smaller, with ivy-shaped leaves and white to lilac flowers, Jan–Jun. Also edible, but don't pick it from road verges (exhaust fumes and road salt).
- [[s:watercress]]: pinnate leaves, grows in fresh running water.

## Uses
- A few leaves raw in salads or sandwiches. It is strong, so treat it as a flavouring.
- Sailors once ate it to prevent scurvy. On a long stay with little fresh food, a small daily handful of a vitamin C-rich green helps.
- Cooking reduces the bitterness but also the vitamin C.

## Cautions
- Eat it in small amounts: in quantity it irritates the stomach.

=== sea-buckthorn
name: Sea buckthorn
latin: Hippophae rhamnoides
group: plant
kind: shrub
status: edible
season: Flowers Mar–Apr (before leaves); berries Sep–Dec
harvest: berries 9-12
habitat: Coastal sand dunes and sea cliffs, native mainly on the east coast of England; widely planted on dunes, road verges and in landscaping elsewhere
summary: Thorny, silvery-leaved shrub covered in dense clusters of bright orange berries. Very sour but edible, and one of the richest wild sources of vitamin C.
lookalikes: spindle, buckthorn, cotoneaster
tags: coast, dunes, berries, orange, vitamin c, thorns
wiki: Hippophae rhamnoides
---
## Identification
- Dense, very **thorny** shrub 1–3 m (sometimes a small tree), suckering into thickets on dunes.
- Leaves narrow and willow-like, 2–6 cm, **silvery-grey with tiny scales**, especially underneath.
- Male and female plants are separate; only females fruit.
- Berries round to oval, 6–8 mm, **bright orange**, packed tightly along the twigs, juicy and easily burst, each with one stone.
- Taste: intensely sour, citrus-like.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:spindle]] (poisonous): orange seeds hanging out of **pink four-lobed capsules**, broad green leaves, no thorns.
- [[s:buckthorn]] (purging buckthorn, poisonous): shares the name only. Black berries, oval green leaves.
- [[s:cotoneaster]] (poisonous): red berries, green leaves, no silvery scales.
- Firethorn (pyracantha) in gardens has orange-red berries and thorns but dark green, toothed leaves. Leave it.

## Uses
- Berries raw, a few at a time, or squeezed for juice and mixed with water or sweeter fruit. Cooked into jelly or syrup.
- High in vitamin C and oils.
- The berries burst when picked. Wear gloves and squeeze them straight into a pot, or snip a few small sprigs (with permission) and strip them.

## Cautions
- Long, sharp thorns. Dunes are fragile: keep to paths where you can.

=== wild-marjoram
name: Wild marjoram
latin: Origanum vulgare
aka: oregano
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible, medicinal
season: Leaves Apr–Oct; flowers Jul–Sep
harvest: leaves 4-10; flowers 7-9
habitat: Dry grassland, scrub, banks, quarries and roadsides on chalk and limestone; commonest in southern England, scattered further north
summary: Bushy aromatic herb with oval leaves in opposite pairs and clusters of small pink-purple flowers. It is wild oregano: a flavouring and a traditional tea.
lookalikes: hemp-agrimony, water-mint, wild-thyme
tags: oregano, herb, flavouring, tea, chalk, mint family
wiki: Origanum vulgare
---
## Identification
- Upright, slightly hairy, often purplish **square stems** 30–60 cm, branching near the top.
- Leaves in **opposite pairs**, oval, 1–4 cm, untoothed or faintly toothed, short-stalked.
- Flowers small (6–8 mm), pink-purple, two-lipped, in dense rounded clusters with dark purple bracts, Jul–Sep. Busy with butterflies.
- **Strong oregano smell** when crushed.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:hemp-agrimony]] (poisonous): similar dull pink flower heads at the same time, but it is much taller (to 1.5 m), grows in damp places, its leaves are split into 3–5 narrow toothed leaflets, and it doesn't smell of oregano.
- Mints such as [[s:water-mint]] smell of mint and have toothed leaves. Edible.
- Wild basil grows in the same places: hairier, with little scent. Edible.

## Uses
- Leaves and flowering tops as a flavouring, fresh or dried.
- A tea of the leaves and flowers is a traditional remedy for colds, coughs and indigestion.

## Cautions
- Avoid medicinal amounts (strong teas) in pregnancy.
- Pick the tops; don't uproot the plant.

=== wild-thyme
name: Wild thyme
latin: Thymus drucei
aka: mother of thyme, creeping thyme, Thymus polytrichus
group: plant
kind: herb (low undershrub)
status: edible, medicinal
season: Leaves all year; flowers May–Sep
harvest: leaves 1-12; flowers 5-9
habitat: Short, dry grassland on chalk and limestone, heaths, sand dunes, rocky outcrops, cliff tops and mountain ledges throughout the UK
summary: Low creeping mat of tiny paired leaves with rounded heads of pink-purple flowers, smelling of thyme. A flavouring and traditional tea.
lookalikes: wild-marjoram, heather
tags: thyme, herb, flavouring, tea, downland, mint family
wiki: Thymus praecox
---
## Identification
- Mat-forming, with creeping woody stems that root as they spread; flowering shoots only 2–10 cm tall.
- Leaves tiny (4–8 mm), oval, in **opposite pairs**, dotted with oil glands.
- Flowering stems square, **hairy on two opposite sides**.
- Flowers pink-purple, two-lipped, in rounded heads at the shoot tips, May–Sep.
- **Thyme smell** when crushed, milder than garden thyme.

## Lookalikes
- Nothing poisonous in the UK looks like thyme *and* smells of it. Use the smell.
- [[s:heather]] grows in the same places: needle-like leaves pressed along the stems, flowers in long spikes, no thyme smell. Not dangerous.
- Large thyme (in the south) is taller, with stems hairy on the corners. Also edible.
- [[s:wild-marjoram]]: taller, larger leaves, oregano smell. Edible.

## Uses
- Flavouring for meat, fish and stews.
- Tea from the flowering tops is a traditional remedy for coughs, sore throats and colds. Thyme oil (thymol) is antiseptic.

## Cautions
- Take a few sprigs from each patch; don't uproot it.
- Avoid medicinal amounts in pregnancy.

=== fat-hen
name: Fat hen
latin: Chenopodium album
aka: lamb's quarters, white goosefoot
group: plant
kind: herb (annual)
status: edible, caution
season: Leaves Apr–Oct; seeds Aug–Oct
harvest: leaves 4-10; seeds 8-10
habitat: Disturbed, rich ground: field edges, gardens, allotments, farmyards, manure heaps and building sites
summary: Common weed with goosefoot-shaped leaves dusted with a white, mealy bloom. A nourishing cooked green, like spinach, eaten in Britain since prehistory.
lookalikes: black-nightshade, dogs-mercury
tags: greens, spinach, weed, seeds, goosefoot, allotment
wiki: Chenopodium album
---
## Identification
- Upright annual 30–150 cm; stems ridged, often streaked red.
- Leaves alternate, diamond- to goose-foot-shaped, irregularly toothed, 3–8 cm.
- **White mealy powder** on young leaves and growing tips, especially underneath: they look floury and water beads off them.
- Flowers tiny, green, no petals, in dense mealy clusters on spikes at the top; then tiny black seeds.
- No strong smell.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:black-nightshade]] (poisonous) grows in the same places. Its leaves are dark green and **not mealy**, and it has white star-shaped flowers with yellow centres and green, then black, berries. If it has petals or berries, it isn't fat hen.
- [[s:dogs-mercury]] (poisonous): a woodland plant with toothed leaves in **opposite pairs**, no mealy coating.
- Oraches (often with arrow-shaped leaves) and Good King Henry are close relatives and also edible.

## Uses
- Young leaves and shoot tips cooked like spinach: steam, boil or add to stews.
- Seeds: rub off, winnow, rinse well (they contain soapy saponins) and cook as porridge. Slow work for little food.

## Cautions
- Contains oxalates: eat it cooked and in moderation. Avoid if you have kidney stones or gout.
- Plants on muck heaps or heavily fertilised ground can build up nitrates; pick from ordinary ground, away from sprayed crops.

=== ground-ivy
name: Ground ivy
latin: Glechoma hederacea
aka: alehoof, creeping Charlie, gill-over-the-ground
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible, medicinal, caution
season: Leaves all year; flowers Mar–Jun
harvest: leaves 1-12; flowers 3-6
habitat: Woodland edges, hedge banks, shady grassland, lawns and gardens, in damp or shady places throughout the UK
summary: Low, creeping mint-family plant with round, scalloped leaves and violet-blue flowers in spring. Strongly aromatic; used as a herb, a tea and once to flavour ale. Not related to ivy.
lookalikes: ivy, lesser-celandine, self-heal
tags: herb, tea, ale, mint family, creeping, lawn
wiki: Glechoma hederacea
---
## Identification
- Long creeping runners rooting at the joints, with upright flowering shoots 10–30 cm; **square stems**.
- Leaves in **opposite pairs**, kidney-shaped to round, 1–3 cm, with **rounded, scalloped teeth** and long stalks; slightly hairy, often purple-tinged.
- Flowers violet-blue, two-lipped, 1–2 cm, in small groups at the leaf joints, Mar–Jun.
- **Strong, pungent, minty-balsam smell** when crushed.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:ivy]] (poisonous): unrelated, despite the name. A woody climber with glossy, leathery, lobed leaves and no smell.
- [[s:lesser-celandine]] (poisonous raw): glossy, hairless heart-shaped leaves in a rosette, yellow flowers, no smell.
- [[s:self-heal]]: oval leaves, flowers in a dense head at the top of the stem. Edible.
- Red dead-nettle and henbit: pinker flowers, more pointed leaves. Edible.

## Uses
- A few young leaves raw in salads, or cooked as greens. The taste is strong and bitter, so use it as a herb.
- Tea from fresh or dried leaves is a traditional remedy for coughs, colds and catarrh.
- Before hops, it was used to clarify and flavour ale.

## Cautions
- Use small amounts. Avoid medicinal amounts in pregnancy.
- It is poisonous to horses in quantity.

=== wood-avens
name: Wood avens
latin: Geum urbanum
aka: herb bennet, clove root
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible, medicinal
season: Leaves Mar–Oct; flowers May–Aug; roots best Mar–Apr
harvest: young leaves 3-5; roots 3-4
habitat: Woodland edges, hedgerows, shady gardens and path sides; common almost everywhere in lowland Britain
summary: Woodland weed with small yellow five-petalled flowers, hooked burr-like seed heads and roots that smell of cloves. The root is a spice and a traditional remedy.
lookalikes: buttercup
tags: clove, spice, root, burrs, hedgerow, astringent
wiki: Geum urbanum
---
## Identification
- 20–60 cm, hairy, branched stems.
- Basal leaves **pinnate**: a large, rounded, three-lobed end leaflet with a few small, unequal leaflets lower down.
- Stem leaves three-lobed, with large **leafy "ears" (stipules)** where the stalk meets the stem.
- Flowers small (1–1.5 cm), **five yellow, matt petals with green sepals showing between them**, upright on long stalks, May–Aug.
- Seed head a round burr of **hooked**, red-brown seeds that stick to clothes and fur.
- Root: a short, thick rootstock with fibrous roots that **smells of cloves**, strongest in spring.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:buttercup]] (poisonous): glossy, varnished-looking petals that overlap, leaves deeply divided into three without leafy ears, no hooked burrs, no clove smell.
- Water avens (damp places) has nodding, pinkish-orange flowers. Its root is used the same way.

## Uses
- Root: wash, dry and use as a clove-like flavouring in stews, drinks or tea.
- Root tea is a traditional remedy for diarrhoea, sore throats and sore gums (it is astringent, full of tannins).
- Young leaves can be cooked as a green.

## Cautions
- Tannins in quantity upset the stomach.

## Law
- Digging up roots without the landowner's permission is an offence under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981.

=== japanese-knotweed
name: Japanese knotweed
latin: Reynoutria japonica
aka: Fallopia japonica
group: plant
kind: herb (giant perennial)
status: invasive, edible, caution
season: Shoots Mar–May; leaves May–Oct; flowers Aug–Oct; dead canes stand all winter
harvest: young shoots 3-5
habitat: Riverbanks, railway lines, roadsides, waste ground, gardens and car parks throughout the UK, especially in towns and along rivers
summary: Dense thickets of bamboo-like, red-speckled canes with shield-shaped leaves. Young shoots are edible, like rhubarb, but stands are often sprayed with weedkiller and the law on spreading it is strict.
lookalikes: himalayan-balsam, solomons-seal
tags: invasive, rhubarb, shoots, bamboo, weed, riverbank
wiki: Reynoutria japonica
---
## Identification
- Dense stands 2–3 m tall that die back in winter to brown, hollow, **bamboo-like canes** left standing.
- Spring shoots: fleshy, **red-purple, asparagus-like spears** with rolled-up leaves, growing fast.
- Stems hollow, green with **red-purple speckles**, with distinct joints, zigzagging from joint to joint.
- Leaves **shield- or heart-shaped with a flat base**, 10–15 cm, untoothed, pointed, alternate along arching stems.
- Flowers: creamy-white tassels at the leaf joints, Aug–Oct.
- Broken rhizome (underground stem) is orange-yellow inside.

## Lookalikes
- Giant and Bohemian knotweeds: bigger, also invasive; the same rules apply.
- Bindweed has heart-shaped leaves but is a twining climber with no canes.
- [[s:himalayan-balsam]] often grows alongside: pink flowers, toothed leaves in pairs or threes, smooth, translucent stems.
- [[s:solomons-seal]] (poisonous) shoots are also spear-like, but smooth and plain green, with no red speckles or joints.

## Uses
- Snap off young shoots 15–25 cm tall, before the leaves open. Peel if stringy, and cook: stew like rhubarb or steam. Sour and lemony.

## Dangers
- **Weedkiller.** Councils, railway and river authorities spray it. Avoid stands with yellowing, browning, twisted or dead patches, and those beside railways and roads. If you can't be sure it hasn't been sprayed, don't eat it.
- High in oxalic acid, like rhubarb: eat it cooked and in moderation. Avoid if you have kidney stones, gout or arthritis.

## Law
- It is listed in Schedule 9 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: it is an offence to plant it or **cause it to grow in the wild**. A fragment of rhizome the size of a fingernail can grow a new plant.
- Carry away only cut shoots, never roots or soil, and don't drop trimmings anywhere. Don't compost it.
- Knotweed waste, including soil containing it, is controlled waste under the Environmental Protection Act 1990 and must go to a licensed disposal site.

=== himalayan-balsam
name: Himalayan balsam
latin: Impatiens glandulifera
aka: policeman's helmet
group: plant
kind: herb (annual)
status: invasive, edible, caution
season: Shoots Apr–May; flowers Jun–Oct; seed pods Jul–Oct
harvest: flowers 6-10; seeds 7-10
habitat: Riverbanks, streamsides, wet woodland, ditches and damp waste ground throughout the UK, often in dense stands
summary: Tall, pink-flowered annual with hollow reddish stems and seed pods that explode when touched, smothering riverbanks. Flowers and seeds are edible; you must not spread the seeds.
lookalikes: japanese-knotweed
tags: invasive, riverbank, seeds, flowers, pink, exploding pods
wiki: Impatiens glandulifera
---
## Identification
- Annual 1–2.5 m; stems hollow, **smooth, translucent and often red-flushed**, jointed, snapping easily.
- Leaves lance-shaped, 5–15 cm, finely toothed, in **opposite pairs or whorls of three**, with small reddish glands at the base of the stalk.
- Flowers pink to purple (sometimes white), 2.5–4 cm, **helmet- or slipper-shaped**, hanging on long stalks, with a heavy sweet scent.
- Seed pods club-shaped, green, 1.5–3 cm, that **explode** when touched, flinging seeds several metres.
- Shallow roots: the whole plant pulls up easily.

## Lookalikes
- Nothing dangerous looks like it in flower. Orange balsam and small balsam are smaller, with orange or pale yellow flowers.
- [[s:japanese-knotweed]] grows in the same places: speckled canes, shield-shaped untoothed leaves.

## Uses
- Seeds: nutty, raw or toasted. Cup a bag or hand round ripe pods and squeeze them gently.
- Flowers: raw, as a garnish or snack.
- Stick to flowers and seeds. The leaves and stems are sometimes eaten cooked, but they are bitter and poorly documented as food.

## Law
- Listed in Schedule 9 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981: it is an offence to plant it or cause it to grow in the wild.
- Don't carry seeds or pods to new places, and check boots and clothes before leaving a stand. Pulling it up before it seeds is a common conservation task (with the landowner's permission).

=== rosebay-willowherb
name: Rosebay willowherb
latin: Chamaenerion angustifolium
aka: fireweed
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible
season: Young shoots Apr–May; leaves May–Aug; flowers Jun–Sep; seed down Aug–Oct
harvest: young shoots 4-5; leaves 5-8; flowers 6-9
habitat: Woodland clearings, felled and burnt ground, railway banks, waste ground, heaths and road verges throughout the UK
summary: Tall spikes of magenta flowers over long, narrow leaves, colonising cleared and burnt ground. Young shoots and leaves are edible, and the silky seed down helps a spark catch.
lookalikes: foxglove, hemp-agrimony
tags: fireweed, shoots, greens, tea, tinder, down
wiki: Chamaenerion angustifolium
---
## Identification
- Upright, unbranched, often reddish stems 50–150 cm, usually in large colonies.
- Leaves **long, narrow and willow-like**, 5–15 cm, untoothed, spiralling up the stem, pale beneath. The side veins **loop round and join before reaching the edge**.
- Flowers magenta-pink, 2–3 cm, **four flat, open petals**, in tall tapering spikes that open from the bottom up.
- Long, thin, reddish seed pods split to release seeds with **white, silky down** in late summer.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:foxglove]] (deadly) also makes tall pink-purple spikes in woodland clearings. Its flowers are **tubular bells hanging to one side**, and its leaves are broad, soft, downy and toothed, from a rosette. Willowherb flowers are flat and open; its leaves narrow and hairless.
- [[s:hemp-agrimony]] (poisonous): fluffy, dull pink flower heads and leaves split into 3–5 leaflets.
- Purple loosestrife (wet places) has six petals and leaves in pairs or threes. Don't eat it.

## Uses
- Young shoots (under 20 cm): peel and cook like asparagus. Bitter raw.
- Young leaves cooked as greens; flowers raw.
- Dried leaves make a tea.
- **Seed down**: a fast-burning tinder extender. Mix it with finer tinder; on its own it flashes and goes out. See [[a:natural-tinders]].

## Cautions
- Mildly laxative in quantity.

=== chicory
name: Chicory
latin: Cichorium intybus
aka: wild succory, blue sailors
group: plant
kind: herb
status: edible, medicinal
season: Leaves Mar–Jun; flowers Jul–Oct; roots Sep–Mar

## Identification
- Large tree to 35 m with a dense, domed crown. Bark smooth and grey when young, later flaking in plates to show pinkish-orange beneath.
- Leaves in **opposite pairs**, large (10–20 cm), with **five pointed, coarsely toothed lobes**, on long, often reddish stalks. Often spotted with black "tar spot" in late summer.
- Buds green, in opposite pairs.
- Flowers in hanging yellow-green spikes, Apr–May.
- Seeds: pairs of **winged keys joined in a V**, spinning as they fall.

## Lookalikes
- Norway maple: leaves with long, fine points, and **milky sap** in the leaf stalk; keys spread wide. Its sap can be tapped too.
- Field maple: small leaves with blunt lobes, milky sap, keys in a straight line.
- London plane: leaves alternate, patchwork peeling bark, round bobble fruits.
- None of these is dangerous to tap.

## Uses
- **Sap** rises in late winter (Feb–Mar), best on mild days after frosty nights, before the buds open. - **Sooty bark disease**: dead or dying sycamores with blistered, peeling bark and **black, sooty spore masses** underneath. Breathing the spores can cause serious lung inflammation. Don't cut, split, carry or burn this wood, and never bring it into a shelter.

- [[s:dandelion]] also has milky sap, but has a rosette of jagged leaves and a yellow flower head.

## Dangers
- Eating it burns the mouth and causes vomiting and diarrhoea.
- The sap is a folk remedy for warts. Don't: a slip into the eye is not worth it.

## If someone has eaten it or got sap on them
- Mouth: rinse well, sips of water, don't make them sick. Get medical advice ([[a:poisoning]]).
- Skin: wash with soap and plenty of water.
- Eyes: rinse with clean water for at least 15 minutes, then get medical help.

=== meadow-saffron
name: Meadow saffron
latin: Colchicum autumnale
aka: autumn crocus, naked ladies
group: plant
kind: herb (corm)
status: deadly
season: Leaves Mar–Jun (with a seed capsule in the middle); flowers Aug–Oct with no leaves
habitat: Damp meadows and open woods on limestone and clay, mainly in southern and central England and the Welsh borders; scarce in the wild but common in gardens and churchyards
summary: Pale pink-purple crocus-like flowers in autumn with no leaves; broad, glossy, tulip-like leaves in spring. Deadly: the spring leaves have been mistaken for wild garlic.

tags: colchicine, autumn crocus, crocus, wild garlic lookalike, corm
wiki: Colchicum autumnale
---
!danger Deadly poisonous
All parts contain colchicine. Symptoms often start several hours after eating, so the person may feel fine at first: then burning of the mouth, severe vomiting and diarrhoea, followed over days by failure of the heart, kidneys and blood. There is no simple antidote. **Get emergency help at once for anyone who may have eaten it, even if they feel well.**
!end

## Identification
- Spring (Mar–Jun): a tuft of 3–8 **broad, lance-shaped, glossy, hairless leaves**, 15–30 cm long and 2–4 cm wide, upright, **wrapped around each other at the base**, with **no garlic smell**.
- Early summer: a fat, green, egg-shaped **seed capsule** (3–5 cm) appears in the middle of the leaves. The leaves wither by July.
- Autumn (Aug–Oct): pale pink-lilac, crocus-like flowers with **six stamens** rise straight from the ground on long white tubes, with **no leaves**.
- Underground: a brown-skinned corm.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:wild-garlic]]: each leaf has **its own stalk** from the ground, is soft and thin, and **smells of garlic**. ## If someone has eaten it

- Don't make them sick. Rinse the mouth; sips of water only.

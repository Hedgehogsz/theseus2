=== saffron-milkcap
name: Saffron milkcap
latin: Lactarius deliciosus
aka: red pine mushroom
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: edible, caution
season: Aug–Nov
harvest: fruiting bodies 8-11
habitat: On the ground under pines, especially Scots pine, often in grass or moss at plantation edges; widespread but commonest in Scotland and on sandy soils
summary: Carrot-orange cap with darker concentric rings, orange gills and orange milk that oozes when you cut it. Bruises and ages green. Lookalikes with white milk are poisonous.
lookalikes: woolly-milkcap, ugly-milkcap, the-sickener
tags: milkcap, lactarius, orange, pine, latex, milk, green bruising
wiki: Lactarius deliciosus
---
!warn Expert confirmation needed
The test is the **milk**: cut a gill and it must ooze **carrot-orange** milk. Any orange or pink milkcap with **white** milk is not this one, and may be the poisonous [[s:woolly-milkcap]]. Never eat any wild fungus unless an expert has checked it in person.
!end

## Identification
- **Cap** 4–12 cm, orange to carrot or apricot, with **darker orange concentric rings** (zones). Convex with a sunken centre, becoming a shallow funnel. Smooth, slightly sticky when wet, with an inrolled edge when young. No wool or hairs on the edge.
- **Gills** crowded, orange, running slightly down the stem. They **bruise green** where handled.
- **Stem** short (3–6 cm), stout, orange, hollow when old, often marked with small darker orange **pits** (like thumbprints).
- **Milk (latex)**: cut or break a gill and drops of **bright carrot-orange** milk appear. It stays orange for some minutes, slowly darkening.
- **Flesh** pale with orange streaks near the edge; whole mushroom becomes **stained green** with age, handling or frost.
- **Smell** faint and fruity. **Taste** mild, perhaps slightly bitter.
- **Spore print** pale pinkish-buff to cream.
- **Habitat and season**: only near **pines**, August to November. The very similar false saffron milkcap (*Lactarius deterrimus*) grows under **spruce** and its milk turns **wine-red** within about half an hour.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:woolly-milkcap]] (poisonous): pinkish-orange cap with a **shaggy, woolly fringe** on the inrolled edge; **white** milk that tastes fiercely hot; grows with **birch**, not pine.
- [[s:ugly-milkcap]] (poisonous): dirty olive-brown to black cap, **white** milk; birch woods.
- Other orange-brown milkcaps: almost all have **white** or watery milk. Several are hot, bitter or poisonous.
- [[s:the-sickener]] and other red brittlegills: no milk at all, white gills, stem snaps like chalk.
- The red-milk relatives (*Lactarius deterrimus* under spruce, *L. sanguifluus* with wine-red milk) are not dangerous, but they show why the tree and the milk colour both matter.

## Eating
- Only once an expert has confirmed it in person. Cook thoroughly. Often maggoty: cut lengthways to check.
- It can turn **urine orange-red** for a day. This is harmless, but it alarms people: tell anyone who has eaten it.
- Picking for your own use is generally tolerated in the UK; commercial picking, SSSIs, nature reserves and many National Trust sites are restricted. See [[a:foraging-rules]].

=== woolly-milkcap
name: Woolly milkcap
latin: Lactarius torminosus
aka: bearded milkcap
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: poisonous
season: Jul–Oct
habitat: Always with birch: heaths, birch scrub, woodland edges, parks and gardens with birch trees
summary: Pale salmon-pink cap with darker rings and a shaggy, woolly beard around the inrolled edge. White, fiercely hot milk. Causes violent vomiting and diarrhoea.
lookalikes: saffron-milkcap
tags: milkcap, lactarius, pink, birch, woolly, poisonous, latex
wiki: Lactarius torminosus
---
!danger Poisonous
A severe gut irritant. Eaten raw or badly prepared it causes violent vomiting, diarrhoea and stomach cramps. It is the classic mistake when people look for [[s:saffron-milkcap]].
!end

## Identification
- **Cap** 4–12 cm, pale salmon-pink to flesh-coloured or pale orange, with **darker pink concentric rings**. Sunken centre, becoming a funnel.
- **Edge**: strongly **inrolled** and covered in long, **shaggy, woolly hairs** like a beard. This is the key feature.
- **Gills** crowded, pale cream to pinkish, running slightly down the stem. They do **not** bruise green.
- **Stem** 3–8 cm, pale pink, often with darker pits, hollow when old.
- **Milk (latex)**: **white**, does not change colour, and tastes **burning hot** (if you have touched it, don't lick your fingers; wash your hands).
- **Spore print** cream to pale pinkish.
- **Habitat and season**: with **birch** only, July to October, often in grass or on heaths.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:saffron-milkcap]] (edible): **carrot-orange milk**, no woolly beard, bruises green, grows under **pine** not birch.
- The rarer *Lactarius pubescens* (also with birch) is paler and smaller, with the same woolly edge and white milk. Also poisonous: treat them the same.

## Dangers
- Symptoms usually start **within 30 minutes to 3 hours**: nausea, repeated vomiting, watery diarrhoea and stomach cramps. Rarely fatal in healthy adults, but children and frail people can become dangerously dehydrated.
- **First aid**: get them to medical help when you can ([[a:when-you-reach-help]]). **Don't** make them vomit. Rinse their mouth and give small, frequent sips of water to replace lost fluid ([[a:diarrhoea-ors]]). **Keep a sample** (the whole mushroom, or scraps from the pan) in paper. Note when it was eaten and when symptoms began. See [[a:poisoning]].
- If symptoms start **more than 6 hours** after eating, suspect something deadlier was in the meal, such as [[s:death-cap]]: get them to hospital urgently even if they improve.

=== ugly-milkcap
name: Ugly milkcap
latin: Lactarius turpis
aka: Lactarius necator
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: poisonous
season: Jul–Nov
habitat: Under birch in woods, heaths and parks, sometimes with spruce; often half-hidden in moss and leaf litter; common
summary: Dull olive-brown to almost black, sticky cap, cream gills that stain brown, and white, burning-hot milk. Contains a toxin that damaged DNA in laboratory tests.
lookalikes: saffron-milkcap, woolly-milkcap
tags: milkcap, lactarius, olive, black, birch, necatorin, poisonous
wiki: Lactarius turpis
---
!danger Poisonous: don't eat
Acrid and irritant to the gut. It also contains **necatorin**, which damaged DNA in laboratory tests and is suspected of causing cancer. Some cuisines process it to make it edible; don't.
!end

## Identification
- **Cap** 5–20 cm, **dirty olive-brown to olive-black**, darker in the centre and paler, more olive-yellow, at the edge. Sticky when wet, so bits of leaf stick to it. Edge inrolled and slightly felty when young; centre sunken.
- **Gills** crowded, cream to buff, running slightly down the stem; they **stain olive-brown to dark grey** where damaged.
- **Stem** short and stout (3–7 cm), olive, paler than the cap, often with darker pits, sticky.
- **Milk (latex)**: **white**, very **hot and acrid** to taste. Where it dries on the gills it leaves grey-green stains.
- **Smell** faint. **Spore print** cream to pale buff.
- **Habitat and season**: almost always with **birch**, July to November.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:saffron-milkcap]]: **orange** cap and **orange** milk, under pine.
- [[s:woolly-milkcap]]: pink with a woolly edge; also poisonous.
- It is drab enough to be passed over, but beginners sometimes pick it as an unfamiliar "brown mushroom": another reason not to eat what you can't name for certain.

## Dangers
- Eaten, it causes nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and stomach pain, usually within a few hours. Any long-term risk from the mutagen is unknown.
- **First aid**: get medical help when you can ([[a:when-you-reach-help]]). **Don't** induce vomiting. Small sips of water. **Keep a sample.** Note what, how much and when. See [[a:poisoning]].

=== charcoal-burner
name: Charcoal burner
latin: Russula cyanoxantha
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: edible, caution
season: Jul–Nov
harvest: fruiting bodies 7-11
habitat: Broadleaf woods, especially under beech and oak, sometimes birch; very common across the UK
summary: A brittlegill with a cap blending purple, slate-blue, grey and green, and white gills that feel greasy and bend rather than crumble. Green forms must be told from the death cap.
lookalikes: death-cap, the-sickener
tags: russula, brittlegill, purple, green, beech, oak, flexible gills
wiki: Russula cyanoxantha
---
!warn Expert confirmation needed
Brittlegills (*Russula*) vary hugely and many are hot or sickening. A greenish one under oak or beech could be [[s:death-cap]]: always dig up the base and look for a ring and a sac (volva). Never eat any wild fungus unless an expert has checked it in person.
!end

## Identification
- **Cap** 5–15 cm, firm, convex then flat or slightly sunken. Colours are **mixed and blended**: violet, purple, slate-blue, wine, grey and often **green** patches, sometimes almost all green. Slightly greasy when wet; the skin peels only part of the way to the centre.
- **Gills** white, crowded, slightly forked near the stem. Run a finger across them: they feel **greasy and flexible** and do **not** shatter. Most other brittlegills' gills crumble like chalk.
- **Stem** 5–10 cm, stout, **pure white** (sometimes flushed lilac), **no ring**, **no sac at the base**. It snaps cleanly, like a piece of chalk or apple, not stringy.
- **Flesh** white, firm, sometimes lilac just under the cap skin. Does not change colour.
- **Smell** faint. **Taste** mild and nutty, never hot.
- **Spore print** **white**.
- **Habitat and season**: under beech and oak, July to November.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:death-cap]] (deadly): olive-green to yellow-green cap, white gills, but **a drooping ring on the stem** and a **bag-like volva** at the base (often buried: dig it up). Its stem is fibrous, not chalky. Never confuse "green mushroom with white gills" with safe.
- [[s:the-sickener]] and other hot brittlegills (poisonous): bright red or mixed colours, brittle crumbly gills, **burning hot taste**. They cause vomiting.
- The greencracked brittlegill (*Russula virescens*, edible) has a cap cracked into green patches like crazy paving. It too must be told from the death cap.

## Eating
- Only once an expert has confirmed it in person. Cook thoroughly; it is often eaten by slugs and maggots.
- Brittlegills have no deadly members in the UK, but the sickening ones cause miserable vomiting, and the danger is mixing in an Amanita. Check every specimen's base.
- Picking for your own use is generally tolerated; commercial picking and many protected sites are restricted. See [[a:foraging-rules]].

=== the-sickener
name: The sickener
latin: Russula emetica
aka: emetic russula
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: poisonous
season: Jul–Nov
habitat: Damp conifer woods, especially under pine, often in moss or sphagnum bogs; common
summary: Shiny cherry-red cap, pure white gills and a pure white, brittle stem. Tastes burningly hot. Causes vomiting and diarrhoea, as the name says.
lookalikes: fly-agaric, charcoal-burner
tags: russula, brittlegill, red, pine, emetic, sphagnum, poisonous
wiki: Russula emetica
---
!danger Poisonous
Causes vomiting, diarrhoea and stomach cramps, usually starting within 30 minutes to 3 hours. Rarely serious in fit adults, but dehydrating.
!end

## Identification
- **Cap** 3–10 cm, **bright cherry to scarlet red**, shiny and sticky when wet, becoming flat or slightly sunken. Rain can wash it paler pink. The skin peels almost to the centre, showing **pinkish-red flesh** just beneath.
- **Gills** white to pale cream, fairly crowded, **brittle**: they crumble when rubbed.
- **Stem** 4–9 cm, **pure white**, no ring, no sac at the base; it snaps like chalk.
- **Flesh** white, crumbly. **Taste** immediately **burning hot** (if you have tasted one, spit it all out and rinse your mouth).
- **Smell** faintly fruity. **Spore print** white.
- **Habitat and season**: with conifers, especially pine, in wet mossy places, July to November. The beechwood sickener (*Russula nobilis*) is a similar red species under beech, also poisonous.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:fly-agaric]] (poisonous): red cap with **white warts** (they can wash off), a **ring** on the stem and a swollen base with rings of volva scales. Dig up the base.
- Other red brittlegills: several are mild and harmless, but telling them apart needs a microscope or an expert. Treat every red brittlegill as the sickener.
- [[s:charcoal-burner]]: mixed purple-green cap, **flexible** gills, mild taste.

## Dangers
- Symptoms: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and abdominal cramps within about 30 minutes to 3 hours. Usually over within a day.
- **First aid**: get medical help when you can ([[a:when-you-reach-help]]), especially for a child or an older person. **Don't** induce vomiting; give small, frequent sips of water. **Keep a sample.** If symptoms only start 6 or more hours after eating, suspect a deadly species and get to hospital urgently. See [[a:poisoning]].

=== orange-birch-bolete
name: Orange birch bolete
latin: Leccinum versipelle
group: fungus
kind: bolete
status: edible, caution
season: Jul–Nov
harvest: fruiting bodies 7-11
habitat: Always with birch: birch woods, heaths and moorland edges with heather; common, especially in the north and west
summary: Big orange cap, grey pores and a tall white stem covered in black scales. The flesh turns grey-violet then blackish when cut. Must be thoroughly cooked.
lookalikes: bitter-bolete, brown-birch-bolete
tags: bolete, leccinum, scaber stalk, orange cap, birch, pores
wiki: Leccinum versipelle
---
!warn Expert confirmation needed
Eaten raw or undercooked, scaber stalks (*Leccinum*) have caused vomiting and diarrhoea. Even when well cooked, a few people react. Never eat any wild fungus unless an expert has checked it in person.
!end

## Identification
- **Cap** 8–20 cm, **orange to tawny orange**, dry and slightly felty. The cap skin **overhangs the edge** as a ragged flap.
- **Pores** (not gills) small, **dark mouse-grey** when young, becoming greyish-buff. Not yellow, not red.
- **Stem** tall (10–20 cm), thick, whitish, covered in **small black-brown scales** (like coarse pepper), widening towards the base. No ring.
- **Flesh** white, firm, then turns **pinkish-grey, grey-violet and finally blackish** within minutes of cutting. The stem base may stain blue-green.
- **Smell** pleasant. **Spore print** ochre-brown.
- **Habitat and season**: under **birch**, July to November, often with heather.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:bitter-bolete]]: **pinkish** pores, a **dark brown net pattern** on the stem (not black scales), and horribly bitter. Not dangerous but spoils a meal.
- [[s:brown-birch-bolete]]: brown cap, pale pores, flesh barely changes colour.
- Other orange scaber stalks (with aspen, poplar or oak) are similar and are treated the same.
- Beginners should still avoid any bolete with **red or orange pores** or flesh that turns quickly **blue** ([[s:devils-bolete]] and relatives).

## Eating
- Only once an expert has confirmed it in person. **Cook thoroughly** (at least 15–20 minutes). It blackens in the pan; this is normal.
- Old, soft specimens go mushy and maggoty. Cut lengthways to check.
- Picking for your own use is generally tolerated; commercial picking and many protected sites are restricted. See [[a:foraging-rules]].

=== slippery-jack
name: Slippery jack
latin: Suillus luteus
group: fungus
kind: bolete
status: edible, caution
season: Aug–Nov
harvest: fruiting bodies 8-11
habitat: Under pines, especially Scots pine in young plantations, heaths and on sandy soils; common
summary: Chocolate-brown, very slimy cap, small yellow pores, and a ring on the stem that turns purplish-brown. The slimy skin upsets some people.
lookalikes: bay-bolete
tags: bolete, suillus, slimy, pine, ring, yellow pores
wiki: Suillus luteus
---
!warn Expert confirmation needed
The slimy cap skin causes diarrhoea in some people and is usually peeled off. Some people are allergic. Never eat any wild fungus unless an expert has checked it in person.
!end

## Identification
- **Cap** 5–12 cm, **chestnut to dark chocolate brown**, covered in **thick slime** when wet, shiny when dry.
- **Pores** (not gills) small, **pale yellow**, becoming olive-yellow. They do not bruise blue.
- **Stem** 4–10 cm, pale yellow above the ring with tiny brownish dots, whitish below.
- **Ring**: a large, floppy, whitish membrane that turns **purple-brown** underneath as spores land on it. This is unusual for a bolete.
- **Flesh** white to pale yellow, soft, does not change colour when cut.
- **Spore print** clay-brown.
- **Habitat and season**: with **pines** only, August to November.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:bay-bolete]] (edible): dry chestnut cap, pores bruising blue, **no ring**.
- Larch bolete (*Suillus grevillei*): bright **yellow-orange** slimy cap and a ring, under **larch** only. Treated as edible with the same cautions.
- Weeping bolete (*Suillus granulatus*): no ring, pores weeping milky droplets; pine.
- Beginners should avoid any bolete with **red or orange pores**, or flesh that turns quickly **blue** ([[s:devils-bolete]]).

## Eating
- Only once an expert has confirmed it in person. Peel the slimy skin, pick young ones, and cook thoroughly. Wear gloves if your skin reacts.
- Picking for your own use is generally tolerated; commercial picking and many protected sites are restricted. See [[a:foraging-rules]].

=== velvet-shank
name: Velvet shank
latin: Flammulina velutipes
aka: winter mushroom, wild enoki
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: edible, caution
season: Oct–Mar; survives frost
harvest: fruiting bodies 10-3
habitat: Clusters on dead and dying broadleaf wood, especially elm, ash, beech, oak and willow, also gorse; stumps, fallen logs and trunks
summary: Clusters of sticky orange-brown caps on dead wood in winter, with a velvety, dark brown to black stem and a white spore print. The deadly funeral bell grows in the same places.
lookalikes: funeral-bell, sulphur-tuft, honey-fungus
tags: winter mushroom, enoki, clusters, wood, velvet stem, frost
wiki: Flammulina velutipes
---
!warn Deadly lookalike on the same logs
[[s:funeral-bell]] grows in small brown clusters on wood and contains the same toxin as the death cap. Check **every** mushroom for a velvety dark stem, no ring, and a **white** spore print. Never eat any wild fungus unless an expert has checked it in person.
!end

## Identification
- **Cap** 2–10 cm, **honey-orange to tan**, darker orange-brown in the centre, paler at the edge. **Sticky to slimy** when wet, shiny when dry.
- **Gills** pale cream to yellowish, fairly widely spaced, notched where they meet the stem.
- **Stem** 3–10 cm, tough, **velvety**, **dark brown to black** at the base, paler and yellowish at the top. **No ring.**
- **Flesh** thin, pale yellow. **Smell** mild.
- **Spore print** **white**.
- **Habitat and season**: tufts on dead broadleaf wood from **October to March**, even after frost and snow. Few other mushrooms fruit then.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:funeral-bell]] (deadly): brown, smooth, not slimy cap; gills turning rusty-brown; a **small ring** or ring zone on a pale, silky (not velvety black) stem; **rusty-brown spore print**. Mostly autumn but overlaps.
- [[s:sulphur-tuft]] (poisonous): sulphur-yellow cap and **greenish-yellow gills becoming purple-black**; spore print purple-brown; very bitter.
- [[s:honey-fungus]] (caution): dark scales on the cap, a thick **ring**, autumn.
- **Always take a spore print** of one cap from every cluster: white is required.

## Eating
- Only once an expert has confirmed it in person. Discard the tough stems. **Cook thoroughly**: raw velvet shank contains a protein (flammutoxin) that cooking destroys.
- The cultivated "enoki" sold in shops is a related, pale form grown in the dark.
- Picking for your own use is generally tolerated; commercial picking and many protected sites are restricted. See [[a:foraging-rules]].

=== horn-of-plenty
name: Horn of plenty
latin: Craterellus cornucopioides
aka: black trumpet, trumpet of death
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: edible, caution
season: Aug–Nov
harvest: fruiting bodies 8-11
habitat: In leaf litter under beech and oak, often on clay or chalky soils and along banks and ditches; local, but often in large groups
summary: Thin, hollow, black-grey trumpets with no gills: smooth or faintly wrinkled grey outside, sooty black inside. Very hard to spot in leaf litter.
lookalikes: trumpet-chanterelle
tags: black trumpet, trumpet, craterellus, beech, no gills, black mushroom
wiki: Craterellus cornucopioides
---
!warn Expert confirmation needed
It has few dangerous lookalikes, but beginners pick mixed bags in dark leaf litter. Check every piece. Never eat any wild fungus unless an expert has checked it in person.
!end

## Identification
- **Shape**: a thin, **hollow trumpet or funnel** 3–10 cm tall and 2–8 cm across, **hollow right down to the base**, with a wavy, rolled-back rim.
- **Inside** (top surface): **sooty brown-black to black**, slightly scaly.
- **Outside** (underside): **ash-grey**, smooth or faintly wrinkled. **No gills, no ridges, no pores.**
- **Flesh** very thin, leathery-brittle, grey-black. No proper separate stem.
- **Smell** pleasant, fruity when dried. **Spore print** white to pale cream.
- **Habitat and season**: in groups under beech and oak, August to November.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:trumpet-chanterelle]]: brownish cap with **grey ridges** underneath and a **yellow** stem.
- The ashen chanterelle (*Cantharellus cinereus*, rare): grey with **blunt ridges** underneath; edible.
- Black elf cups and black earth tongues are small, not funnel-shaped, and not hollow to the base.

## Eating
- Only once an expert has confirmed it in person. Slit each trumpet to clean out grit and insects. Cook thoroughly; it dries very well.
- Picking for your own use is generally tolerated; commercial picking and many protected sites are restricted. See [[a:foraging-rules]].

=== trumpet-chanterelle
name: Trumpet chanterelle
latin: Craterellus tubaeformis
aka: winter chanterelle, yellowfoot
group: fungus
kind: mushroom
status: edible, caution
season: Aug–Dec; survives the first frosts
harvest: fruiting bodies 9-12
habitat: Mossy conifer and mixed woods on acid soils, often among moss or on very rotten wood; commonest in Scotland, Wales and the west
summary: Small brown funnel caps with grey, blunt, forked ridges underneath and a hollow yellow stem. Grows in the same mossy conifer woods as the deadly webcap.
lookalikes: deadly-webcap, false-chanterelle, chanterelle
tags: winter chanterelle, yellowfoot, funnel, ridges, conifer, moss
wiki: Craterellus tubaeformis
---
!danger Deadly lookalike in the same moss
[[s:deadly-webcap]] is small, orange-brown and grows in the same mossy conifer woods. It has **true, rusty-brown gills** and a rusty spore print, and destroys the kidneys days later. Check every single mushroom in your basket. Never eat any wild fungus unless an expert has checked it in person.
!end

## Identification
- **Cap** 1–6 cm, **yellow-brown to dark brown**, thin, funnel-shaped, often with a hole in the centre leading down into the hollow stem. Wavy edge.
- **Underside**: **blunt, shallow, forked ridges** (false gills), **grey to yellowish-grey**, running down the stem. Not thin, sharp, separate gills.
- **Stem** 3–8 cm, **hollow**, **yellow to orange-yellow**, often flattened or grooved.
- **Flesh** thin and rubbery. **Smell** faintly earthy-fruity.
- **Spore print** white to cream.
- **Habitat and season**: in large troops in mossy conifer woods, August to December.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:deadly-webcap]] (deadly): orange-brown cap often with a small pointed hump; **true gills, rusty-brown** when mature; stem not hollow, with yellowish bands; **rusty-brown spore print**. Symptoms start 2–21 days later, from kidney failure.
- [[s:false-chanterelle]]: orange, with true, thin, **crowded, forked gills**; not hollow.
- [[s:chanterelle]]: bigger, egg-yolk yellow all over, solid stem.
- Small brown mushrooms in general: if it has thin, separate gills, it is not this.

## Eating
- Only once an expert has confirmed it in person. Cook thoroughly; good dried.
- Picking for your own use is generally tolerated; commercial picking and many protected sites are restricted. See [[a:foraging-rules]].

## Identification
- **Shape**: a rounded mass **10–40 cm** across (sometimes larger), made of many **flattened, crinkled, wavy lobes** packed together, like a cauliflower, a natural sponge or egg noodles.
- **Colour**: cream to pale yellow when fresh, darkening to tan and brown at the lobe edges with age.
- **Texture**: firm and elastic, rubbery when young. Grows from a thick, tough, rooting base.
- **No gills, no pores.** Spores form on the lobe surfaces. **Spore print** white to cream.
- **Smell** pleasant, sweetish and spicy.
- **Habitat and season**: at the base of **pines**, August to November. It is a parasite that rots the heartwood of its tree.

## Lookalikes
- Coral fungi (*Ramaria* species, on the ground): **upright, rounded branches** like a coral, not flat curled lobes; some, such as *Ramaria formosa*, cause vomiting and diarrhoea.
- [[s:hen-of-the-woods]]: many small, grey-brown overlapping caps with **pores** underneath; at the foot of **oaks**.
- *Sparassis spathulata* (rare, under oak): broader, flatter lobes with faint bands; same cautions.

## Eating
- Only once an expert has confirmed it in person. Cut it off above the base so it can fruit again. Clean thoroughly (pine needles, grit and insects hide between the lobes). Cook thoroughly.
- Picking for your own use is generally tolerated; commercial picking and many protected sites are restricted. See [[a:foraging-rules]].

## Identification
- **Cap** 5–15 cm, egg-shaped then flattened. The brown cap skin breaks into **coarse, shaggy, upturned brown scales** on a fibrous whitish background, leaving a smooth brown centre. No raised hump in the centre.
- **Gills** white, crowded, **free** (not touching the stem), bruising reddish-brown.

status: poisonous

habitat: Leaf litter in woods, path edges, gardens, lawns, nettle beds and compost; rich, disturbed soil; common

!danger Poisonous: small relatives are deadly
Several small dapperlings (for example *Lepiota brunneoincarnata* and *Lepiota subincarnata*) contain **amatoxins**, the death-cap toxin, and have killed people in Europe. They are hard to tell apart. **Never eat any small parasol-shaped mushroom.** If someone has, treat it as a deadly poisoning.

## Identification
- **Cap** 2–5 cm, white, bell-shaped then flat with a central hump. The **reddish-brown** centre breaks up into **concentric rings of small brown scales** on a white background.

- [[s:parasol]] and [[s:shaggy-parasol]]: much bigger (cap 10–30 cm), with a thick, movable double ring. Anyone collecting parasols must reject every small one.

- *Lepiota cristata* itself causes vomiting and diarrhoea. But a mistaken identity could mean an amatoxin species: **6–24 hours** with no symptoms, then violent vomiting and watery diarrhoea, an apparent recovery, then liver and kidney failure 3–5 days later.

- **Egg stage**: a soft, white to off-white "egg" 3–6 cm across, half-buried, attached by a **white root-like cord**. Cut in half, it shows a layer of clear **jelly** around a dark olive core and a pale developing stalk. No gills.
- **Mature**: a **white, spongy, hollow stalk** 10–25 cm tall, rising from the torn egg (which stays as a cup at the base).
- **Cap**: a small thimble, honeycombed underneath, covered in **dark olive-green slime** that carries the spores.
- **Smell**: **overpowering, like rotting meat**. You usually smell it before you see it. Flies strip the slime within a day or so, leaving a white honeycombed cap.
- **Habitat and season**: woods and gardens, June to November.

## Lookalikes
- [[s:death-cap]] and other Amanita **eggs**: also white and egg-shaped. Cut in half, an Amanita egg shows the **outline of a cap, gills and stem** inside, with no jelly layer.
- [[s:common-puffball]] and young [[s:giant-puffball]]: solid, firm white flesh right through when cut, no jelly, no cord.
- Dog stinkhorn (*Mutinus caninus*): smaller and thinner, with an **orange** tip. Sand stinkhorn (*Phallus hadriani*): pinkish-violet egg, in sand dunes.

## Dangers
- Not known to be poisonous, but there is no reason to eat it, and the egg could be an Amanita egg. Never eat any wild fungus unless an expert has checked it in person.
- Its smell and flies are a good reason not to pitch a shelter beside one.
